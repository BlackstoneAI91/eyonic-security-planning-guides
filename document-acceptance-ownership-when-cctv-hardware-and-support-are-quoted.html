Document Acceptance Ownership When CCTV Hardware and Support Are Quoted Separately

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical guide for Sarawak premises managers on building a responsibility matrix for documentation, installer checks and handover when CCTV hardware and support arrive in separate quotes.

Start with a responsibility matrix, not a price comparison

When hardware and support are quoted separately, the first document to write is not a comparison table of totals. It is a responsibility matrix. The matrix answers one question for every item in the project: who owns this, and who signs it off? Write the rows before you read the quotes closely, so the quotes have to fit your structure rather than the other way around.

A workable matrix has four columns: item, owner during the project, owner after handover, and evidence of acceptance. Keep the items plain. Examples include the site survey record, the camera placement plan, the recording and NVR setup notes, the remote viewing arrangement, the user guide, the training session and the list of who to contact for support. None of these rows require you to decide in advance whether a quote includes installation or any warranty; they simply record who is expected to produce or receive each item.

For context, Eyonic Security Systems plans, supplies and installs monitoring systems with camera placement, recording and remote viewing for homes, offices, shops and industrial sites. That description tells you what the brand does; it does not tell you what any particular quote contains. The matrix is how you find out, item by item, without assuming.

A fictional example: a two-storey office in Sarawak receives one quote for cameras and recording equipment and a second quote for support visits. The premises manager lists eight rows, marks the hardware supplier against the equipment rows and the support provider against the guide and training rows, then leaves the acceptance column blank until each provider confirms in writing.

Separate the survey record from the installation promise

A CCTV site survey is a document, and documents have owners. Before any CCTV installation is scheduled, agree who receives the survey record, who may request changes to it and how a revision is dated. This matters most when the hardware quote and the support quote were prepared by different people who may not have visited the premises on the same day.

Build a short survey register with three fields: date of visit, person or role who attended, and the revision number of the record produced. Then add a fourth field for the person who accepted that revision. If a later quote refers to camera placement that differs from the accepted survey, the register shows you the gap immediately.

Keep the language neutral. You are not judging whether a survey was good; you are recording which version the project is working from. A fictional example: a shop receives a hardware quote that mentions four camera positions and a support quote that mentions five. The register shows the support provider visited a week later and produced revision two. The premises manager asks both providers to confirm in writing which revision they are pricing against before either is accepted.

This is also where you decide who holds the drawing. Name a role, not a person, so the record survives staff changes. The role that holds the current revision should also be the role that receives future updates.

Write down who receives the handover pack

The handover pack is the set of documents and briefings that lets your team operate the system after the project ends. When hardware and support are quoted separately, the pack can fall between the two providers unless you name a recipient in advance.

List the items you expect in the pack and mark, for each, whether it comes from the hardware side, the support side or both. Typical items include a plain-language user guide, a record of the recording and NVR setup as configured, a note on how remote viewing is arranged for authorised staff, and a named contact for ordinary questions. You do not need to specify settings or credentials in this list; you are recording what should exist and who hands it over.

Then name the receiving role. A fictional example: a warehouse manager in Sarawak assigns the handover pack to the facilities supervisor, with a copy to the office administrator. Both providers are told, in writing, that the pack is not accepted until the facilities supervisor confirms each listed item is present and readable.

Keep a simple acceptance line for each item: received, reviewed, questions raised, closed. This turns a vague promise of support into a record you can point to later without needing to remember who said what.

Decide who signs off installer checks

Installer checks are the point where the project moves from quoted to accepted. The matrix should say, before work starts, which checks the installer will evidence and which role on your side receives that evidence. Avoid writing the checks as technical procedures; write them as things you expect to be shown or explained.

A practical set of rows might include: the camera placement matches the accepted survey revision; the recording arrangement is explained to the receiving role; remote viewing is demonstrated to the people who will use it; and the user guide has been walked through with at least one member of your team. Each row gets a named receiving role and a date.

A fictional example: a clinic reception area is covered by a hardware quote and a separate support quote. The practice manager asks the support provider to walk the reception supervisor through the user guide and to confirm in writing that the walkthrough happened. The hardware provider confirms the placement matches revision one. Neither confirmation claims a warranty; both simply record that a check took place and who received it.

Where a check cannot be completed on the day, record it as open with an owner and a review date rather than leaving it blank.

Keep support questions and hardware questions in separate logs

When two providers are involved, a single shared inbox or chat thread quickly becomes unmanageable. Split your records into a hardware log and a support log, and give each a named owner on your side. The split is not about blame; it is about routing a question to the party who can answer it.

Each log needs four fields: date raised, plain description of the question, role who raised it, and role who follows up. Add a closing field for the answer received. Keep descriptions free of credentials, access codes and detailed layouts; a short neutral note is enough to route the question.

A fictional example: a retail premises manager notices that a staff member cannot find the user guide. That goes in the support log, addressed to the support provider. A separate question about whether a camera position matches the drawing goes in the hardware log. Because the two are separate, neither provider is asked to answer for the other, and the premises manager can see which questions are still open at the weekly review.

Review both logs on a fixed day. A short weekly review is an author heuristic, not a rule; choose a rhythm that fits your site and record it in the matrix.

Use a short acceptance worksheet before you close the project

Before you declare the project closed, run a one-page worksheet against the matrix. The worksheet is a checklist, and the scores below are examples of how you might weight items, not established research.

Row one: every matrix item has a named owner after handover. Row two: the current survey revision is recorded and matches what was installed. Row three: the handover pack items are marked received and reviewed. Row four: the installer checks are evidenced with a date and a receiving role. Row five: both logs have a named owner and a review day. Row six: the support contact for ordinary questions is written down and known to the receiving role.

A fictional example of scoring: give each row one point if it is complete, half a point if it is in progress with an owner, and zero if it is unassigned. A total below four is a signal to hold the closure meeting rather than a formal verdict. The point is to make gaps visible while the providers are still engaged.

If your project also touches smart lock door access, add a row for who holds the user guide and who reviews user roles, since smart locks work best when door condition, user roles and backup method are reviewed before installation.

Hand the matrix to the next premises manager

The final step is continuity. A responsibility matrix is only useful if the next person can read it without a briefing. Store it with the handover pack, date it, and add a short cover note explaining what each column means and which role currently holds each owner position.

Keep the cover note to one page. Name the roles, not the individuals, and note the review date for the logs. If a provider changes, the matrix shows which rows need to be reassigned and which evidence already exists, so the new provider is not asked to redo work that was already accepted.

A fictional example: a shop changes facilities supervisor six months after handover. The new supervisor opens the matrix, sees that the support log owner is the office administrator, sees the current survey revision and the date the handover pack was accepted, and knows which two questions are still open. No one has to reconstruct the project from memory.

For readers planning a CCTV installation in Sarawak, the matrix is the document that keeps hardware and support accountable to the same handover, whichever providers you choose.
