Access Control Card Issuance Procedure for a Small Business

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical card issuance procedure for Sarawak premises managers: request, approval, encoding, handover, tracking and revocation of access cards for employees and contractors, with a register template and a quarterly review step.

Why a written card procedure matters more than the reader itself

A card reader at the door is only as disciplined as the paperwork behind it. The reader checks a credential; it does not know whether the person holding it should still have it. In a small business, cards are often handed out informally — a new hire gets one on the first morning, a contractor borrows a spare, a resigned staff member keeps a card 'in case'. Each of those shortcuts is invisible until something goes wrong.

A written issuance procedure fixes that by making every card traceable to a named person, an approval, and a date. It also gives you something to show an auditor, a landlord, or an insurer if access is ever questioned. The procedure below is a template, not a regulation; adapt the approval names and retention periods to your own organisation.

Step 1: Define who may request, approve and issue

Before any card is printed, name three roles in writing. The requester is usually the supervisor or department head who needs the person on site. The approver is the person with budget and headcount authority — often the owner or manager in a small Sarawak business. The issuer is whoever holds the enrolment software or card stock, and should not be the same person as the approver.

Separating approver from issuer is the single most useful control in a small team. It means one person cannot both decide and execute access. Where the team is very small, record the conflict openly in the register rather than pretending it does not exist.

Decide also who may hold a card at all: permanent staff, probationers, long-term contractors, cleaners, and perhaps a landlord's representative. Each category should have its own default access zone and expiry rule. A contractor card that expires on the last day of the works is far easier to manage than one that quietly rolls on.

Step 2: The request and approval form

Keep the form to one page. It should capture: full name, staff or contractor reference, department or host, card type, access zones requested, start date, expiry date, and the approver's signature with date. Add a line for 'reason for access' so that a cleaner's card for the second floor is explained rather than assumed.

For contractors, add the company name, the works order or contract reference, and the name of the site supervisor who will escort them if escort rules apply. For employees, note whether the card is also used for attendance or payroll integration, because that changes who owns the data.

A useful discipline is to require the form before the card is encoded, not after. If the person starts on a Monday, the form should be approved by the previous Friday. Where that is impossible, issue a temporary card with a short expiry and convert it once the paperwork catches up.

File approved forms by card number, not by name. When a card is lost, you search by number; when a person leaves, you search by name. Keeping both indexes, or a spreadsheet with both columns, saves time later.

Step 3: Encoding the card and recording the credential

Encoding is where the physical card meets the software. Record the card number printed on the plastic, the internal credential number the reader sees, the access group assigned, and the date and time of encoding. If your system supports it, note the reader or door group so that a card issued for the front door is not silently valid at the store room.

Test every new card at the doors it is meant to open, and at one door it is not. A card that opens everything is a configuration error, not a convenience. Write the test result on the form.

For business doors, the choice between a standalone smart lock and a panel-based reader affects how cards are managed. That check belongs at the design stage, because a standalone lock may not support the card register you are building here.

Step 4: Handover to the cardholder

Hand the card over in person where possible, and ask the holder to sign for it. The signature confirms three things: they received the card, they understand it is personal and not transferable, and they know how to report a loss.

Give a short written note with the card. It should state the doors the card opens, the hours it works, the rule against lending it, the lost-card contact, and the expectation that the card is returned on the last day of employment or contract. Keep the note to half a page; a long policy will not be read.

For smart lock door access on a business door, the same logic applies to codes and mobile credentials as to cards: permissions and a backup entry method should be planned before the door goes live. Step 5: The card register and its review cycle

The register is the heart of the procedure. One row per card, with columns for card number, holder name, category, access group, issue date, expiry date, approver, return date, and status. Keep it in a place that survives staff turnover — a shared drive or a printed binder held by the manager, not a personal laptop.

Review the register on a fixed rhythm. A monthly check of cards with no expiry, and a quarterly reconciliation against the staff and contractor list, will catch most drift. Mark each review with a date and initials so the check itself is evidenced.

A worked example: a small office in Sarawak has twelve staff and two regular contractors. The register shows fourteen active cards. The quarterly review finds one card belonging to a staff member who left two months earlier and one contractor card that expired at the end of the previous works order. Both are disabled the same day, and the review sheet records the action. No drama, no investigation — just a routine catch.

Decide in advance what happens to a card that is not returned. The usual options are to disable it immediately and to record the loss; the register should show 'disabled' rather than 'returned' so the distinction is honest.

Step 6: Revocation, loss and contractor expiry

Revocation should be as fast as issuance. Write down who can disable a card, how, and within what time. For a lost card, the priority is to disable the credential, not to find the plastic. For a leaver, the priority is the same, plus recovery of the card if it is offered.

Contractor cards deserve their own rule. Set the expiry to the end of the works order, and require the site supervisor to confirm in writing when the works finish. If the works overrun, re-approve rather than extend silently.

Keep a short revocation log: date, card number, reason (resigned, lost, contract ended, disciplinary), who disabled it, and who confirmed. This log is the evidence that the procedure is alive. It also pairs naturally with the camera record, since a disputed entry can be checked against footage if the cameras cover that door.

A one-page worksheet you can copy

Use this as a starting structure, adapting names and retention to your organisation. It is an author's template, not a standard.

Section A — Roles: requester, approver, issuer, and the person who can disable a card in an emergency. Write names, not job titles alone.

Section B — Card categories: employee, probationer, contractor, cleaner, other. For each, note default access zones and maximum validity period.

Section C — Request form fields: name, reference, host, zones, start, expiry, reason, approver signature, date.

Section D — Encoding record: card number, credential number, access group, test result at permitted door, test result at a denied door, issuer initials.

Section E — Handover: holder signature, date, note issued (yes/no), lost-card contact given (yes/no).

Section F — Register columns: as listed in Step 5, plus a review date and reviewer initials column.

Section G — Revocation log: date, card number, reason, disabled by, confirmed by.

Score your draft out of seven, one point per section that exists and is filled in for at least one real card. A score below five is a signal to finish the paperwork before the next card is issued. Treat the score as a house heuristic, not a benchmark.

When the card system is part of a wider security project, the same discipline applies to the other systems: a site survey before quoting, camera placement agreed in writing, and a handover that names who holds each credential.
