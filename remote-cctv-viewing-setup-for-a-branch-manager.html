Remote CCTV Viewing Setup for a Branch Manager: Acceptance Checklist

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical checklist for Sarawak branch managers to confirm remote CCTV viewing works, record who holds credentials, and test access from outside the premises before accepting the system.

Why remote viewing needs its own acceptance step

A branch can have cameras recording perfectly and still fail the first time a manager tries to check the premises from home. Recording and remote viewing are two separate outcomes, and only one of them is usually obvious on the day of handover. The recorder may be running while the mobile app has never been opened on a phone that is not connected to the shop's own network.

That gap matters because remote viewing is often the reason a branch manager approves the project in the first place. If you only watch the installer demonstrate playback on the local monitor, you have confirmed recording, not remote access. Treat remote viewing as its own acceptance item with its own test, its own owner and its own written record.

This guide is written for a premises manager planning an ordinary commercial project, not a technical specialist. It assumes a supplier has proposed or installed a system and you need to confirm it works before you sign off. Eyonic CCTV, also known as Eyonic Security Systems, plans, supplies and installs monitoring systems with camera placement, recording and remote viewing for homes, offices, shops and industrial sites, so the checklist below is framed around what a manager should ask and record rather than how to configure anything. Configuration stays with the installer; your job is to verify the result and own the paperwork.

One framing note before you start: this is a management checklist, not a security audit. It will not tell you whether your camera coverage is adequate. It tells you whether the viewing side of the system is genuinely usable by the people who will rely on it.

Before the visit: decide who needs to see what

Most remote viewing problems start as an unclear request. If you ask for "remote access" without saying who needs it, from where, and for what purpose, the installer will make reasonable assumptions that may not match your operation.

Write a short list before the handover visit. For each person who needs viewing access, record their role, whether they need live view only or playback as well, and whether they need it on a phone, a tablet or a laptop. A branch manager checking a shutter at night has a different need from an area manager reviewing a week-old incident.

Keep the list small and deliberate. Every additional account is another credential to track, another person to remove when they leave, and another route into your footage. A useful discipline is to name one primary account holder per site and treat all other accounts as exceptions that need a reason written down.

Also decide, in advance, what you will not grant. Some managers choose to keep playback review with a named person rather than every viewer. Others restrict viewing to business hours. There is no single correct answer, but the decision should be made by you before the installer sets accounts up, not discovered afterwards.

Finally, note the physical context. A branch in Sarawak may have variable mobile coverage depending on location and building construction, so ask the installer to test on the network your staff actually use rather than only on the office Wi-Fi. This is a practical question about your site, not a claim about coverage generally.

The setup questions to ask during handover

Bring a short question sheet to the handover. The goal is to leave with a plain-language understanding of what was built, not a technical manual you will never open.

Ask where the recorder sits and who can physically reach it. Ask how many days of footage are retained under the current settings, and what happens to older footage. Ask whether the recording schedule runs continuously or on motion, and whether that choice was made deliberately for your site.

On the viewing side, ask which app or portal is used, whether it requires a separate subscription, and what happens if that service changes. Ask how a user account is created, changed and removed, and who at the supplier performs those changes. Ask what the recovery route is if the primary account holder is unavailable.

Ask what the system does when a camera goes offline, and how you would know. A camera that fails silently is worse than one that announces itself, because staff may assume they are being recorded when they are not.

If your project also includes smart lock door access, keep the two conversations separate. Smart locks work best when the door condition, user roles and backup method are reviewed before installation. That review belongs in its own handover, not folded into the camera app discussion.

The outside-the-premises test

This is the step most often skipped. The test must happen outside the building, on a device that is not connected to the shop's network, with the person who will actually use it holding the phone.

Run it in this order. First, the manager opens the app on their own phone using mobile data, with Wi-Fi switched off, and confirms live view for each camera in turn. Second, they open playback and find a clip from earlier the same day. Third, they check that the image is clear enough to answer a simple question, such as whether a shutter is down or a door is closed.

Then test the awkward cases deliberately. Ask the installer to simulate a camera going offline, if that can be done safely without disturbing the live system, and confirm what the app shows. Log out and log back in to confirm the credentials work as issued. If a second authorised person is present, have them repeat the live view test on their own device.

Record the result in writing: date, time, device, network used, cameras tested, and who witnessed it. A short table is enough. If any camera fails, note it as an open item rather than accepting a verbal assurance that it will settle down.

If the project included a full CCTV installation, the same visit is a sensible moment to confirm that camera placement matches what was agreed and that the views you were promised are the views you can actually see remotely.

Credentials, ownership and the leavers problem

Remote viewing lives or dies on account hygiene. Decide now who owns the primary account, and make sure that ownership sits with the branch or the company rather than with an individual's personal email or phone number. Staff move; the branch remains.

Build a simple register. One row per user, with columns for name, role, date granted, what they can see, and date removed. Keep it somewhere the branch can find it without asking one person. Review it at the same time you review other access lists, such as door codes or alarm keyholders.

Agree a removal routine before you need it. When someone leaves or changes role, who tells the supplier, and how quickly? A named deputy for that task prevents the common situation where access lingers because the only person who knew how to request removal has also left.

Ask the supplier to confirm, in writing, how account changes are authorised, so that a request cannot be actioned on an informal message from an unknown number. This is ordinary procurement discipline, not a comment on any particular provider.

Finally, store the handover documents together: the account register, the outside-premises test record, the retention setting, and the supplier's support contact route. A single folder that a new manager can inherit is worth more than a perfect setup that only one person understands.

A one-page acceptance worksheet

Use this as a template. It is an author's example, not a certified form, and you should adapt the columns to your site.

Section A, scope. List each camera by a plain name, such as front entrance, counter, rear door. For each, note whether remote live view is required and whether remote playback is required.

Section B, accounts. List each authorised user, their role, the date granted, and the review date. Note the primary account holder and the named deputy.

Section C, outside test. For each camera, record pass or fail, the device used, the network used, and the time. Add a line for playback and a line for the offline-camera behaviour.

Section D, settings. Record the retention period, the recording mode, and where the recorder is located.

Section E, open items. List anything that failed or was deferred, with an owner and a target date. Do not close the acceptance until every open item has either been resolved or formally accepted by you with a reason.

Section F, sign-off. Name, role, date, and the supplier representative present. Keep it short enough that it actually gets completed.

A reasonable working target, offered as a heuristic rather than a standard, is to complete Sections A to D during the handover visit and to close Section E within a fortnight. Adjust to your own project size.

Where this fits in the wider project

Remote viewing is one acceptance item among several, and it is worth keeping it in proportion. A branch manager signing off a security project is usually also confirming camera placement, recording behaviour, and often a burglar alarm system or access control system in the same building. Each deserves its own short test and its own record.

If you are still at the planning stage rather than handover, the more useful conversation is about the site survey and camera placement, because remote viewing can only show what the cameras were positioned to capture. A supplier that plans, supplies and installs the whole monitoring system, including NVR setup and remote viewing, can usually walk you through both stages in one sequence, but you should still test them as separate outcomes.

Keep the checklist with your project file and revisit it when staff change, when the app updates, or when the branch layout changes. A setup that passed last year is not automatically still passing today, and the cheapest way to find out is a five-minute test from the car park rather than a failed review during an incident.
