Alarm False Activation Review Worksheet: Logging Each Trigger Before You Complain

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical log-and-review worksheet for Sarawak premises managers who want to record each alarm activation by zone, time and follow-up action so patterns surface before a nuisance-alarm complaint is raised.

Why an activation log beats a memory of nuisance alarms

A burglar alarm system that triggers at an inconvenient hour is easy to remember and hard to describe. By the time a complaint is raised, the details that would explain the trigger have usually faded: which zone lit up, what time it happened, whether the door was opened just before, whether the same sensor had already fired twice that week. A written log turns a vague irritation into a set of observations you can act on.

The worksheet in this guide is deliberately plain. It records four things for every activation: the zone, the time, what was happening at the premises, and the follow-up action taken. Nothing in it requires special equipment. A shared spreadsheet or a printed sheet kept at the panel is enough.

One framing note before you start. This is an operational record for ordinary premises management, not a fault-finding procedure for electrical work or a substitute for a qualified technician. If an activation suggests a hardware problem, the log is what you hand to the installer so the visit starts from evidence rather than a description.

The worksheet columns and what belongs in each

Set up the log with these columns. Keep the order fixed so entries stay comparable across months.

Date and day of week. The day matters more than people expect. A trigger that only appears on Sundays tells a different story from one that appears on weekday mornings.

Time armed and time of activation. Two timestamps, not one. The gap between them is often the useful number.

Zone number and zone name. Use the same zone names that appear at the panel and on the handover note. If the panel says Zone 3 and your log says back corridor, the two records cannot be reconciled later.

State at the time. Armed, disarmed, part-armed, or entry delay running. An activation during entry delay is a different event from one at 2 a.m. with the premises empty.

What was happening. A short factual note: staff arrived, shutter was raised, cleaner entered, heavy rain, no one on site. Write what you observed, not what you assume caused it.

Follow-up action. Who was told, what was checked, whether the zone was bypassed, whether a technician was called. Include the date the action closed.

Reviewed by. A name and a date, so the log has an owner rather than drifting between staff.

A worked fictional example: a small office in Kuching logs an activation on Zone 2 at 07:42 on a Tuesday, armed at 19:10 the previous evening, state armed, note reads staff member opened the rear door before disarming, follow-up is a reminder to the opening staff and a note to review the entry delay setting with the installer. That single line is more useful than a paragraph written from memory a fortnight later.

Separating the four kinds of activation before judging any of them

Not every activation is the same kind of event, and lumping them together is what makes review conversations unproductive. Sort each logged entry into one of four buckets.

Genuine intrusion or attempted entry. Rare, serious, and should be handled through your normal incident process rather than through this worksheet alone.

User-related. Someone armed late, disarmed incorrectly, opened a door during entry delay, or used a code that was not theirs. These entries usually cluster around shift changes and opening times.

Environmental or physical. A door left ajar, a loose contact, movement from an unexpected source, weather. Record what you saw; leave the diagnosis to the technician.

Unknown. The log has no explanation. Unknown entries are not failures of the worksheet; they are the reason to keep it. Three unknowns in the same zone within a month is a pattern worth escalating.

This sorting step is the difference between a complaint and a brief. A brief says: Zone 4 produced four unknown activations in three weeks, all between 01:00 and 03:00, no staff on site, technician visit requested. That is a request an installer can plan around.

A monthly review meeting that takes twenty minutes

Once a month, sit down with the log and answer five questions in order. Treat the timings below as an author heuristic for keeping the meeting short, not as a researched standard.

First, count activations by zone. Any zone with more than a handful of entries in a month deserves a closer look. Second, count by bucket. If user-related entries dominate, the fix is usually a conversation and a refresher on arming and disarming, not a hardware visit. Third, look at the time-of-day spread. A cluster in one narrow window is a clue; a scatter across the week is a different clue. Fourth, check whether follow-up actions from last month actually closed. An open action carried forward twice is a decision waiting to be made. Fifth, decide the single next step: no action, staff reminder, settings review with the installer, or a technician visit.

Write the decision into the log. A review that ends without a recorded decision tends to be repeated next month with the same entries.

When the log points to a settings or hardware conversation

Some patterns belong in a conversation with the installer rather than in a staff memo. Bring the log, not a summary of it. The entries carry the timestamps and zone names that make the conversation concrete.

Useful things to ask, phrased as questions rather than instructions: which zones are configured with entry delay and how long it runs; whether any zone is set to a sensitivity that suits the room it covers; whether the panel records an event history that can be compared against your log; and whether the handover note lists the zone names you are using.

Keep the scope of that conversation to the alarm. If the same review keeps raising questions about who was at a door and when, that is a separate matter and belongs with your access records, not with the alarm log. Mixing the two makes both harder to resolve.

Keeping the log useful across staff changes

A log that only one person understands stops being maintained the month that person leaves. Three habits keep it alive.

Keep the zone names identical to the panel and the handover note. When a new manager takes over, the first task is to walk the premises with the zone list and confirm each name still matches a physical location. A zone called store room that now covers a relocated partition is a source of confusion, not evidence.

Record the reviewer's name every month, even when the answer is no action. An empty month is still a data point.

Archive completed months rather than deleting them. A pattern that repeats every quarter is invisible in a rolling single-month view.

Finally, decide in advance what would make you escalate. A simple example rule: three unknown activations in the same zone within one month, or any activation during a period when the premises were confirmed empty and secured. Write the rule at the top of the sheet so the decision is not made fresh each time under pressure.

None of this replaces the installer's own testing or your acceptance process. It sits alongside them, turning everyday activations into a record you can read.
