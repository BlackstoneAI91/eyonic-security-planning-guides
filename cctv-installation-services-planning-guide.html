Planning CCTV coverage for a small shop with two entrances

Prepared for Eyonic Security System (Eyonic CCTV)

A practical planning guide for small shop owners in Sarawak covering camera placement, lighting, field of view, acceptance footage and recording access when the premises has two entrances.

Start with a simple plan of both entrances

Before choosing cameras, draw the shop as a rectangle on paper and mark both entrances, the counter, the stockroom door and any rear service lane. Add arrows for how customers walk in, where they queue, and where staff move between the front and back. This rough plan is the single most useful document in the whole project because every later decision, from lens choice to recorder capacity, refers back to it. A shop with two entrances is not simply a one-entrance shop with an extra camera; the two doors create two separate flows that may need different treatment.

Label each entrance with what you actually need to see. At a main customer entrance you may want a clear view of faces as people step in, plus enough of the doorway to show whether the door was open or closed. At a secondary or rear entrance you may care more about deliveries, staff coming and going, or after-hours activity. Write these needs in plain words next to each door. When a supplier visits for a CCTV site survey, that annotated plan lets the conversation start from your requirements rather than from a generic package. It also helps you notice gaps early, such as a blind corner between the counter and the rear door that no single camera can cover.

Keep the plan realistic about the building. Note ceiling height, whether walls are solid or partitioned, where power points already exist, and whether cabling can run above a false ceiling. These details affect where cameras can physically go and how much work installation involves. If you rent the unit, check what alterations are permitted before drilling. A plan that ignores these constraints tends to produce awkward compromises later, such as a camera mounted lower than ideal because the intended ceiling point was not accessible.

Match each camera to a job, not to a count

A common mistake is to decide on a number of cameras first and then look for places to put them. A better approach is to list the jobs you want footage to answer. For a two-entrance shop, typical jobs include seeing who enters the main door, seeing the counter transaction area, covering the secondary door, and watching the stockroom threshold. Each job implies a direction of view and a distance to the subject, and those two facts drive the choice of camera and lens far more than a headline megapixel figure.

Think in terms of identification, recognition and observation. Identification means you could reasonably tell one person from another; recognition means you can tell what kind of event happened; observation means you can see general movement. A camera aimed at a face-height point near the main entrance can support identification if the distance is short and the view is not too wide. A camera covering a long shop floor may only support observation, and that is fine if you accept it. Being honest about which job each camera serves prevents disappointment when you review footage later.

Camera placement should also respect the geometry of the room. Avoid aiming a camera straight at a bright doorway, because the contrast between the bright opening and the darker interior can leave faces in shadow. Angling the camera slightly across the doorway rather than directly through it often gives a more usable image. Keep cameras out of easy reach where practical, and avoid positions where a display stand, signage or a hanging product will block the view as the shop changes through the year. If you plan to rearrange shelving seasonally, note which camera views depend on the current layout.

Lighting, glass and the two-entrance problem

Lighting is where many small shop plans quietly fail. A camera that looks fine at midday may produce a weak image at closing time when only a few interior lights are on, and a doorway that faces the late afternoon sun can flare badly. Walk the shop at the times of day that matter, including opening and closing, and look at each proposed camera position from the camera's point of view rather than from yours. Note where interior lights reflect off glass, where a bright window sits behind the subject, and whether the entrance area is lit at night.

Glass doors deserve special attention. A camera aimed at a glass entrance from inside may record reflections of the shop interior instead of the street, and a camera aimed from outside may record reflections of the sky. Tilting the camera, moving it closer to the glass, or choosing a position that looks across the doorway at an angle can reduce the problem. If the shop has a covered walkway or awning, check whether it casts a hard shadow line across the entrance, because a subject stepping from bright shade into deep shadow can be hard to see in a single frame.

For a two-entrance shop, the lighting at the two doors is often completely different. The main frontage may be bright and busy while the rear door is a dim service corridor. Treat them as separate lighting problems and describe each in your plan. Where lighting is genuinely poor, the honest options are to improve the lighting, accept a grainy night image, or choose a camera suited to low light. A supplier reviewing the site can advise which of these fits your budget, but the decision should be recorded so nobody expects more from the footage than the conditions allow.

Recording, retention and remote viewing

Decide early how long you want to keep footage and who needs to look at it. Retention is a storage question: more cameras, higher resolution and longer retention all consume recorder capacity, so the recorder and its hard drive should be sized against your plan rather than chosen last. A practical approach is to write down the retention you want for each camera, then let the installer translate that into storage. If you are unsure, ask for the trade-offs in plain terms rather than accepting a single figure without explanation.

Remote CCTV viewing is useful for a shop owner who is not always on site, but it should be set up with clear rules. Decide which staff or family members get access, what they are allowed to view, and how access is removed when someone leaves. Keep the recorder itself in a locked or supervised space, and agree who holds the administrator password. These are management decisions as much as technical ones, and writing them down now avoids awkward conversations later. If the shop also uses smart lock door access on a staff or stockroom door, think about how door events and camera footage relate, so that a door opening at an odd hour can be checked against the relevant camera view.

A camera placement worksheet you can fill in

Use a simple table to turn the plan into decisions. For each camera, record a location name, the job it serves, the direction it faces, the distance to the main subject, the lighting at the busiest and quietest times, and any obstruction risk. Add a column for the acceptance shot you will check at handover. This worksheet is an author heuristic, not a standard, but it keeps the discussion concrete and gives the installer something specific to respond to.

As an example, a fictional shop might list four rows. Camera one sits above the main entrance, angled across the doorway, job is identification of people entering, acceptance shot is a person walking in at opening time. Camera two covers the counter from the ceiling corner, job is recognition of transactions, acceptance shot is a staff member handing over an item. Camera three covers the secondary door from inside, job is recognition of who uses the door, acceptance shot is a delivery being passed through. Camera four watches the stockroom threshold, job is observation of movement, acceptance shot is a person crossing the threshold. Your own rows will differ, but the structure holds.

Keep the worksheet with the floor plan and update it when the shop layout changes. If a new display unit blocks camera two, the worksheet tells you which job is now compromised. Reviewing it once or twice a year is a small habit that keeps the system aligned with how the shop actually operates.

Acceptance footage and the handover check

Acceptance is the moment you confirm the system does what the plan asked. Do it at realistic times, ideally including one session in daylight and one after closing when the shop lights are as they will be overnight. Walk to each camera's subject position and check the recorded image against the acceptance shot in your worksheet. Look for faces that are too small, doorways that are washed out, and views blocked by signage. If something does not meet the job you defined, raise it during handover rather than months later.

Check the practical details too. Confirm that the time and date on the recorder are correct, that footage can be found by searching a known time, and that remote viewing works on the device you will actually use. Ask how the system behaves after a power cut and what the recovery steps are. Confirm who to contact for support and what information to have ready. These checks are not about testing the installer's competence for its own sake; they are about making sure you can use the system confidently on an ordinary trading day.

Ask the installer to list camera supply, recording configuration and mobile viewing handover separately in the quote. Use the worksheet to see whether the proposed coverage and the handover match what you will test at both entrances.

Who handles access to recordings

Footage from a shop entrance can include customers, staff and passers-by, so access should be deliberate. Name the people who can view recordings, name the person who approves exports, and decide how requests from staff or others are handled. Keep a simple log of who accessed footage and why. This is good practice for any small business and helps if a question arises later about how a clip was used. Avoid sharing clips casually on messaging apps without a clear reason.

Review access when roles change. Revoke departing staff members’ access promptly. Give authorised administrators individual accounts and document the recovery process so the shop can regain access if the usual administrator is unavailable.

Finally, write a one-page summary for the shop: the plan, the worksheet, the retention decision, the access list and the support contact. Keep it where the person opening and closing the shop can find it. A modest, well-documented two-entrance setup is easier to trust than an elaborate one nobody understands.
