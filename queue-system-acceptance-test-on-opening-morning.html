Queue System Acceptance Test on Opening Morning: A One-Morning Script for Sarawak Premises

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical one-morning acceptance test script for a queue management system, covering ticket issuance, counter calls, display updates, and a fallback plan when the system is unavailable.

Why the First Busy Morning Is the Right Test

A queue management system can look finished during a quiet handover. The dispenser prints, the counter buttons light up, and the display shows a number. None of that proves the system works when twenty people arrive at once and three counters are calling tickets at the same time.

The first genuinely busy morning is the most honest test you will get, because it exercises the parts that only interact under load: ticket issuance speed, the order in which calls are announced, whether the display keeps up, and whether staff can recover when something stalls. Treat that morning as a scheduled acceptance test rather than an ordinary trading day. Tell the staff it is a test, keep a clipboard at the counter, and give yourself permission to fall back to manual queuing if the system misbehaves.

This guide is written for premises managers in Sarawak planning ordinary service-hall projects, such as a payment counter, a service desk or a front office. It is a test script, not a substitute for the installer's own commissioning procedure. Where the supplier has documented expected behaviour, that document wins.

One framing note: the same discipline you would apply to a CCTV installation handover applies here. Before the Doors Open: A Fifteen-Minute Pre-Flight

Arrive early and run a short pre-flight before the first customer walks in. This is not a full commissioning check; it is a confirmation that nothing changed overnight.

Work through these items in order and tick each one:

1. Power and network. Confirm the dispenser, counter terminals and display are powered and connected. If any part relies on the office network, confirm it is reachable. 2. Ticket stock and paper. Check the dispenser has enough stock for the expected morning and that the last printed ticket is legible. 3. Counter sign-in. Have each counter operator sign in with their own account, not a shared one, so the test can tell you who called which ticket. 4. Display visibility. Stand where a customer would stand and read the display. If you cannot read it from the back of the waiting area, note it now rather than at peak time. 5. Fallback kit. Put the manual fallback in place before you need it: a numbered pad or pre-printed slips, a written calling order, and a named person who decides when to switch.

Write the start time on your test sheet. Everything that follows is timed against it.

A useful habit borrowed from security-system handovers: record the state of the system at the start, not just the faults. If you later need to explain what changed, a clean baseline is worth more than a memory.

Test 1: Ticket Issuance Under Real Arrival Pressure

The first thing to test is the dispenser, because every later step depends on a ticket existing.

Run this sequence in the first twenty minutes:

- Issue a single ticket yourself and note the time from button press to printed slip. - Watch the first ten real customers issue tickets. Note any who press twice, hesitate, or ask for help. - Note whether the ticket number on the slip matches the number shown on the display. - Check whether the ticket shows the service category clearly enough that a customer knows where to wait.

Record three things for each observation: the time, what happened, and whether it is a pass, a fail, or a note for later. Do not try to fix anything mid-flow. The point of the morning is to collect evidence, not to troubleshoot in front of customers.

If the dispenser jams or runs out, that is a fail for the morning and a trigger for the fallback. Note the time it happened and how long recovery took, because recovery time is the number that matters to the people waiting.

One practical caution: a dispenser placed where customers queue for something else will collect accidental tickets. If you see tickets issued that nobody claims, note the position rather than the software. Placement problems are usually solved by moving the unit, not by changing settings.

Test 2: Counter Calls and Display Updates

This is the core of the morning. With real customers at the counter, watch how calls behave when more than one operator is serving.

Observe and record:

- Call order. When two counters call at nearly the same moment, does the display show both, or does one overwrite the other? Note which behaviour you actually want for your hall. - Announcement clarity. Can a customer in the waiting area hear or see which counter to go to? Ask one customer, politely, whether they could tell. - Recall and no-show. Watch what happens when a called ticket does not appear. Note how the operator recalls or skips, and whether the display reflects it. - Transfer between counters. If your service model allows a customer to be passed to a second counter, test it once with a willing customer and note the result. - Display lag. Compare the counter screen with the hall display. A short delay may be acceptable; a delay long enough that customers stand up for the wrong counter is not.

Keep a simple tally: tickets called, tickets served, tickets recalled, tickets marked no-show. At the end of the morning those four numbers tell you more about the system than any single observation.

If your service hall also runs a keyphone system at the same desk, note whether staff can hear the queue announcement while on a call. That is a layout and acoustics question as much as a system question, and it is worth writing down while you are standing there.

Test 3: The Fallback When the System Is Unavailable

A queue system that fails without a plan turns a busy morning into an unmanaged one. Test the fallback deliberately, at a quiet moment, rather than discovering it during a fault.

Agree in advance on four things:

1. The trigger. Who decides the system is unavailable, and what do they say to staff? Write the exact sentence down so it is not improvised. 2. The manual order. How will customers be called without the display? A numbered pad, a staff member at the head of the line, or a simple first-come order all work, but only one should be in force at a time. 3. The announcement. How will waiting customers be told what is happening? A short, calm sentence at the counter is usually enough. 4. The record. Who writes down the start and end time of the fallback, and how many customers were served during it?

Run a short drill: switch to manual for ten minutes, serve a handful of customers, then switch back. Note how long the switch took in each direction and whether any customer was missed in the handover.

This is the same principle that applies to a burglar alarm system handover: the value of the test is in the written record of what happened, not in the fact that the equipment powered on. A fallback that has never been rehearsed is a plan on paper only.

The Morning Test Sheet: What to Capture

Keep the record on one page. A sheet that is too detailed will not be filled in while customers are waiting.

Suggested columns, as an example format rather than a standard:

- Time - Event (ticket issued, counter called, display updated, recall, fallback started, fallback ended) - Counter or location - What was expected - What happened - Pass, fail, or note - Initials

At the end of the morning, add a short summary block: total tickets issued, total served, total recalled, total no-shows, longest observed wait, and whether the fallback was used. Sign and date it.

Two habits make the sheet more useful. First, write times as they happen, not from memory afterwards. Second, separate observations from opinions. "Display showed counter 3 at 10:42" is an observation; "display felt slow" is an opinion, and it belongs in a comments line, not in the pass/fail column.

If you are running this test alongside other acceptance work on the same premises, keep the queue sheet separate from your CCTV or access-control records. Mixed sheets get lost, and a queue test that cannot be produced later is hard to defend at sign-off.

Turning the Morning Into a Sign-Off Decision

The morning produces evidence. The decision comes after, with the supplier present.

Sort your sheet into three piles:

- Passes. Behaviour that matched what was agreed. These need no discussion. - Defects. Behaviour that did not match, with a time and a description. Ask the supplier to explain the cause and propose a fix. - Changes of mind. Things that work as designed but do not suit your hall, such as call order or display wording. These are configuration requests, not faults, and should be priced or scheduled separately.

Then agree a retest. A sensible pattern, offered as an author heuristic rather than a rule, is to retest the specific failed items on the next comparable busy morning rather than rerunning the whole script. Record the retest result on the same sheet so the history stays in one place.

Finally, write a short handover note for the staff who will run the counter daily: how to call, how to recall, how to switch to fallback, and who to call when something breaks. Keep it at the counter, not in an office drawer.

If your premises also runs CCTV security systems, resist the temptation to fold the queue test into the camera handover. They answer different questions, and combining them usually means neither gets a proper look.

Where This Fits in a Wider Premises Plan

A queue system rarely stands alone. In a service hall it sits beside access control at the staff door, a keyphone system at the desk, and cameras covering the counter and the waiting area. Each has its own acceptance test, and each test is stronger when it is run on a normal working day rather than an empty building.

The discipline is the same across all of them: define what good behaviour looks like, watch it under realistic conditions, write down what happened, and agree what counts as done. A queue system acceptance test on opening morning is simply that discipline applied to the busiest ten metres of your premises.

For premises managers planning a broader project, the practical advice is to sequence the tests rather than stack them. Test the queue on one morning, the cameras on another, and the door access on a third. You will get cleaner evidence and fewer arguments at sign-off.
