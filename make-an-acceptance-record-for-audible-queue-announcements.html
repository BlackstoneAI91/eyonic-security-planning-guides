Make an Acceptance Record for Audible Queue Announcements

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical guide for Sarawak premises managers on recording staff hearing checks, repeat conventions and adjustment notes when accepting audible queue announcements, with a fictional worked example and a sign-off worksheet.

Why an acceptance record matters for audible announcements

An audible announcement is one of the few parts of a queue management system that cannot be checked by looking at a screen. A ticket can be printed and inspected; a counter display can be photographed. A spoken announcement only exists in the moment it plays, and it is heard differently by the person at the front of the line, the person near the door and the staff member behind the counter. That is why a written acceptance record is worth preparing before the handover meeting rather than after it.

The record does two jobs. First, it gives the project team a shared description of what was heard, where, and by whom, so that a later disagreement is about a documented observation rather than a memory. Second, it separates two kinds of note: what staff observed with their own ears, and what needs a qualified person to adjust. Keeping those apart protects everyone. A receptionist can honestly say the announcement was unclear from the waiting chairs; only the provider should decide what to change.

This guide is written for premises managers in Sarawak planning ordinary security and service projects. It does not describe equipment settings or wiring. It focuses on the paperwork and the conversation around it. Where a brand is mentioned, it is as identity context for the project, not as a claim about how any particular announcement will perform.

Set the listening positions before anyone plays a test announcement

The most common weakness in an acceptance record is that it lists a result without saying where the listener stood. Fix that first. Walk the public area with the premises manager and mark a small number of ordinary visitor positions on a simple floor sketch. Keep the list short and repeatable: the front of the queue, the middle of the waiting area, the seats nearest the entrance, and the counter where staff call the next person. Add any position that matters for your own layout, such as a side corridor or a waiting bench near a door.

For each position, write a one-line description a stranger could follow, for example "third chair from the entrance, facing the counter". Avoid describing anything that would help someone tamper with or bypass equipment; a plain location note is enough. Decide in advance who will stand at each position during the check, and record their role rather than personal details. A rota of two or three staff is usually enough for an ordinary premises.

If your project also covers CCTV security systems, resist the temptation to merge the two acceptance records. Camera placement and announcement audibility are different questions with different owners, and combining them makes both harder to sign off. Keep this record focused on what people hear.

Write the repeat convention in plain words

An announcement that plays once is easy to miss; one that repeats too often becomes background noise. Before the check, agree a written repeat convention and record it in the acceptance document. The convention should answer three plain questions: how many times a call is repeated, how long the gap is between repeats, and what happens if nobody attends. Write the answers as sentences, not codes, so that a new supervisor can read them later without training.

A fictional example makes this concrete. A service centre agrees that each call is announced twice, with a short pause between, and that an unattended call is announced again after a stated interval. The record notes the agreed wording, the number of repeats and the interval, and adds a line saying who may change the convention. That last line matters: without it, a well-meaning staff member may alter the pattern during a busy morning and the next acceptance check will not match the document.

Keep the convention separate from any technical description of how it is produced. The record should describe the experience the visitor and staff expect, not the mechanism behind it. If the provider proposes a different pattern, note the proposal and the date it was discussed rather than editing the agreed text silently.

Run the staff hearing check and record what was actually heard

The hearing check is a structured listening exercise, not a pass or fail verdict. At each marked position, the listener notes three things: whether the announcement was audible, whether the words were clear enough to act on, and whether the listener could tell which counter was being called. Use a simple three-column table with a short comment box. Record the time of the check and the general conditions, such as whether the area was busy or quiet, because an announcement heard easily at a quiet moment may be harder to follow during a rush.

Be honest about variation. Two staff members at the same position may disagree, and that disagreement is useful evidence rather than a problem. Note it. If a listener reports that the announcement was audible but the counter name was unclear, write exactly that. Avoid converting observations into technical diagnoses; "unclear at the far seats" is a good note, while a guess about why belongs to the provider.

This is also the point to record any adjustment request in neutral language. A line such as "staff request a review of clarity at the entrance seats; to be assessed by the provider" keeps the record accurate without implying that anyone on site has changed a setting.

Separate observations from adjustments that need qualified staff

A good acceptance record has two clearly labelled parts. The first is the observation log: what staff heard, where, and when. The second is the adjustment list: items that need review by the provider or another qualified person. Keeping them apart stops the document from drifting into instructions that untrained staff should not follow.

On the adjustment list, write the request, the position it relates to, the date it was raised and the name of the role that will follow up. Do not write how the change should be made. If the provider later confirms a change, add a dated note saying what was confirmed and when the next listening check will happen. That gives you a clean audit trail without turning the record into a technical manual.

For projects that also include a burglar alarm system or access control system, apply the same discipline: observations on one side, qualified adjustments on the other. A single house style across your acceptance documents makes them easier to review together and easier to hand to a successor. The aim is a record that a new manager can read in five minutes and understand what was agreed and what remains open.

A short sign-off worksheet you can adapt

The worksheet below is an author heuristic, not a standard or a certification. Adapt the fields to your own premises and keep it to one or two pages.

Header: premises name, date, project reference, and the roles present at the check. Use roles rather than personal contact details.

Positions table: position description, listener role, audible yes or no, words clear yes or no, counter identifiable yes or no, comment.

Repeat convention: number of repeats, interval between repeats, unattended behaviour, who may change the convention.

Adjustment list: request, position, date raised, follow-up role, status.

Sign-off: one line for the premises manager, one for the provider representative, and a date for the next review. Leave space for a short note on anything deferred.

When the project also involves a site survey, ask whether the survey notes already describe the public area layout; if so, reference them rather than redrawing the sketch. A site survey record and an acceptance record serve different purposes, but they should not contradict each other.

Handover, storage and the next review

Decide where the completed record lives before the handover meeting, not after. A shared project folder with a clear name and a single owner is usually enough. Note who may edit it and who may only read it. If the premises has a document register, add the record to it with a review date, so that a change of layout or a new counter arrangement triggers a fresh listening check rather than leaving an old document in place.

At handover, walk the new owner through the record in the same order as the check: positions, repeat convention, observations, adjustments, sign-off. Ask them to confirm they can find the document unaided. That small test is more useful than a long briefing.

Finally, keep the record honest about its limits. It describes what a small group of staff heard on a particular day in ordinary visitor positions. It is not a guarantee of future performance and it is not a substitute for review by qualified staff when something changes. Written that way, it remains a useful operational document rather than an overclaim.
