CCTV Site Survey Questions for a Premises With Shared Walls

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical question set for Sarawak premises managers whose building shares walls with neighbours, covering boundary lines, mounting rights, cable routes and the decisions a CCTV site survey should produce before quoting.

Why a shared wall changes the survey

A premises with a shared wall is not just a standalone building with a camera problem. The wall itself is a boundary, and everything attached to it or run through it touches a neighbour's interests. That makes the CCTV site survey less about counting cameras and more about agreeing what may be fixed where, who owns the surface, and what happens when a cable route or a lens crosses into someone else's space.

The practical risk is not dramatic. It is ordinary: a camera aimed slightly too far over a fence, a bracket drilled into a party wall without a note in the file, a cable clipped along a shared corridor, or a view that captures a neighbour's window. Each of these can turn a straightforward quote into a negotiation after the invoice is signed.

So treat the survey as a decision meeting, not a wish list. The questions below are grouped so that each one ends in a recorded answer: yes, no, needs landlord approval, or needs a written agreement. If a question cannot be answered on the day, write down who will answer it and by when. A survey that leaves with three open questions and named owners is more useful than one that leaves with twenty camera positions and no boundary agreement.

Boundary and ownership questions to settle first

Before anyone discusses lens angles, settle the wall. These questions are best asked with the tenancy agreement, sale document or building plan on the table, because the answer usually lives in paperwork rather than in opinion.

Who owns the shared wall, and is it a party wall, a dividing wall inside one building, or an external wall that happens to sit on the boundary? Is the wall load-bearing or a partition, and does the landlord or management corporation have rules about drilling into it? If the premises is leased, does the tenancy permit fixtures on external surfaces, and does it require reinstatement when the lease ends? If the building is stratified, does the management corporation require a formal application before any bracket, conduit or camera is mounted on common property?

Then move to the neighbour. Is there a written understanding about attachments to the shared wall, and does it cover maintenance access? If a camera on your side of the wall looks toward a shared driveway, alley or corridor, has the neighbour been told what the lens will see? Who is the named contact if a camera needs adjusting because it has drifted?

Record each answer with a date and the document it came from. A survey note that says "party wall, landlord approval required, application to be filed by premises manager" is a decision. A note that says "check wall" is not.

Mounting rights and physical constraints

Once ownership is clear, the survey turns to what the wall can physically take. Shared walls are often older, sometimes rendered, sometimes hollow, and frequently already carrying conduits, downpipes or air-conditioning brackets from two households.

Ask where the camera bracket will be fixed and what is behind the fixing point. Is the surface solid masonry, hollow block, timber stud or metal cladding? Is there a cavity that a fixing might not grip? What is already mounted on that stretch of wall, and will a new bracket obstruct a drain, a vent or a neighbour's window opening? How high will the camera sit, and who will provide safe access for installation and later cleaning or adjustment?

Height matters for a second reason. A camera mounted high on a shared wall may look over a fence or into an adjacent upper floor. Ask the installer to describe, in plain words, what the lens will see at its proposed angle, and whether that view includes any part of a neighbouring property. If it does, decide now whether to adjust the angle, add a privacy mask, or seek agreement.

This is where camera placement and mounting rights meet. A position that is technically excellent but legally awkward is not a good position. Ask for the proposed camera positions to be marked on a simple floor or elevation sketch, with the fixing surface named for each one.

Cable routes across and through shared structure

Cable is where shared-wall projects quietly become complicated. A camera position agreed on the wall still needs power and a signal path back to the recorder, and that path may cross a boundary, a common corridor or a neighbour's ceiling void.

Ask which route the cable will take and which surfaces it will cross. Will it run on your side of the wall only, or does it need to pass through the shared structure? If it passes through, is there an existing penetration that can be reused, and who is responsible for sealing it afterwards? Will conduit be surface-mounted, and if so, is that acceptable to the landlord or management corporation? Is there a route through the ceiling that avoids a neighbour's space entirely?

Then ask about the recorder end. Where will the recorder sit, and is that location secure, ventilated and within reach for routine checks? Does the cable run stay within the length the installer is comfortable with, or will it need a network switch or a repeater? These are ordinary questions, but they are much cheaper to answer during a survey than after the walls are closed up.

If the answer to any route question is "we will decide on the day", treat that as an open item. A route decided on the day is a route decided by whoever is holding the drill.

Coverage, recording and viewing questions

With the wall and the route settled, the survey can turn to what the system is for. Keep this grounded in named gaps rather than a general wish for coverage.

Which entrances, counters, storage areas and vehicle approaches need to be seen, and what does a useful image look like at each one? Is the purpose deterrence, after-the-fact review, or live monitoring during trading hours? Will the system need to record continuously or on motion, and for how long should footage be kept before it is overwritten? Who will review footage, and on what device?

Remote viewing deserves its own question because it changes the network design. Who needs to view the cameras away from the premises, on what kind of device, and how will that access be withdrawn when a staff member leaves or changes role? Ask the installer to explain how accounts are created and removed, and who holds the administrator account. A viewing arrangement that nobody can revoke is a liability, not a convenience.

Finally, ask what the recorder setup will include at handover: recording schedules, time synchronisation, user accounts and a playback demonstration. A short walkthrough at handover is worth more than a manual left in a drawer.

A one-page survey decision sheet

The following is an author's worksheet, not a standard or a certification. Use it to leave the survey with decisions rather than notes. Copy the headings into a single page and fill it in during the walk.

Boundary: wall type, owner, and the document that confirms it. Mounting: proposed positions marked on a sketch, fixing surface named for each, and approval status for each. Cable route: path described, surfaces crossed, penetrations agreed, and who seals them. Coverage: each camera tied to a named gap, with the purpose stated. Recording: retention target and who set it. Viewing: named viewers, device type, and the revocation owner. Open items: each with a named owner and a target date.

A simple scoring habit helps prioritise. Mark each open item as blocking, meaning work cannot start until it is resolved, or non-blocking, meaning it can be settled before handover. Blocking items are usually boundary, mounting and route questions. Non-blocking items are often viewing accounts and retention settings.

When the sheet is complete, it becomes the brief the quote is priced against. That is the point of the exercise: a CCTV installation priced against agreed positions, agreed routes and agreed approvals is a far more stable number than one priced against a verbal description of a building.

Turning survey answers into a quote and a handover

The last set of questions is about the commercial and handover side, and it is worth asking before the survey ends.

Will the quote list camera positions by name, so each line can be matched to the sketch? Will it state what is included in the cable route and what is excluded, such as making good after drilling or sealing a penetration? Will it name the recorder location and the retention setting assumed? Will it describe what the handover includes, and who from your side will sign it off?

Ask also how changes are handled. If the neighbour objects to a bracket after the quote, or the landlord requires a different route, what is the process and who bears the cost of the change? Getting this in writing before work starts is ordinary procurement practice, not distrust.

For a shared-wall premises, the survey's real output is a set of agreed constraints. Once those are on paper, the technical work of camera placement, recorder setup and viewing access becomes a normal installation rather than a series of surprises.
