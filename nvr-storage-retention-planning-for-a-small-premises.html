Working Out NVR Retention Before You Buy Cameras

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical worked example for Sarawak premises managers: turn camera count, resolution and hours of cover into an NVR retention estimate, then put the right questions to your supplier before cameras are ordered.

Why retention should be settled before the camera order

Retention is the number of days of recorded video you can actually go back and watch. It is decided by three things working together: how many cameras record, how much data each camera produces, and how much storage the recorder holds. Buy cameras first and you inherit whatever retention the recorder happens to give you. Decide retention first and the camera and recorder choices follow from it.

This matters most when the reason for the system is review rather than live watching. A manager who needs to check an incident from last week needs days of cover, not just a working screen in the office. A manager who only wants to glance at the counter live needs far less. Those are different purchases, and the difference is storage.

The worked example below is an author heuristic for structuring that conversation. It is not a substitute for the supplier's own sizing, and it is not a promise about any particular recorder.

The four inputs you need before any estimate

You cannot size storage from a camera count alone. Gather four inputs first.

First, the camera list. How many cameras, and at what resolution will each record? A camera that records at a higher resolution produces more data per hour than one recording at a lower setting, so the recording profile matters as much as the model.

Second, the recording schedule. Will every camera record continuously, or only on motion, or on a schedule that follows trading hours? Continuous recording is the simplest to reason about because the data rate is steady.

Third, the hours of cover you need. Convert your retention goal into hours: seven days of continuous recording is 168 hours per camera. Write the number down; it becomes the target every later decision is tested against.

Fourth, the recorder's usable capacity. The NVR has a maximum number and size of drives it supports, and some of that capacity is consumed by the system itself. Ask for the usable figure, not the headline one.

A CCTV site survey is the natural place to collect these inputs, because camera count and recording profile are usually settled alongside camera placement. If the survey is done properly, the retention estimate is a short calculation rather than a guess.

A worked example for a small premises

Here is a fictional example to show the shape of the calculation. The numbers are illustrative only; your supplier should substitute real figures from the equipment being quoted.

Suppose a small shop in Sarawak plans eight cameras. Six record continuously at a moderate setting, and two at the entrance record at a higher setting. Assume, purely for this example, that a moderate camera produces about 2 GB per hour and a higher-setting camera about 4 GB per hour. That gives 6 × 2 + 2 × 4 = 20 GB per hour across the system.

Now apply the retention goal. For 14 days of continuous cover, multiply 20 GB per hour by 24 hours by 14 days: 20 × 24 × 14 = 6,720 GB, or roughly 6.7 TB. Add a working margin, say 20 percent, for system overhead and variation between scenes, and the target becomes about 8 TB of usable storage.

That single figure now drives the purchase. It tells you whether the quoted recorder has enough drive bays, whether the drive sizes proposed add up to the target, and whether the recording profile needs adjusting. If the quoted recorder only reaches 4 TB, you have a clear choice: accept fewer days of cover, reduce resolution on some cameras, switch some cameras to motion recording, or specify a larger recorder. Each of those is a decision you can make deliberately rather than discover later.

The same arithmetic works in reverse. If the supplier quotes a recorder with a known usable capacity, divide by the hourly data rate to see how many days it actually delivers at your chosen settings.

Turning the estimate into questions for the supplier

Bring the estimate to the quotation conversation as a short list of questions. The goal is to make the supplier show their working, not to test them.

Ask which recording profile each camera will use, and whether that profile is continuous, scheduled or motion-triggered. Ask for the estimated data rate per camera at that profile, and for the total daily data figure for the whole system. Ask for the usable storage capacity of the proposed recorder after system overhead, and how many drives that represents.

Then ask the reconciliation question: at the proposed settings and capacity, how many days of recording will the system hold? Compare the answer with your target. If it falls short, ask what would need to change to reach it.

Two further questions are worth adding. First, what happens when the drives are full, and how will you know if a drive has failed? Second, if you later add cameras, how much retention headroom remains? A recorder sized exactly to today's camera count leaves nothing for the camera you add next year.

NVR setup is where these answers become settings on the machine, so it helps to agree the recording profile in writing before installation day. That way the handover can be checked against the same numbers used in the quote.

Where retention and remote viewing meet

Retention planning does not stop at the recorder. If staff will check footage remotely, the way remote viewing is set up affects what they can retrieve and how quickly.

Decide who needs remote access, what they are expected to look for, and whether they need live view only or playback of recorded footage. Playback over a remote connection depends on the recorder's capacity and the network path, so it is worth testing during handover rather than assuming it will work on the first busy morning.

Keep the retention target visible in that conversation. A viewer who can only see the last few days because the recorder is undersized will ask for more storage soon after go-live. Settling retention first avoids that second purchase.

Eyonic's service scope includes remote viewing alongside recording, which makes it sensible to raise both in the same planning meeting. If the premises also uses a smart lock door access arrangement at a controlled entrance, note that the two systems are reviewed separately: smart locks work best when the door condition, user roles and backup method are reviewed before installation, and for business use the supplier usually checks whether a standalone smart lock or an access-control panel is more appropriate. That is a separate decision from camera retention, but both belong in the same overall security plan.

A one-page retention worksheet to take to the meeting

Before you request a quote, fill in a single page with these rows. It gives the supplier a fixed requirement instead of a vague brief.

Row one: camera count, split by recording profile. Row two: resolution or recording setting per group. Row three: recording schedule, with hours per day. Row four: retention goal in days, converted to hours. Row five: estimated data per hour for the system. Row six: total storage needed, including your chosen margin. Row seven: usable capacity of the proposed recorder. Row eight: the resulting days of cover, calculated back from the quote.

Add a short notes column for anything that would change the answer, such as a camera planned for later or a scene with constant movement that may raise the data rate. Keep the page with the quotation so the numbers can be compared across suppliers on the same basis.

When the installation is arranged, this page becomes the reference for NVR setup and for the handover check. If the recorder is configured differently from the agreed profile, the difference is visible immediately. That is the practical value of doing the arithmetic early: it turns retention from a vague hope into a number you can verify.

What to confirm before signing

Close the planning loop with three confirmations. First, that the quoted storage meets your retention target at the agreed recording profile, stated in days. Second, that the recorder has headroom for the cameras you expect to add. Third, that the handover will include a check of the actual recording settings against the agreed profile.

If any of these is unclear, ask for it in writing before the order is placed. A supplier who can show the arithmetic is easier to hold to the result than one who simply says the system will be sufficient.

For premises in Sarawak planning an ordinary CCTV installation, the sequence is straightforward: settle the retention target, collect the four inputs, run the estimate, and put the questions to the supplier. Cameras and recorders are then chosen to meet a stated requirement rather than the other way round.
