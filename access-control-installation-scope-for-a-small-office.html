Access Control Installation Scope for a Small Office: A Sign-Off Sheet

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical scope sheet for a small-office access control installation in Sarawak, listing doors, readers, exit buttons and the person who approves each addition so the job does not grow without sign-off.

Why a small office needs a written scope before installation

An access control installation starts as a short list of doors and quietly becomes a longer one. A staff member asks for a reader on the store room. Someone wants the back door on the same schedule. A contractor asks to be let in through the side entrance. Each request sounds small, and each one adds hardware, cabling, permissions and a person who must approve it. Without a written scope, the installer is left guessing which doors are in and which are out, and the manager is left explaining a bill that does not match the original conversation.

A scope sheet fixes the starting point. It records every door in the office, marks which ones are in the first phase, names the reader and exit method for each, and states who signs off any addition. It is a planning document, not a technical drawing, and it can be filled in by a premises manager before any supplier visits.

The sheet below is a template you can adapt; the example entries are fictional and exist only to show how the columns work.

The door register: the first table on the sheet

Start with a door register. Walk the office and give every door a number, including doors you do not intend to control in phase one. A register that lists only the doors you want makes it easy to forget the fire exit, the cleaner's cupboard or the door to the adjoining unit.

For each door, record four things: what the door is used for, who normally passes through it, whether it is an external or internal door, and whether it is in phase one. Add a column for the door's condition, because a door that does not close squarely or has a damaged frame will affect what can be fitted. The brand's own review of smart lock door access makes the same point in a narrower context: smart locks work best when the door condition, user roles and backup method are reviewed before installation. That principle applies to any controlled door, not only a smart lock.

A fictional example: Door 1, main entrance, staff and visitors, external, phase one, door closes cleanly. Door 2, rear service door, staff only, external, phase one, frame slightly warped. Door 3, manager's office, manager only, internal, phase two, good condition. The register now gives the installer a map and gives you a basis for asking what each door needs.

Readers, exit buttons and the access control system itself

The second table covers the hardware per door. For each door in phase one, note the entry method, the exit method and whether the door needs a request-to-exit button, a push bar or a simple handle. Keep the language plain: "card reader outside, exit button inside" is enough for a scope sheet. Leave the wiring, lock type and power details to the installer's survey.

This is where the access control system decision belongs. A small office may need only a few controlled doors, and the choice between a standalone smart lock and a panel-based system should follow the door register, not precede it. The brand's review notes that for business use, Eyonic usually checks whether a standalone smart lock or an access-control panel is more appropriate. That check is easier when the sheet already shows how many doors are involved, whether they are internal or external, and how many people need different levels of access.

Add a column for who approves each door's inclusion. A door added by a department head without the premises manager's sign-off is exactly the kind of quiet expansion the sheet is meant to catch.

User roles and the approval column

Access control is as much about people as hardware. The third table lists user groups rather than individuals: full-time staff, part-time staff, cleaners, contractors, visitors and management. For each group, note which doors they should reach and who authorises changes. Keep the table at group level so the sheet does not become a staff directory.

A fictional example: full-time staff reach Doors 1 and 2 during office hours; cleaners reach Door 2 only, escorted; contractors reach Door 1 with a named host; management reaches all phase-one doors. The approval column names the premises manager for staff changes and the office manager for contractor access. When a new person joins, the question is not "which code?" but "which group, and who approves?"

This is also where backup entry belongs. Every controlled door needs a documented fallback for the day a reader fails or a card is lost, and the scope sheet should say who holds that fallback and how it is used. The brand's product note describes smart lock door access as good for controlled convenience when user permissions and backup entry are planned properly. The scope sheet is where that planning happens, before installation rather than after.

What the sheet deliberately leaves out

A scope sheet is not an installation manual. It should not contain wiring diagrams, lock specifications, power calculations or instructions for overriding a door. Those belong to the installer's site survey and technical proposal. Keeping them out of the manager's sheet keeps the document readable and keeps the manager's role at the level of decisions rather than trade work.

It should also avoid naming a final price per door before the survey. A scope sheet records what is wanted and who approves it; the quote records what it costs. Mixing the two makes every later change a negotiation about money rather than a decision about scope.

Finally, the sheet should not assume that every door in the register will be controlled. Marking doors as phase two or "not controlled" is a useful outcome, because it shows the installer that the omission was a decision, not an oversight.

A worked sign-off routine for the first week

Once the sheet is filled in, give it a simple routine. Before the installer's survey, the premises manager reviews the door register and confirms phase one. During the survey, the installer adds notes to each door and flags anything that changes the plan. After the survey, any door added to phase one needs the named approver's initials and a date in the approval column.

A fictional example of the routine in use: the office manager asks for the store room to be added. The premises manager checks the register, sees the store room is listed as phase two, and asks the installer for a separate note on what the addition involves. The note goes to the approver, who either initials the change or defers it. Nothing is installed on a verbal request.

This routine is an author's suggestion, not a standard. Adapt the timing to your own project. The value is that every addition has a name and a date attached to it, so the final handover matches the scope that was agreed.

Handing the sheet to the installer and keeping it current

The completed sheet becomes part of the project file. Give a copy to the installer before the survey and keep the master with the premises manager. When the installation is handed over, compare the installed doors against the sheet: any difference is either an approved addition or an open question.

Keep the sheet after handover as the record of who approved what. It is useful when a new manager takes over, when a supplier changes, or when someone asks why a particular door is not controlled.
