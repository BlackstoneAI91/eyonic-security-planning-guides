Preparing an Emergency Contact List That Stays Useful During an Office Move

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical guide for Sarawak premises managers on building an emergency contact list that survives an office move: naming role owners, separating internal and external contacts, recording schedule changes, and keeping personal data out of shared copies.

Start With Roles, Not Names

An office move breaks a contact list in a predictable way: the people stay the same, but their desks, extensions and working hours change. If the list is built around names first, every entry needs editing at once. If it is built around roles first, most of the list survives the move untouched.

Begin with a role column. Typical roles for a premises with security equipment include the premises manager, the person who holds the alarm account, the person who approves after-hours access, the reception lead, and a nominated deputy for each. Write the role, then the current holder, then a review date. When someone leaves or changes desk, you replace one holder and the role stays intact.

Keep the list short enough to be read under pressure. A single page with eight to twelve roles is more useful than a directory of forty names. For each role, record only what a caller needs: the role title, an internal extension or desk reference, and the hours that role is normally covered. Do not paste in personal mobile numbers on a shared copy; hold those in a separate controlled record and note who can retrieve them.

Decision for this section: circle the roles that must be reachable on the first working day after the move, and mark any role with no named deputy. Those gaps are the ones to close before removal day, not after.

Separate Internal Owners From External Suppliers

A contact list that mixes colleagues and suppliers in one alphabetical block becomes hard to use during a move, because the two groups are reached differently. Split the page into two blocks.

The internal block covers people inside the organisation: premises, reception, facilities, the person authorised to speak to suppliers, and the person who signs off completed work. The external block covers the suppliers and service providers who support the premises. For each external entry, record the company, the service it provides, the account or reference the company uses, and the internal role that is allowed to raise a request. Do not record passwords, codes or entry instructions in this document.

Where security equipment is part of the move, the external block should name the provider for each system separately, because one supplier may not cover everything. A provider that plans, supplies and installs monitoring systems with camera placement, recording and remote viewing for homes, offices, shops and industrial sites is a single point of contact for that scope, but alarm, door access and telephone systems may sit with different teams. Eyonic Security Systems is the brand identity used here for context; the point for your list is that each system gets its own row and its own named internal owner.

Decision for this section: for every external row, write the internal role permitted to contact that supplier. Any row without an owner is a row that will be used by whoever happens to be nearest the phone.

Record the Move Schedule Against Each Contact

The most common failure in a move is not a missing name; it is a correct name attached to an out-of-date schedule. A supplier told to attend on Friday when the office is now closed until Monday is a wasted visit and a frustrated team.

Add a schedule column to the list with three entries per role or supplier: the date the current arrangement ends, the date the new arrangement starts, and the date the entry was last confirmed. During the move window, treat the middle of that range as uncertain and confirm before acting. A short confirmation note in the list, such as "confirmed by phone on the Tuesday before the move", is enough; you do not need a long narrative.

Build a small change log at the foot of the page. Each line records the date, what changed, and which role confirmed it. This is the part of the document that makes it trustworthy three weeks later, when nobody remembers which version of the schedule was circulated.

If the move includes a burglar alarm system, the schedule column matters more than usual, because the arming and disarming routine is tied to who is on site and when. Record the role that holds responsibility for the alarm during the transition and the date that responsibility transfers. Keep the detail at the level of who and when, not how the system is operated.

Decision for this section: set a single review date in the middle of the move week and assign one role to run it.

Write the List So It Can Be Handed Over

A contact list is only as good as the person who can pick it up cold. Test yours by asking a colleague who was not involved in drafting it to answer three questions from the page alone: who do I call about the alarm, who approves a supplier visit after hours, and who owns the telephone system during the move.

If any answer requires asking a second person, the list needs a clearer role description. Write each role in plain language, for example "approves after-hours supplier attendance" rather than a job title alone, because titles vary between offices and mean little to a new starter.

Keep one master copy in a controlled location and note where it lives. Circulate extracts rather than the whole page: reception may need the internal block and the supplier names, while the finance role may need only the account references. This limits how widely personal details travel.

When the move involves several systems, a short handover note per system helps the incoming manager. For door access, note who approves changes to user permissions and where the written record of those approvals is kept. Smart lock door access works best when user permissions and a backup entry method are planned properly, so the contact list should name the role that owns that planning rather than the individual who happens to hold a key today.

Decision for this section: run the three-question test before the move and again in the first week after it.

Keep Personal Data Out of Shared Copies

An emergency contact list is a working document, not a staff directory. The difference matters during a move, when copies get printed, photographed and forwarded in a hurry.

Apply a simple rule: shared copies carry roles, extensions and company contacts; personal mobile numbers and home details stay in a separate controlled record with a named custodian. If a role genuinely needs an out-of-hours number, record the role that holds it and how to reach the custodian, not the number itself.

Review what is printed. A printed copy left at a temporary reception desk is the most likely place for a list to be seen by someone who should not have it. Number the printed copies, note who holds each one, and collect them at the end of the move. Treat the collection as a task with an owner and a date, not an afterthought.

When you share the list with a supplier, send only the rows that supplier needs. A security provider does not need the full internal directory, and reception does not need supplier account references. Narrow extracts are easier to keep current and easier to withdraw if a relationship ends.

Decision for this section: write down who holds each printed copy and the date it will be collected. If you cannot answer that for a copy, destroy it and reprint from the master.

Review the List After the First Week

The first week in a new office exposes the entries that looked fine on paper. Schedule a short review once the team has settled, and use it to correct rather than to rewrite.

Work through four checks. First, confirm that every role still has a named holder and a deputy. Second, confirm that each supplier row still matches the service actually in place, since a move is a common moment for a provider to change. Third, confirm the schedule column reflects the new opening hours and any temporary arrangements that have become permanent. Fourth, confirm the change log has an entry for every correction made during the week.

Where security systems were part of the move, ask the internal owner for each system to confirm in writing that the contact details on the list match what the supplier holds. A mismatch here is worth catching early, because it usually surfaces at the worst possible moment.

Keep the review short and dated. A fifteen-minute review with a written outcome is more valuable than an annual tidy-up, and it gives the next premises manager a clear starting point. If a system was added during the move, such as a new access control system, add its row and its owner at the same review rather than waiting for the next cycle.

Decision for this section: book the review date before the move happens, and name the role that will run it.

A One-Page Template You Can Adapt

The template below is an example structure, not a fixed standard. Adjust the columns to your premises and keep the page to one side of A4 where possible.

Header: premises name, version number, date of last review, and the role that owns the document.

Block one, internal roles: role title, plain-language description of what the role decides, internal extension or desk reference, deputy role, and hours covered.

Block two, external suppliers: company, service provided, account reference, internal role permitted to contact them, and the date the arrangement was last confirmed.

Block three, schedule: current arrangement end date, new arrangement start date, and confirmation note.

Footer: change log with date, change and confirming role, plus a line recording where the master copy is held and who holds each printed copy.

Two practical notes. First, keep the descriptions of what each role decides, because that is what makes the list usable by someone new. Second, resist the urge to add columns for information you would not act on during a move; every extra column makes the page harder to read under pressure.

If your premises uses several security systems, consider a companion page per system that records the internal owner, the supplier, and the review date. That keeps the main list short while still giving each system a clear line of responsibility.
