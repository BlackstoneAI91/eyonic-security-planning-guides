Smart Lock User and Code Governance Plan: A Register Template for Sarawak Premises

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical governance plan for smart lock door access: a user-and-code register template, rotation and revocation rules, and a worked example for a small office with staff turnover.

Why a code register matters more than the lock itself

A smart lock is often bought for convenience: fewer keys to cut, fewer keys to lose. But convenience without a register becomes its own problem. If nobody can say who holds which code, when it was issued, or whether it still works, then the lock has simply moved the risk from a key drawer to a spreadsheet nobody maintains.

This guide is for premises managers in Sarawak planning ordinary security-system projects, where a smart lock is one part of a wider setup rather than a standalone gadget. It gives you a register template, a rotation and revocation routine, and a worked example for a small office with staff turnover. Treat the timings and scores here as author heuristics and examples, not as established research or a standard you must follow.

One framing point before the template. Smart lock door access is good for controlled convenience when user permissions and backup entry are planned properly. That sentence is the whole governance argument in miniature: permissions and backup are planned, not discovered on the day someone leaves.

A register is not a security product. It is a document that makes the lock's behaviour explainable after the fact. Keep it where the person who approves access can reach it, and decide in advance who else may read it.

Decide the model first: standalone lock or access-control panel

Before writing a single row in a register, settle what kind of system you are governing. A standalone smart lock usually stores its own users and codes on the device. An access-control system typically centralises users, schedules and audit records at a panel or management platform. The governance burden differs between the two, and so does the register.

For business use, the sensible first question is whether a standalone smart lock or an access-control panel is more appropriate. That is a scoping decision, not a brand preference, and it should be made with the door condition, the user roles and the backup method in view. Smart locks work best when those three things are reviewed before installation rather than after.

A simple decision test you can run yourself:

If the door serves one small team, the user list changes rarely, and a mechanical key or a second entrance covers backup, a standalone lock with a tidy register may be enough.

If the door is shared across departments, needs time-based rules, or must produce a record of who entered when, an access-control panel is usually the better fit, and your register becomes a summary of a system that already logs events.

If you are unsure, write both options into your project brief with the door and user counts, and ask the installer to explain the trade-off in writing. A supplier who plans, supplies and installs security systems will normally welcome a scoped question over a vague one.

The register template: columns that earn their place

A register only works if it is short enough to update. The template below is deliberately lean. Add columns only when a real decision depends on them.

Column 1, User ID. A neutral identifier, not a name, if the register may be seen by people who do not need to know who holds access. Keep the name-to-ID mapping in a separate, more restricted list.

Column 2, Role. Choose from a short fixed list, for example manager, keyholder, cleaner, contractor, temporary. Roles drive rotation frequency, so keep the list small and stable.

Column 3, Door or zone. Which door the credential opens. If a user needs two doors, that is two rows, not one row with a comma.

Column 4, Credential type. Code, card, fob, phone app, or mechanical key. Record each separately; a person may hold more than one.

Column 5, Issued date and issued by. Who approved it and when. This is the column that answers the awkward question later.

Column 6, Review or expiry date. Set it when the credential is issued, not when someone remembers.

Column 7, Status. Active, suspended, revoked. Use the same three words every time.

Column 8, Revoked date and method. When access was removed and how, for example code deleted at the lock, card disabled at the panel, or key returned and logged.

Column 9, Backup entry note. For each door, record what happens if the lock fails or the battery runs down. This is a planning note, not an instruction sheet, and the detail belongs with your installer.

Two supporting documents sit beside the register: a door list (each door, its lock type, and its backup method) and a change log (every addition, suspension and revocation, with a date). Neither needs to be elaborate. Both need an owner.

Rotation and revocation rules you can actually keep

Rotation schedules fail when they are ambitious. A rule that says every code changes monthly will be quietly abandoned by the second month. Pick a cadence you can defend and actually run, and write down why you chose it.

A workable set of example rules, to adapt rather than copy:

Role-based rotation. Manager and keyholder credentials rotate on a fixed cycle you set, for example every six months. Shared or temporary credentials rotate at the end of each engagement, not on a calendar.

Event-based rotation. Any of the following triggers an immediate review: a credential holder leaves, a device is lost, a code is suspected to have been shared, or a contractor's work period ends.

One person, one credential. Shared codes defeat the register, because revocation then means changing the code for everyone. Where a shared code is unavoidable, mark it clearly in the register and rotate it more often.

Revocation is a two-step act. First suspend, then confirm. Suspending at the panel or lock is step one; confirming the credential no longer opens the door is step two, and the confirmation goes in the change log with a date.

Named approver. One person approves new credentials and revocations. A deputy is named for leave periods. Without a named approver, the register drifts.

Backup entry is reviewed at the same time. Every rotation is a prompt to check that the backup method still works and that the person who holds it is still the right person.

None of this requires technical knowledge of the lock. It requires a calendar, a named owner and the discipline to log the boring entries.

Worked example: a small office with staff turnover

The following is a fictional example, used only to show how the template behaves under pressure.

A small office in Sarawak has one main entrance with a smart lock, a rear door on a mechanical key, and eight staff. The office manager is the named approver, with the admin lead as deputy. The register has eleven active rows: eight staff codes, one cleaner code on a temporary role, one contractor code due to expire at the end of a three-month engagement, and one shared code for the manager and deputy as a fallback.

Month one. A new staff member joins. The manager adds a row with role staff, door main entrance, credential type code, issued date, a review date six months out, status active, and a backup note pointing to the rear door key held by the admin lead. The change log records the addition.

Month two. The contractor's engagement ends early. The manager suspends the contractor row, confirms the code no longer opens the door, and records the revocation date and method. The row stays in the register with status revoked rather than being deleted, so the history is visible.

Month four. A staff member resigns with one week's notice. On the last working day the manager suspends the code, confirms it fails at the door, and logs the revocation. The mechanical key, if one was issued, is returned and logged in the same entry.

Month six. The review date arrives for four staff rows. The manager checks each holder is still employed in the same role, rotates the shared fallback code, and confirms the rear door key is still with the admin lead. Two rows are extended, one is rotated, one is closed because the person moved to a different site.

The register is not glamorous. Its value shows up in month four, when the question "can the leaver still get in?" has a written answer rather than a shrug.

Where the register meets the rest of your security setup

A smart lock rarely stands alone. The same premises often has cameras, an alarm and a gate, and the register should connect to those systems at the level of ownership rather than wiring.

Three handoffs worth writing down:

Who reviews door activity. If the lock or panel records entries, name the person who reviews them and how often. Keep the review privacy-conscious: a record of who entered is personal data, and access to it should be limited to people with a reason to look.

Who holds the camera and recording credentials. If your site also runs CCTV security systems, the same governance questions apply to the recorder: who has an account, when it rotates, and how access is removed. A single owner for both registers is often simpler than two.

Who responds to an alarm or gate event. If a door is forced or left open, the register should say who is called and in what order. That contact list belongs with the alarm and gate documentation, not buried in the lock manual.

For premises that need centralised user management, schedules and audit records across several doors, an access control system installation is the natural next step, and the register becomes a summary of what the panel already enforces. For a single door with a small team, the standalone lock plus this register is usually the lighter option. Either way, the decision should be recorded in your project brief with the door count and user count that drove it.

A one-page acceptance check before you sign off

Before you accept a smart lock installation, run a short check that the governance plan is real rather than aspirational. Score each item yes, no, or not yet. The scoring is an author heuristic, not a certified audit.

The register exists and has an owner. Yes means a named person, not a department.

Every active credential has a row, a role, an issued date and a review date.

Every door has a backup entry note, and the backup method has been confirmed to work by the person who will use it.

The approver and deputy are named, and both know where the register lives.

A revocation has been tested end to end at least once, with the confirmation logged.

Door activity records, if any, have a named reviewer and a stated review frequency.

If the site also has cameras, the recorder account holder is named in the same document set.

A practical tip: ask the installer to walk you through adding a user, suspending a user and confirming a suspension, and write the steps in your own words while they are there. The steps belong in your handover file, not in your head. If the door condition, user roles or backup method were not reviewed before installation, raise it now rather than after the first staff departure.
