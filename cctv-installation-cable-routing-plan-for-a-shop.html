CCTV Installation Cable Routing Plan for a Shop: A Checklist

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical checklist for Sarawak shop managers to plan cable routes, conduit placement and power points before CCTV installation, so the quote matches the site and rework is reduced.

Why the cable route deserves its own plan

Camera positions get most of the attention in a shop security project, but the cable route is what decides how much disruption the work causes and how tidy the finished job looks. A route sketched on the back of a quotation is not a plan. Before you compare suppliers, walk the shop with a simple drawing and mark where each cable will travel from camera to recorder, where it will be hidden, and where it will cross a public area.

Treat this checklist as your own preparation document. It does not replace a supplier's site survey, and it does not tell an installer how to do their job. It simply means that when the survey happens, you already know the constraints and can ask better questions.

One working rule: if you cannot describe the route in words to a colleague, it is not planned yet. "From the front camera, up the wall beside the shutter box, across the ceiling void above the display, down the service column to the recorder cabinet" is a plan. "Somewhere through the ceiling" is a hope.

Start with a route map, not a shopping list

Before discussing brands or camera counts, produce a one-page route map of the shop. Use a floor sketch and mark four things: each candidate camera position, the recorder location, every power point you believe is available, and the paths you would prefer cables to take. Number each camera so the map, the quotation and the final handover all use the same references.

A useful habit is to draw two versions. The first is the ideal route, hidden above ceilings and inside trunking. The second is the fallback route for any position where the ceiling is solid, the wall is tiled, or a landlord forbids drilling. Having both on paper means a surprise during the survey becomes a choice rather than a delay.

This is also the moment to note what the shop does during trading hours. A route that crosses a busy aisle at head height will need surface trunking, and that trunking will be visible to customers every day. If that is unacceptable, the route has to change before anyone quotes, not after the first cable is clipped in place.

Keep the map with your other project documents. It becomes the reference for later additions, and it is far easier to extend a numbered drawing than to reconstruct where cables run two years from now.

The pre-installation routing checklist

Work through these items with the person who will sign the quotation. Each one is a question to answer, not a box to tick without thinking.

1. Camera positions confirmed. Every position on the map has a purpose: entrance, counter, stockroom door, cash handling area, loading point. Positions without a stated purpose should be removed. 2. Recorder location agreed. The recorder needs a ventilated, lockable position that is not in a customer-facing area and not in a spot that floods or overheats. 3. Power points counted. List every point the recorder, monitor and any active equipment will need, and note which are already in use. 4. Cable path per camera. For each numbered camera, write the route in one sentence, including where it enters the ceiling or trunking. 5. Surface versus concealed. Mark which runs will be visible and confirm that the shop accepts the appearance. 6. Crossing points. Note every doorway, aisle and wall the cable must pass, and who owns that wall if the unit is rented. 7. Landlord and building constraints. Record any rule about drilling, facade changes or common-area work before the survey. 8. Future additions. Note where a second phase might add a camera, so the first phase does not block it. 9. Sign-off owner. Name the person who approves route changes on the day.

A completed checklist is not a guarantee of a smooth job, but it turns vague assumptions into stated decisions. When a supplier's surveyor arrives, you can hand over the map and the list and spend the visit on the questions that actually need a site answer.

Conduit, trunking and the appearance question

Conduit and trunking are the visible signature of a CCTV installation. In a shop, that matters more than in a warehouse, because customers see the ceiling and walls. Decide early how much visible containment you will accept, and where.

A practical approach is to divide the shop into three zones. Back-of-house areas such as stockrooms and service corridors can usually take surface trunking without complaint. Customer-facing walls and ceilings deserve concealed routes or carefully aligned containment that follows existing architectural lines. Entrances and shutters need a route that survives daily opening and closing without strain on the cable.

Ask the surveyor to mark on your map where containment will be visible, and in what colour or finish. If a run will cross a painted feature wall, that is worth knowing before installation day rather than after. For rented units, confirm in writing whether the landlord requires specific containment or restoration at the end of the tenancy.

None of this is exotic. It is the ordinary detail that separates a tidy job from a functional one. The point of planning it now is that containment decisions affect the cable route, and the cable route affects the quotation.

Power points, recorder placement and NVR setup

Cable routing and power planning are usually discussed separately, which is a mistake. The recorder needs power, the monitor needs power, and any active network equipment needs power, all in the same cabinet or corner. If that corner has one socket already feeding a fridge, the plan is not finished.

Walk the proposed recorder position and count what is there. Note the distance to the nearest spare socket, whether the position is ventilated, and whether the cabinet can be locked. A recorder tucked above a cooking area or beside a hot display will have a shorter, more troublesome life than one in a cool back office.

Routing also affects NVR setup. The number of cable runs, the length of each run and the way they terminate all influence how neatly the recorder can be commissioned. Ask the supplier to explain, in plain terms, how each camera will be identified at the recorder and how you will confirm during handover that every numbered camera on your map appears where expected. A worked example: a small shophouse unit

Consider a fictional single-storey shophouse selling household goods, with a front shutter, a central aisle, a counter on the left and a stockroom at the rear. The manager wants four cameras: entrance, counter, aisle and stockroom door.

On the route map, the entrance camera sits above the shutter and its cable runs up the wall beside the shutter box, then across the ceiling void to a back-office cabinet. The counter camera drops directly into the cabinet through the partition wall. The aisle camera is the awkward one: the ceiling above the aisle is a solid slab, so the fallback route uses surface trunking along the top of the shelving, painted to match. The stockroom camera runs through the rear wall into the same cabinet.

Power is counted at the cabinet: one socket for the recorder, one for the monitor, one spare. The landlord's rule about not drilling the front facade is noted, which is why the entrance cable stays inside. The sign-off owner is the shop manager, with the landlord contactable for any common-area question.

This example is invented to show the shape of the document, not a template for your site. Your own map will have different constraints, and the surveyor may propose a better route. The value is that the conversation starts from specifics.

Handing the plan to the installer

A routing plan is only useful if it reaches the people doing the work. Bring the numbered map and the completed checklist to the site survey, and ask the surveyor to mark any changes directly on your copy. Keep the annotated version as the agreed record.

During installation, route changes are common and usually reasonable. What matters is that each change is recorded against a camera number and approved by the named owner, so the final handover matches what was built. At handover, walk the routes with the installer and confirm that visible containment is where you agreed and that every camera on the map is live at the recorder.

If the shop later adds a smart lock door access point or another system, the same map becomes the starting document for that conversation. Eyonic's own review notes that smart locks work best when door condition, user roles and backup entry are reviewed before installation, which is the same principle applied to a different piece of equipment: decide on paper first, then install. For CCTV installation in a Sarawak shop, a clear cable routing plan is the cheapest form of rework prevention available.
