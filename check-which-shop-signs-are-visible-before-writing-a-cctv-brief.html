Check Which Shop Signs Are Visible Before Writing a CCTV Brief

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical guide for Sarawak premises managers on recording shop signage, maintenance ownership and visitor communication before a CCTV brief is written, with worksheets and example timings.

Why signage belongs in the brief, not the afterthought

A CCTV brief usually opens with cameras, recorders and cabling. Signage tends to arrive late, often after the installer has already fixed mounting heights and cable routes. That order is backwards for a shop, because the signs a visitor sees at the entrance shape how the premises communicates that cameras are present, who to contact, and what happens at the door after hours. If the brief does not describe the signs, the installer has to guess, and guesses become variations.

Start by treating signage as a scope item with an owner. For each sign, record four things: what it says in plain words, where it is fixed, who approved the wording, and who replaces it when it fades or a tenant changes. This is ordinary project documentation, not a legal opinion. Questions about what a notice must legally contain, or where privacy rules apply, should go to qualified staff or advisers rather than being settled in a supplier meeting.

Walk the shopfront and list every sign a visitor can see

Before writing anything, stand outside the shop at the times customers actually arrive. Morning, midday and closing time often show different signs because shutters, display racks and parked vehicles change what is visible. Walk the approach from both directions and note each sign in the order a visitor meets it.

Use a simple three-column list: sign text, location, and visibility note. A fictional example for a hardware shop might read: opening hours board, left of the shutter, partly blocked by a display rack after 10am; unit number plate, above the door, clear from the main walkway; after-hours contact notice, inside the glass, unreadable when the interior lights are off. The visibility note is the part that earns its place, because it tells the installer and the sign maker what to fix.

Add a column for who owns each sign. Landlord signs, tenant signs and shared building notices often have different owners, and a brief that blurs them creates arguments later. If a sign belongs to the building rather than the shop, say so explicitly and name the person who approves changes. Keep the list to one page so it can travel with the brief.

Turn the sign list into camera placement decisions

The sign list is not only a communications record. It is a placement input. A camera aimed at the entrance will also capture whatever signage sits in that view, and a sign that blocks a sightline is a placement problem before it is a design problem.

Take each camera position you are considering and mark, on a printed floor plan or a simple sketch, which signs fall inside the intended view. A fictional example: a camera above the till looking toward the entrance may have its lower frame filled by a hanging promotional banner, so the brief should note that the banner is seasonal and ask how the view is checked when it changes. You are not designing the system; you are giving the installer the facts that affect camera placement.

This is also the moment to record mounting constraints created by signs. If a lightbox or fascia panel occupies the wall where a camera would sit, write that down with a photo reference in the brief. The installer can then propose an alternative rather than discovering the conflict on the day. Keep the language descriptive: what is there, what it blocks, and who can approve moving it.

Agree maintenance ownership before the quote is finalised

Signs fade, crack and get covered by new promotions. A brief that names no maintenance owner tends to produce a system where the camera is serviced on schedule and the notice beside it is not. Decide, in writing, who checks each sign, how often, and what triggers a replacement.

A workable pattern is a short register with three fields: sign, checker role, and review interval. Example intervals, offered as author heuristics rather than researched standards, might be a monthly visual check by the shop supervisor and a fuller review whenever the shopfront changes. Record the review date so it does not depend on memory.

Separate the maintenance of physical signs from the maintenance of the security system itself. The installer's scope may cover cameras, recording and remote viewing, but that does not automatically include replacing a faded notice. Write the boundary into the brief so both sides know where their work stops. If the same visit could cover both, say so and name who confirms completion. This keeps the handover clean and avoids a situation where each party assumes the other is responsible.

Describe ordinary visitor communication in plain steps

The brief should describe what an ordinary visitor experiences, in sequence, without drifting into legal interpretation. A short narrative works better than a policy document. For example: a visitor approaches, sees the opening hours board, enters, and is greeted at the counter; after closing, the shutter is down and the after-hours notice directs enquiries to the published channel.

Write that sequence down and mark where a sign carries the message. Where no sign carries it, note the gap. This gives the project team a shared picture and shows the installer which entrance views matter most. It also helps reception or counter staff explain the setup to customers without improvising.

Keep personal data out of this section. Do not list staff names, phone numbers or individual schedules in a document that will be circulated to suppliers. Refer to roles instead, such as counter supervisor or duty manager, and keep the contact detail in a separate controlled list. If the shop also uses door access, the same discipline applies: describe the visitor's experience of the door, not the credentials behind it.

A one-page sign-and-scope worksheet to attach to the brief

Consolidate the work into a single attachment so the brief stays readable. A practical layout has five blocks: the sign list with visibility notes; the camera positions affected by signage; the maintenance register with checker roles and review intervals; the visitor sequence in plain steps; and a sign-off block naming who approves wording, who approves placement, and who confirms completion.

Add a short open-questions list. Typical entries might be: who owns the fascia sign, whether the seasonal banner returns next quarter, and which role checks the after-hours notice. Open questions are useful because they show the installer where decisions are still pending rather than hiding them inside assumptions.

When the project includes more than cameras, group the related destinations by reader intent. A premises manager comparing door hardware will want the access control discussion kept separate from the camera discussion, even when both appear in one brief. Review the brief before the site survey visit

Give the draft brief and the sign worksheet to the person attending the CCTV site survey, and ask them to mark anything the walk-through contradicts. A sign that looked clear from the pavement may be hidden from the counter, and a camera position that looked simple on paper may sit behind a projecting sign. The survey is where those differences get settled.

Set a short review window before the visit, for example a few working days, and label it as a suggested timing rather than a rule. Use the time to confirm the sign owners and to close the open questions that affect placement. After the survey, update the worksheet with what changed and keep the earlier version, so the brief shows its own history.

Finally, check that the brief answers the reader's real question: what will be visible, who maintains it, and who confirms the work is done. If those three answers are on one page, the CCTV installation discussion starts from a fixed requirement instead of a walk-around guess. That is the practical value of checking shop signs first.
