Prepare a Privacy-Safe Visitor FAQ for a Newly Managed Building Entrance

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical guide for Sarawak premises managers writing a visitor FAQ for a newly managed building entrance: what to publish, what to leave out, who owns each answer, and how to keep the page current without collecting personal records.

Start with the decisions the FAQ must support

A visitor FAQ is not a brochure. It is a short set of answers that lets a first-time visitor reach the right person without being asked to hand over information they should not need to give at the door. Before drafting a single question, write down the three decisions the page should support. A workable example set is: how do I announce myself, who do I ask for when something is wrong, and what should I do if I arrive outside the published hours. Each decision becomes one section of the FAQ and one named owner inside the building team.

Keep the scope deliberately narrow. A newly managed entrance usually needs to explain arrival, reporting and escalation. It does not need to explain how doors open, which equipment is fitted or how the building is watched. Those details belong in internal documents held by authorised staff, not on a public page. If a question can only be answered by describing a device, a credential or a route through the building, it is probably the wrong question for a visitor FAQ.

Write the owner's role next to each draft question, for example reception supervisor or facilities coordinator. A question with no owner will drift out of date within a month of the building opening.

Write the arrival answer without exposing the access control system

The arrival answer is the hardest to write well. Visitors want to know what to do; they should not be told how the entrance works. A safe pattern is to describe the visible, ordinary steps: where to stand, which counter or intercom point to use, and what a visitor should say. Keep the wording about people and places rather than about hardware.

Where the building uses an access control system, the FAQ can say that entry is arranged by the reception team and that visitors should wait to be admitted. It should not describe credentials, codes, reader behaviour or which door is unlocked at which hour. The same restraint applies to smart lock door access in shared rooms: the visitor needs to know who to ask, not how the lock is operated. Eyonic's own product notes make the point that smart locks work best when door condition, user roles and a backup method are reviewed before installation, which is a planning conversation for the building team rather than a visitor topic.

Test the draft by reading it aloud as if you were a courier with two minutes and no prior knowledge. If the answer still makes sense, it is probably pitched correctly.

Decide what visitors may be asked, and what they never should be

A visitor FAQ should state plainly what information the reception team will ask for and what it will not. A short, honest list works better than a long policy paragraph. For example: we will ask your name, who you are visiting and the purpose of your visit. We will not ask you to leave identity documents at the counter, and we will not ask you to share a code or a device.

This is where privacy-safe drafting matters most. Do not publish a sample form, a specimen logbook page or a filled-in example with realistic names, plate numbers or telephone numbers. Use placeholders such as Visitor A and Company B if an example is genuinely needed. Do not describe where completed records are stored, who can read them or how long they are kept unless the building's own approved policy says so and the responsible owner has signed it off.

A useful worksheet column is: question, answer, information collected, owner. If the information collected column is not empty for a question that does not need it, rewrite the question. The goal is to reduce what is gathered at the door, not to explain how it is handled afterwards.

Give visitors a clear reporting route and a named role

Visitors rarely need to know the building's security arrangements, but they do need to know where to report something odd. Write one short paragraph covering three situations: something that looks unsafe, a delay at the entrance, and a complaint about how a visitor was treated. For each, name the role that receives the report and the ordinary channel, such as the reception counter during published hours.

Keep the wording about roles, not individuals. A FAQ that lists a named officer's mobile number becomes wrong the week that person changes role, and it publishes personal contact details that should not sit on a public page. Write Facilities Coordinator, not a person's name.

If the building has a queue management system at reception, the FAQ can explain in one sentence that visitors take a ticket and wait for their category to be called, and that staff at the counter can help if the category is unclear. That is a description of ordinary visitor experience, not an operating instruction. Eyonic lists queue management among the systems it supplies, so if the building team is still choosing, the question of how visitors are sequenced belongs in the project brief rather than in the published FAQ.

Keep equipment and provider details out of the public page

It is tempting to reassure visitors by describing the building's security provision. Resist it. A public FAQ that names camera positions, recording arrangements or remote viewing capability tells a reader more than a visitor needs and dates quickly when the service changes.

A better approach is a single neutral sentence: the building is managed and monitored by the facilities team, and visitors should follow staff instructions at the entrance. Everything else about CCTV security systems, camera placement, NVR setup and remote CCTV viewing belongs in the building's internal handover pack, held by the manager and the appointed provider. Eyonic's published service scope covers planning, supplying and installing monitoring systems with camera placement, recording and remote viewing for homes, offices, shops and industrial sites, which is the kind of detail a procurement file needs and a visitor page does not.

Apply the same rule to other trades. Autogate installation, burglar alarm system work and keyphone system changes are project topics for the building team. The visitor FAQ only needs to say who to contact when something at the entrance is not working.

Assign an owner, a review date and a change route

A FAQ written at handover and never reviewed becomes a source of wrong answers. Give the page an owner, a review date and a simple change route before it goes live. A practical pattern is: the facilities coordinator owns the content, reception staff may propose changes, and any change to the arrival or reporting answers needs the building manager's approval.

Set the first review for a short interval after opening, for example four to six weeks, then move to a longer cycle. These intervals are author heuristics, not a standard; choose what fits the building's activity. At each review, read the page against three tests. Does every answer still name a current role? Has any answer crept into describing equipment? Has anyone added a real name, number or example record?

Keep the review record short and separate from the public page: date, reviewer role, questions changed, approval. Do not store visitor records alongside it. If the building team is planning entrance works and needs a starting point for the technical conversation, the access control system installation page is the right place to look, while the FAQ stays focused on what a visitor reads at the door.

A short pre-publication checklist

Before the FAQ goes live, run a five-minute check with the person who will answer the counter. Read each question and confirm that the answer matches what actually happens on a normal day. Then confirm the following, marking each item pass or revise.

One: every answer names a role, not a person. Two: no answer describes how a door, lock, reader or gate is operated. Three: no sample record, plate number, telephone number or identity detail appears anywhere on the page. Four: the reporting route is stated once, clearly, with the hours it applies. Five: the page has a named owner and a review date recorded elsewhere. Six: any question that needs a policy decision has been referred to the responsible owner rather than answered on the page.

If an item fails, remove the answer rather than soften it. A shorter FAQ with six reliable answers serves a new building better than a long one with two that are wrong.
