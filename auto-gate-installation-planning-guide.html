What to Document Before an Autogate Site Survey

Prepared for Eyonic Security System (Eyonic CCTV)

A practical documentation guide for owners and facilities teams in Sarawak preparing for an autogate site survey, covering swing clearance versus sliding runback, grade, drainage, power and vehicle movement, with safety checks left to the installer.

Why a Documentation Pack Beats a Verbal Brief

An autogate survey goes quickly when the installer arrives with a clear picture of the site and slowly when the visit turns into a fact-finding exercise. The difference is usually paperwork, not technical knowledge. Before anyone measures a driveway, gather the documents that describe what the gate must do, who uses it, and what constraints the property already has. This is not about specifying motors or wiring; it is about handing over accurate context so the surveyor can focus on the physical assessment.

Think of the pack as three layers. The first is property context: a simple site sketch, the property boundary, and where vehicles enter and leave. The second is usage context: how many vehicles pass daily, whether staff or visitors arrive at set times, and whether anyone needs pedestrian access beside the gate. The third is constraint context: power availability, drainage paths, slope, and any existing structures the gate must avoid. Each layer answers a question the surveyor would otherwise ask on site, and each one is easier to prepare calmly at a desk than in a hurry at the gate.

Keep the pack factual and dated. A one-page summary with a sketch and a short list of open questions is more useful than a thick file of unrelated documents. If the premises already has security equipment, note it, because gate planning often sits alongside wider CCTV security systems decisions and the surveyor may need to understand how the entrance fits the rest of the site. The goal is a shared starting point, not a finished design.

Draw the Entrance: Swing Clearance Versus Sliding Runback

The single most useful document you can bring is a measured sketch of the entrance. Mark the gate opening width, the driveway edges, and any walls, pillars, drains or planting that sit close to the gate line. Then note which gate type you are considering, because swing and sliding gates consume space in completely different ways. A swing gate needs a clear arc on the inside of the property as each leaf opens. A sliding gate needs a straight runback alongside the fence or wall where the leaf can travel and park. Both need room for the gate to sit when fully open without blocking a path, a parking bay or a fire route.

On the sketch, shade the area you believe is free for gate movement and label anything that cannot move, such as a boundary wall, a utility pillar or a fixed planter. If the free area looks tight, say so rather than guessing. Surveyors routinely find that a swing gate is proposed where the arc would clip a parked car, or that a sliding gate has a runback that crosses a walkway. Documenting this early lets the survey compare options honestly instead of discovering the conflict after a motor has been recommended.

Include a note on how the gate should behave during a power interruption and who would need manual access. You do not need to solve this; you need to record that it matters. The same discipline applies to pedestrian entry: if people walk in beside the vehicle gate, mark that path and whether it needs its own controlled door. Where a separate door is planned, the door condition, user roles and backup method should be reviewed before installation, which is the same principle Eyonic applies when assessing smart lock door access for business premises.

Record Grade, Drainage and Surface Conditions

Driveway slope changes how a gate behaves and how water moves. Walk the entrance with a simple level or take photos from several angles, and note whether the ground rises, falls or stays flat across the opening. Mark any point where the driveway meets a public road, because a crown or kerb can create a lip that affects how vehicles cross the gate line. You are not expected to calculate gradients; you are expected to show the surveyor where the ground changes so the physical check is targeted.

Drainage deserves its own note. After heavy rain in Sarawak, water often pools at gate lines, along runback channels or against boundary walls. Photograph any standing water, staining, moss or eroded gravel and label where it appears. A gate that sits in a low point may need the surveyor to consider how the structure and its moving parts are kept clear of water and debris. Record whether there is an existing drain, culvert or grating near the entrance and whether it is clear or blocked.

Surface material matters too. Note whether the driveway is concrete, asphalt, interlocking pavers or gravel, and whether the surface is level enough for a vehicle to stop and wait. If the gate will open onto a slope, say so plainly. These notes help the surveyor ask the right questions about foundation and clearance without you attempting an engineering judgement. Keep the language descriptive: where water collects, where the ground rises, what the surface is made of.

Map Power, Control Points and Existing Services

Autogates need power, and the survey is much smoother when you can show where power is available and where control equipment could reasonably sit. On your sketch, mark the nearest electrical point, any outdoor socket, and the route a cable might take. Note whether the area is covered or exposed to rain, and whether there is a suitable place for a control box that is out of reach of casual tampering but accessible for maintenance. Do not open panels or attempt to trace wiring; simply record what is visible and where it is.

List the existing services that cross or run near the entrance: water pipes, drainage, telephone or fibre lines, and any buried cable you know about. If you are unsure, write that down as an open question. Surveyors can allow for services when they know they exist, and unexpected buried lines are a common reason for changes mid-project. Also note any existing security equipment at the entrance, such as a camera, intercom or alarm sensor, and whether it is working.

This is where gate planning connects to the wider site. If the entrance already has cameras, the surveyor may want to understand how the gate area is watched and recorded. Eyonic CCTV plans, supplies and installs monitoring systems with camera placement, recording and remote viewing for homes, offices, shops and industrial sites, and a site survey is part of that process. Documenting existing cameras and their coverage helps the surveyor see the entrance as part of one coordinated setup rather than an isolated gate.

Describe Vehicle and Pedestrian Movements

A gate is a traffic management tool as much as a security device. Write a short movement profile: which vehicles arrive, roughly when, and whether they queue. Note whether large vehicles such as lorries or vans need to enter, because their turning circles affect how far the gate must open and where it can safely sit. Mark the waiting position for visitors and whether a vehicle can stop clear of the public road while the gate opens.

Pedestrian movement is just as important. Record where staff, customers or residents walk, and whether they cross the vehicle path. If a separate pedestrian gate or door is planned, note how people would use it during busy periods and after hours. For premises that combine vehicle and door access, it helps to describe who needs which permission. Eyonic notes that smart locks work best when door condition, user roles and backup method are reviewed before installation, and for business use it usually checks whether a standalone smart lock or access-control panel is more appropriate. That same role-based thinking applies to the gate: who holds a remote, who needs a code, and who should be able to open the gate manually.

Include a simple table of users and access needs. Keep it to roles rather than named individuals where possible, and note any change of use, such as a new shift pattern or a delivery arrangement. This gives the surveyor a realistic picture of daily load and helps avoid a gate that is technically sound but awkward in practice.

Note Safety Context Without Designing the Safety System

Safety around a moving gate is a specialist matter, and the surveyor or installer should assess it. Your job is to document the context that affects safety, not to specify sensors or controls. Note where children, pedestrians or cyclists might pass, whether the gate opens toward a road or footpath, and whether the area is lit at night. Record any blind spots where a driver cannot see someone approaching the gate line.

Photograph the approach from a driver's seat and from the pedestrian side. These images help the surveyor understand sight lines and stopping distances. Note whether the gate would open across a public footway, and whether there is a place for a person to stand clear while the gate moves. If the site has a history of near misses or damage at the entrance, describe what happened in plain terms. Do not attempt to design the safety arrangement yourself; instead, list the observations and let the installer carry out the proper safety testing and handover.

This is also the point to record who will be responsible for routine checks after installation. A short note on who opens and closes the gate, who reports faults, and how the site would manage access during a breakdown is useful. Keep it operational rather than technical. The surveyor can then confirm what testing and handover will cover, and you will have a clear record of the site conditions that shaped the recommendation.

Prepare a Question and Decision Log

Before the survey, write down the decisions you expect to make and the questions you want answered. Typical items include the preferred gate type, the budget range you are working within, the desired opening method, and whether the gate should connect to other site systems. A decision log prevents the survey from drifting and gives you a fair basis for comparing recommendations later.

Be clear about what is fixed and what is open. If the gate must match an existing fence, say so. If the opening method is undecided, list the options you are considering. If you have a budget in mind, state the range and ask what it covers, including site preparation, controls, testing and handover.

Finally, add a short section for follow-up. After the survey, record what was measured, what was recommended, and what still needs confirmation. This turns the visit into a documented decision rather than a conversation you have to remember. If the gate is part of a broader upgrade, note how it relates to camera placement and recording, since those choices are easier to coordinate when the entrance plan is settled first. A tidy pack, a measured sketch and a clear question list are modest efforts that make the survey more accurate and the eventual installation easier to manage.
