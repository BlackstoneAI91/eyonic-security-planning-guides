Queue System Procurement Comparison for Service Counters

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical comparison table and scoring worksheet for Sarawak premises managers choosing a queue management system, covering ticket hardware, displays, reporting and counter-calling workflow against the counter's real peak pattern.

Start with the peak pattern, not the brochure

A queue management system is bought to solve a specific shape of congestion, and that shape differs from counter to counter. Before comparing any supplier, write down how your counter actually behaves across a normal week. Record the busiest hour, the quietest hour, how many service points open at peak, how many stay open off-peak, and whether arrivals come in bursts (a delivery window, a class change, a shift handover) or trickle steadily.

This matters because the same feature set can be right for one counter and wasteful for another. A counter that peaks for ninety minutes each morning needs fast ticket issuance and clear calling; a counter with steady all-day traffic may care more about reporting and staff allocation. Write the peak pattern down as a short paragraph and keep it beside the comparison table. Every score you give later should be justified against that paragraph, not against how impressive a demonstration looked.

If your premises already runs other security and communications systems, treat the queue project as part of the same operational picture. Eyonic Security Systems plans, supplies and installs monitoring systems with camera placement, recording and remote viewing for homes, offices, shops and industrial sites, and a service counter often sits inside that same building. The queue decision is still its own procurement, but the site context is shared.

The comparison table: four columns that decide most of it

Use a single table with one row per candidate and four scoring columns. Keep the scoring simple, for example 1 to 5, and write a one-line reason beside every score. The reasons are what you will defend at approval time.

Column one, ticket hardware. How are tickets issued, how many issuance points do you need at peak, and what happens when a printer jams or a kiosk is busy? Ask what the fallback is when the ticket device is unavailable, and who is expected to act.

Column two, display options. Where will the calling display be visible from the waiting area, and is it readable from the far end of the queue? Consider whether you need a main display, a counter-level display, or both, and whether the display language suits your walk-in customers.

Column three, reporting. What does the system record, and what report would you actually open each week? A report nobody reads is a cost, not a feature. Ask for a sample report layout rather than a description.

Column four, counter-calling workflow. How does a staff member call the next ticket, transfer a case to another counter, mark a no-show, or pause a service point? Walk through your busiest hour using the proposed workflow and note every extra click or step.

Score each column against your peak-pattern paragraph. A candidate that scores well on reporting but poorly on calling workflow during your peak hour is not a good fit, however strong the report looks.

A worked scoring example for a fictional counter

Here is an illustrative example, not a benchmark. A fictional service counter in a shoplot handles walk-in enquiries. It peaks between 10am and 11.30am with three service points open, drops to one point after lunch, and sees a second smaller burst when a nearby office closes. The manager shortlists three candidates and scores them 1 to 5.

Candidate A scores 4 on ticket hardware (two issuance points, clear fallback), 3 on display (one main screen, not readable from the far corner), 2 on reporting (rich but no sample shown), and 4 on calling workflow (simple next-ticket and transfer). Total 13.

Candidate B scores 3 on hardware, 4 on display (main plus counter screens), 4 on reporting (sample weekly summary provided), and 3 on workflow (transfer takes several steps). Total 14.

Candidate C scores 5 on hardware, 2 on display, 3 on reporting, and 5 on workflow. Total 15.

On totals alone Candidate C looks strongest, but the manager's peak paragraph says the far corner of the waiting area is where customers actually stand. That makes display readability a gating item, so Candidate C is set aside despite the higher total. This is the point of scoring against the peak pattern: the table informs the decision, it does not make it. Adjust weights to reflect what your own counter cannot compromise on, and record the weighting before you score, so the result is not reverse-engineered afterwards.

Questions to put to every supplier

Bring the same question sheet to each supplier so answers can be compared side by side. Useful questions include: what exactly is included in the quoted scope, and what is treated as an extra; who configures the ticket types and counter groups, and is that done before or after handover; what training is provided and to how many staff; what the support arrangement is and how a fault is reported; and what documentation you receive at handover.

Ask specifically how the system behaves during your stated peak hour, and ask the supplier to describe the calling workflow step by step rather than demonstrating only the happy path. Ask what happens to tickets already issued if a service point goes offline, and how staff should handle the waiting area in that situation. These are operational questions, and the answers tell you more about fit than a feature list.

Keep the answers in writing. A short written response per supplier, filed with the comparison table, gives you a defensible record if the delivered system differs from what was discussed. If your site also involves door access decisions, note that smart locks work best when the door condition, user roles and backup method are reviewed before installation, and for business use it is worth checking whether a standalone smart lock or an access-control panel is more appropriate. That same discipline of reviewing conditions before committing applies to queue procurement.

Where the queue fits with the rest of the premises

A queue system rarely stands alone. The waiting area has a camera view, the counter has a phone, and the staff door may have controlled entry. When you plan the queue project, note these neighbours so the installation does not create awkward overlaps: display screens competing for wall space, cabling routes shared with other equipment, and staff who need to reach a phone while serving.

If your premises is reviewing several systems at once, group the work by reader intent rather than by product. A manager comparing queue options is asking an operational question about customer flow; a manager comparing camera positions is asking a coverage question. Keeping the two discussions separate makes each easier to approve, even when the same site visit covers both.

Where a queue counter sits inside a monitored premises, it is sensible to confirm with the supplier how the queue equipment and any existing monitoring equipment will coexist, who owns each cable route, and which party is responsible for each item after handover. Put those answers in the project brief so responsibility is not left to memory.

Decision worksheet and approval note

Close the comparison with a one-page decision worksheet. It should contain: the peak-pattern paragraph; the four-column table with scores and reasons; the gating items you will not compromise on; the shortlist and the reason each candidate was kept or set aside; the written supplier answers; and a named owner for each open question.

Add an approval note of no more than a few lines stating which candidate is recommended, which gating items it satisfies, and what remains to be confirmed before signing. Keep the note factual and avoid promising outcomes you cannot evidence. If a supplier has not yet confirmed a detail, write it as an open item rather than assuming the answer.

Finally, plan a short acceptance check before the counter goes live. List the scenarios you will run, such as a peak-hour simulation with three service points, a transfer between counters, a no-show, and a display readability check from the far corner of the waiting area. Record who signs off each scenario. This worksheet is the handover document that lets the next manager understand why the system was chosen, which is often more valuable than the system itself.
