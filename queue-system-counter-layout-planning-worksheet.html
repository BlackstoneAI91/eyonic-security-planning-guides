Queue System Counter Layout Planning Worksheet: Sketch Before You Quote

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical worksheet for Sarawak premises managers to map the ticket dispenser, counters and waiting area on a floor sketch before requesting a queue management system quote.

Why a Floor Sketch Comes Before a Quote

A queue management system quote is only as good as the layout it is priced against. When a supplier is asked to quote without a plan, they must assume where the ticket dispenser goes, how many counters need a display, and how much cabling is involved. Those assumptions may not match your premises, and the quote becomes a starting point for negotiation rather than a fixed price. A simple floor sketch changes that. It gives every supplier the same picture, so their quotes can be compared line by line. This worksheet is for premises managers in Sarawak who are planning an ordinary security-system project and want to add a queue system without turning the exercise into a redesign of the whole front office. The goal is not a professional architectural drawing. It is a one-page sketch with labelled positions and a short list of constraints that a supplier can price from. Before you start, gather a tape measure, a notebook, and a copy of your floor plan if one exists. If you already work with a security supplier on CCTV security systems or an access control system, ask whether they also handle queue systems or can recommend a specialist. The sketch you produce will be useful either way.

Step 1: Mark the Fixed Points on Your Sketch

Start by drawing the outline of the service hall or front office. Mark the entrance and exit doors, the counter positions, any pillars or walls, and the location of power outlets and network points. These are the fixed points that will constrain where equipment can go. If your premises already has a CCTV installation, note where cameras are mounted, because a new ticket dispenser or counter display should not block a camera view. Similarly, if you have an access control system at the entrance, the ticket dispenser should sit after the entry point so that visitors take a ticket only once they are inside. For each counter, write down whether it is a full-service counter, a cashier, or an enquiry desk. This matters because a queue system may need different call buttons or display messages for each type. Keep the sketch simple: a rectangle for the room, lines for counters, and circles for fixed points. Label everything with a short name. The aim is to create a shared reference that you can photograph and attach to your quote request.

Step 2: Place the Ticket Dispenser and Waiting Area

The ticket dispenser is the first physical interaction a visitor has with the queue management system. Its position affects crowd flow, accessibility, and whether people can see the counter displays from where they wait. On your sketch, mark a candidate spot for the dispenser just inside the entrance, clear of the door swing and away from direct sunlight if possible. Check that there is a power outlet nearby or note that one will be needed. Next, sketch the waiting area. Draw the seats or standing zone and use an arrow to show the path a visitor takes from the dispenser to the waiting area and then to the counter when called. Look for bottlenecks: a dispenser placed too close to the counter may cause people to crowd the counter before their number is called. A dispenser placed too far away may be missed entirely. If your premises has a keyphone system at reception, consider whether the dispenser should be near the reception desk so staff can assist visitors who are unsure. Mark any accessibility considerations, such as a clear route for wheelchair users or a lower dispenser height if needed. These details help the supplier propose a suitable model and mounting method.

Step 3: Map Counter Displays and Call Buttons

Each counter that serves queued visitors will likely need a call button and a way to see the next ticket number. Counter displays may be mounted above the counter, on the counter itself, or on a shared screen. On your sketch, mark where each display would go and note whether the counter has a clear line of sight to the waiting area. If a counter is behind a glass partition or around a corner, a display may need to be positioned differently. Also note the distance from each counter to the nearest power and network point, as this affects cabling. For counters that handle sensitive transactions, consider whether the display should be visible only to staff or to visitors as well. Write a short note next to each counter describing its function and whether it needs a display, a call button, or both. This list becomes the basis for the bill of quantities in the quote. If you are also planning a burglar alarm system or smart lock door access, keep those separate from the queue layout for now; mixing systems in one sketch can confuse the supplier about what is being priced.

Step 4: Note Constraints and Existing Systems

A layout sketch is more useful when it includes the constraints that a supplier cannot see from a simple floor plan. Walk the space and note anything that could affect installation: ceiling height and material, wall construction, whether cabling can be run above a false ceiling, and whether the waiting area has enough space for the expected number of visitors. If your premises already has CCTV security systems, note the camera positions and whether any new equipment would obstruct a view. If you have a keyphone system at the counter, note where the handsets are so that a queue call button does not conflict with phone use. For premises with an existing access control system, note how visitors enter and whether the queue system should integrate with it, for example by issuing a ticket only after a visitor has signed in. These notes help the supplier decide whether the queue system can be standalone or needs to connect to other systems. They also give you a record of what was agreed before installation. Keep the notes factual and avoid assuming that any particular integration is possible; ask the supplier to confirm.

Step 5: Build a One-Page Quote Request

Once the sketch is complete, turn it into a one-page quote request. Attach the sketch and add a short table listing each item: ticket dispenser (quantity and preferred location), counter displays (one per counter, with counter names), call buttons, waiting area signage, and any cabling or power points that need to be added. Include the constraints you noted, such as ceiling type or the need to avoid obstructing cameras. State that the quote should be based on the attached layout and that any assumptions should be listed separately. This makes it easier to compare quotes from different suppliers. If you are working with a supplier who also provides CCTV site survey services, ask whether they can visit to verify the sketch before quoting. A site visit can catch issues that a sketch misses, such as signal interference or power availability. The goal is a fixed plan that both you and the supplier sign off on before any equipment is ordered. This reduces the chance of variations later.

Step 6: Review the Layout Against Practical Use

Before sending the sketch out, do a quick walk-through as if you were a visitor. Start at the entrance, take a ticket, sit in the waiting area, and listen for your number. Check whether you can see the counter display from where you sit. Then stand at each counter and check whether you can see the waiting area and reach the call button comfortably. Note any issues on the sketch. This simple test often reveals problems that a drawing alone does not, such as a display that is too far away or a dispenser that is awkward to reach. If your premises has staff who will operate the system, ask them to review the sketch and point out anything that would slow them down. Their feedback can be added as notes. Remember that the queue management system is a tool for managing visitor flow, and the layout should support that flow rather than fight it. A few minutes of walk-through can save hours of adjustment after installation.
