Comparing Alarm Service Evidence Before and After a Scheduled Visit

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical guide for Sarawak premises managers on comparing the evidence submitted before and after a scheduled alarm service visit, covering visit approval, completion records and unresolved support questions.

Start with a visit approval record, not a comparison table

Before comparing anything, write down what the scheduled visit was approved to cover. A visit approval record is a short document, agreed by the premises manager and the provider, that names the site, the date window, the person who authorised access, and the scope in plain words. For a burglar alarm system, that scope might read: review the existing arrangement at the ground-floor office and report findings in writing. Keep it descriptive. Do not include control-panel details, wiring notes or any instruction that could weaken the installation.

The approval record does two jobs. First, it gives the after-visit evidence something to be measured against, so a completion note that drifts beyond the agreed scope is visible rather than invisible. Second, it separates the person who authorises the visit from the person who receives the report, which matters when a tenant cannot attend.

A fictional example: a shop in a Sarawak town centre approves a visit to review its alarm arrangement. The approval names the landlord's representative as the authorising party and the shop supervisor as the report recipient. When the completion record arrives, the supervisor can check it against that one page rather than against memory.

Eyonic Security Systems plans, supplies and installs security systems for homes, offices, shops and industrial sites in Sarawak, so a provider-side scope note is a normal part of the paperwork. Treat the approval record as your own document, not the provider's.

What belongs in the pre-visit evidence pack

The pre-visit pack is everything you send or hand over before the scheduled date. Keep it small and dated. A workable pack has four parts: the approval record described above, a one-page site description in plain language, a list of open questions, and a named contact for the day.

The site description should describe the premises, not the outcome you hope for. A fictional example: a two-storey office with a front counter, a rear service door and a small store room, occupied on weekdays. That is enough for a provider to prepare. It is not a place for detailed layouts or anything that would help someone defeat the arrangement.

The open-questions list is the part most often skipped. Write each question as a sentence a non-specialist can read back later, such as: which parts of the premises are covered by the current arrangement, and what written record will follow the visit? Number the questions so the completion record can answer them by number.

Finally, note who will be present and who can authorise a change of plan. If the answer is nobody on site, say so in the pack rather than discovering it on the day.

Reading the completion record against the approval

After the visit, the completion record is the provider's written account of what was done and what remains open. Compare it to the approval record line by line, in this order: scope, findings, open items, and next step.

Scope: does the record describe the same site and the same purpose that was approved? A record that quietly widens the job is a prompt for a conversation, not a reason to sign.

Findings: are they written in language the report recipient can use? A finding such as the existing arrangement covers the front counter but the rear service door was not part of the review is useful. A finding that only names equipment is harder to act on.

Open items: does the record answer your numbered questions, or say plainly that a question needs a separate discussion? An unanswered question is a legitimate entry, provided it is visible.

Next step: who does what, and by when? If the record is silent, that is the gap to raise.

Keep the comparison to one page. A short table with four columns, approved, reported, difference, action, is usually enough for a premises manager to brief an owner.

Separating unresolved support questions from completed work

The most common confusion in after-visit paperwork is treating an open question as a complaint, or treating a completed visit as a closed matter. Keep them apart deliberately.

A completed item is something the approval record asked for and the completion record describes. An unresolved support question is anything else: a question the provider could not answer on the day, a decision that needs the owner, or a follow-up the premises manager must schedule. Give each unresolved question an owner and a review date, even if the owner is simply the person who will ask again.

A fictional example: a small industrial site receives a completion record that answers three of five numbered questions. The two remaining questions are copied into a short support log with the date they were raised and the role responsible for chasing them. Nothing about the arrangement itself is written into the log.

This separation matters because it lets you close the visit without pretending everything is settled. It also gives the next scheduled visit a starting point.

A comparison worksheet you can reuse

A reusable worksheet saves argument later. Use one page per visit and keep the fields identical each time so two visits can be compared side by side.

Suggested fields, offered as an author heuristic rather than a standard: site reference; approval date; approved scope in one sentence; authorising role; report recipient; date of visit; completion record received yes or no; numbered questions answered; numbered questions open; owner of each open question; review date.

Score nothing. A worksheet that produces a number invites false precision. The useful output is a short list of differences and a short list of open questions, both readable by someone who was not present.

When several quotations or visits are in play, keep the worksheet format the same across providers so the comparison is about content, not layout. If a provider's completion record uses different headings, map it onto your fields rather than rewriting theirs.

Store the worksheet with the approval record and the completion record in one place. Future property managers inherit the file, not the conversation, so the file has to stand alone.

Where related security paperwork overlaps

Alarm service paperwork rarely sits alone. Premises managers in Sarawak often hold several security documents at once, and the same discipline applies to each.

If the site also has cameras, the visit approval can note that camera placement and recording arrangements are outside the alarm scope, so the completion record does not appear to cover them. Where remote viewing is part of the wider arrangement, keep any account or access details out of the service file entirely and record only that a separate owner holds them.

Where door access is involved, the same principle applies. Smart locks work best when the door condition, user roles and backup method are reviewed before installation, and for business use Eyonic usually checks whether a standalone smart lock or access-control panel is more appropriate. That review is a separate conversation from an alarm service visit, and the paperwork should say so.

Handing the file to the next person

The final test of any service file is whether a colleague can pick it up cold. Write a one-paragraph cover note at the front: what the visit was for, what was approved, what the completion record said, and which questions remain open with their owners.

Keep the cover note free of technical detail. It should not describe the arrangement, only the paperwork. A fictional example: visit approved to review the alarm arrangement at the rear service door; completion record received and answers three of five questions; two questions open, owned by the premises manager, review in one month.

Name roles, not people, wherever the file may outlive a staffing change. Where a name is unavoidable, note the role beside it so a successor knows who to ask.

Finally, set a review date for the open questions and put it somewhere the next person will see it. A file that ends with a date is a file that gets picked up again. A file that ends with a signature is a file that gets archived and forgotten.
