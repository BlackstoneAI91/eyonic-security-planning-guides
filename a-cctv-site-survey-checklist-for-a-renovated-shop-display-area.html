A CCTV Site Survey Checklist for a Renovated Shop Display Area

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical walkthrough checklist for Sarawak premises managers surveying a newly renovated shop display area, covering camera placement, changed lighting, privacy masks for neighbouring shopfronts, a written recording test and acceptance photos.

Why a renovated display area needs its own survey

A renovation rarely leaves a shop looking the way the old survey assumed. Walls move, shelving changes height, a new backlit display throws light across an aisle, and a doorway that was once a stockroom entrance may now be the main customer route. If the existing camera plan was drawn before the fit-out, treat it as a starting point rather than a finished design. The purpose of this checklist is to help a premises manager in Sarawak walk the finished display area with the installer and agree, in writing, what the cameras should see, what they should not see, and how the result will be tested before sign-off. It is written for ordinary retail and office display spaces, not for high-security or regulated environments. Keep the tone practical: the survey is a conversation recorded on paper, and the checklist is the record of that conversation. Before the walk: assemble the paperwork

A survey goes faster when the manager arrives with the renovation drawings, the old camera list if one exists, and a simple floor sketch marked with entrances, counters, display zones and any neighbouring shopfront that shares a wall or a glass line. Note where power and network points were added during the fit-out, because a camera position that looked sensible on the plan may now sit far from a usable point. Bring a clipboard with the checklist sections below and leave space for notes and photo references. Decide in advance who can approve a change to the plan on the day, and who needs to be consulted afterwards. For a shop, that is often the owner or the operations lead; for an office display area, it may be the facilities contact. Write those names on the sheet so the survey has a clear owner. This is also the moment to confirm the scope in plain language: which areas are in scope, which are out, and whether the recording is intended for daily operations, after-hours review, or both. A short written scope prevents the common drift where a survey quietly expands into corridors and back rooms nobody agreed to cover.

Camera placement: work from the customer path outward

Walk the display area the way a customer would, then the way a staff member would, and mark the points where a camera would actually help. Typical candidates are the main entrance, the till or service counter, the highest-value display run, and the transition between the public floor and any staff-only door. For each candidate, note the height, the direction of view, and what sits directly behind the camera, since a bright window or a reflective display panel can wash out a view. Record what the camera is meant to answer: who entered, what happened at the counter, whether a display was disturbed. A camera that cannot answer a specific question is usually better moved or dropped. Where the display area meets a neighbouring shopfront, mark the boundary on the sketch and note any view that would look into the neighbour's space. This is where camera placement and courtesy meet: the installer can adjust angle or apply a privacy mask so the neighbouring shopfront is excluded from the recorded view. Agree those masks during the survey, not after the first complaint. Changed lighting after the fit-out

Renovations change light. New spotlights over a display, a brighter shopfront sign, or a glass partition that catches afternoon sun can all shift what a camera records between morning and evening. The survey should include at least two walkthroughs at different times of day if the shop trades into the evening, and a note on whether the display lighting is on a timer or a manual switch. For each camera position, record the light source in front of the lens and any source behind it, and describe the likely effect in plain words: backlit entrance, glare from a display panel, a dark aisle after closing. Ask the installer how the proposed camera handles low light and whether the recording test will include the evening condition. Do not accept a verbal assurance alone; the written recording test in a later section is where lighting gets proven. If a position cannot be made reliable without moving a light fitting, record that as an open item for the fit-out contractor rather than assuming the camera will compensate.

Privacy masks for neighbouring shopfronts and shared areas

A display area in a row of shops often shares a glass line, a corridor or a rear service lane with neighbours. The survey should identify every view that could capture a neighbouring shopfront, a shared corridor used by other tenants, or a staff rest area, and agree a privacy mask or an adjusted angle for each. Write the decision down as a line item: which camera, which part of the frame, and why. Masks are a design choice, not a substitute for a sensible angle, so prefer moving the camera where that solves the problem cleanly. Where the display area sits inside a larger building, note who else needs to be told about the change, such as a landlord or a building manager. Keep the discussion at the level of layout and courtesy; the installer handles the configuration. If the shop also controls a staff door, the survey is a good moment to note whether a smart lock door access approach is being considered, since door condition, user roles and a backup method should be reviewed before installation rather than after. Record it as a separate item so the camera survey stays focused.

Recording, NVR setup and remote viewing questions

The survey should settle what happens to the footage, not just where the cameras point. Ask and record: how many days of recording are expected, where the recorder will sit, whether it has a locked or restricted location, and who will be allowed to review footage. For NVR setup, note the proposed channel count against the agreed camera list so there is room for the positions you actually approved. For remote viewing, agree who needs access, on what device, and how access will be removed when a staff member leaves. These are operational questions the manager owns; the installer configures the system to match. A useful habit is to write the answers as short sentences a new manager could follow, because the person who signs off the survey may not be the person who reviews footage next year. If the shop has a service counter, note whether counter activity and the entrance need to be reviewed together, since that affects how the recorder is organised. Keep the sheet free of technical configuration detail; the goal is a clear operational record that the installer can work from.

The written recording test and acceptance photos

Before sign-off, run a recording test that matches the questions written during the survey. As an example heuristic, plan a short session at opening, at the busiest display period and after closing, and for each agreed camera confirm that the intended subject is visible and the agreed privacy mask holds. Record the result line by line as pass, adjust or open, with a note of what changed. Take acceptance photos of each camera position, the recorder location and the masked view, and file them with the survey sheet so a future manager can see what was agreed. The photos are evidence of the handover, not a claim about performance. Where the brand is involved, Eyonic's project work for a public office in Kuching focused on arrival flow, staff areas, visitor movement and dependable daily operation, which is a useful reminder that the test should follow the way people actually move through the space. A similar office fit-out for an engineering consultancy in Kuching emphasised staff movement, visitor handling and tidy cabling, so add a cabling and tidiness check to the acceptance walk. Close the sheet with the date, the names of those present and the open items with an owner.

A one-page sign-off worksheet

Turn the survey into a single page the manager can keep. Suggested rows: area and camera reference; intended question; agreed position and angle; privacy mask or exclusion; lighting note; recording days; who reviews footage; test result; photo reference; open item and owner. Add a short scope line at the top and a signature block at the bottom. As an author heuristic, aim to complete the sheet in one walkthrough plus one recording test, and treat any row left blank as an open item rather than a pass. When the display area is ready for installation, the sheet becomes the brief for the installer and the record for the manager. If the shop is also reviewing door access or a broader security setup, keep those decisions on separate sheets so each can be tested on its own terms.
