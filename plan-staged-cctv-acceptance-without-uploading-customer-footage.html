Plan Staged CCTV Acceptance Without Uploading Customer Footage

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical guide for Sarawak premises managers on accepting a new CCTV system in stages, using staged scenes and consented test clips instead of real customer footage.

Why acceptance needs a plan before the first camera goes up

Acceptance is the moment a premises manager confirms that what was supplied matches what was agreed. It is easy to treat that as a single walk-through on the last day, but a walk-through only shows what happens to be in front of the lens at that hour. A staged approach breaks acceptance into small, repeatable checks that can be run while the work is still fresh and while the installer is still on site.

The privacy question sits alongside the technical one. A camera pointed at a shop entrance, a till or a loading bay will record real people doing ordinary things. You do not need those recordings to prove that a camera works. You can prove focus, coverage, recording and playback with a colleague standing in a marked spot, a printed target board, or a short clip recorded with the consent of everyone in frame. That keeps the evidence useful and keeps customer footage out of the acceptance file.

Set the acceptance stages before the CCTV installation begins

Decide the stages in the brief, not on the final day. A workable sequence for an ordinary premises is: survey and drawing sign-off, first-fix and cabling, camera placement confirmation, recorder and viewing checks, then a probation period. Each stage gets a named owner on your side and a short written outcome.

For the survey stage, the useful output is a marked floor plan showing where each camera is intended to look and what it is meant to see. Ask the installer to note any location that changed from the original discussion and why. This is also the point to agree which scenes you will use for testing, so nobody has to improvise a test in front of real visitors later.

For the placement stage, walk the plan with the installer and confirm each view against the intended purpose: an entrance face, a counter, a doorway, a yard gate. Record the agreed position in one line per camera. If a position cannot be achieved, write the alternative down rather than accepting a verbal explanation.

Keep the stage list short enough to finish. Four or five stages with clear owners will be used; a twelve-stage matrix usually will not.

Build a probation checklist around staged scenes

A probation checklist is a list of scenes you can reproduce on demand, each with a pass condition written in plain language. The point is that anyone on your team can run the check again in three months without calling the installer.

Example scenes for a small premises, written as illustrations rather than fixed standards:

Scene A: a colleague walks through the main entrance at a normal pace. Pass if the face and upper body are recognisable in the live view and in playback.

Scene B: a printed A4 target board is held at the counter for ten seconds. Pass if the board is legible in playback at the agreed review speed.

Scene C: a colleague opens and closes the rear service door. Pass if the door and the person are both visible and the time stamp matches the actual clock.

Scene D: a short consented clip is recorded, played back, and then deleted from the test folder by the person who recorded it.

Write the pass condition as something you can see, not something you feel. "Recognisable" and "legible" are testable; "good quality" is not. Keep the checklist to one page and store it with the handover documents.

Use consented test clips and keep the file clean

The simplest way to avoid uploading customer footage is to decide, in advance, that the acceptance file contains no customer footage at all. Test material should be recorded deliberately, with the knowledge of everyone in frame, and kept only as long as it is needed to confirm the check.

A short procedure that works for most small premises:

First, agree with the installer which test clips will be recorded and where they will be stored. Second, record each clip with a colleague who has agreed to appear, and note the date, the camera and the scene on a one-line log. Third, play the clip back on the recorder and, if remote viewing is part of the scope, on the device you will actually use. Fourth, delete the test clip once the check is signed off, and note the deletion on the log.

If a real incident happens during the probation period, treat it as an incident, not as acceptance evidence. Keep it in the normal recording path and out of the test folder. That separation keeps the acceptance record about the system, not about the people it happened to record.

Check recording, playback and remote viewing as separate items

Recording and viewing fail in different ways, so test them separately. A live picture on a monitor proves the camera and the cable; it does not prove that the recorder is storing anything, that the time is right, or that a manager can retrieve a clip next week.

For recording, ask for a demonstration of the schedule that was agreed and confirm that the stored footage covers the period you expect. For playback, search for a known test clip by time and camera and play it back. For remote viewing, open the view on the device and network you will actually use, at the hour you will actually use it, and confirm that the picture and the time stamp match what the recorder shows.

Write the result of each item as pass, pass with note, or not yet demonstrated. A "not yet demonstrated" line is more useful than a tick you are unsure about.

Run the probation period and close it in writing

A probation period gives you a short window to use the system normally and note anything that does not match the brief. Thirty days is a common example, but the right length depends on your own approval process; treat any number here as an author's suggestion, not a rule.

During probation, keep a simple log with three columns: date, what was checked, and outcome. Add a fourth column for the action taken and who owns it. Review the log weekly with the person who holds the installer relationship, so small issues are raised while they are still small.

At the end of the period, hold a short close-out meeting. Confirm that each stage has a written outcome, that the test clips have been deleted, and that the handover documents are in one place. Record who holds the recorder account details and where the written record is kept, without writing the credentials themselves into the acceptance file.

If you are still comparing suppliers at this stage, the same staged logic applies to the quote: ask each supplier to describe how they will evidence each stage, and compare the answers rather than the summary page.

A one-page acceptance worksheet you can adapt

The worksheet below is a template. Fill it in for your own premises and delete any row that does not apply.

Project and premises: one line. Installer and your named contact: one line each. Stage list with owner and planned date: survey and drawing, first fix, camera placement confirmation, recorder and viewing checks, probation close-out.

Scene table: scene name, camera, pass condition, tested by, date, result. Keep to four or five scenes.

Test clip log: clip reference, camera, date recorded, consented participant, played back yes or no, deleted yes or no.

Recording and viewing: schedule confirmed, playback search confirmed, remote viewing confirmed on the agreed device, time stamp checked.

Close-out: documents received, account holder recorded, open actions with owners, sign-off date.

A worksheet like this does not replace the installer's own commissioning record; it sits beside it and gives you a version written in your own words. For premises in Sarawak planning an ordinary security project, the value is that the acceptance conversation starts early, stays specific, and never depends on keeping a real customer in frame.
