Visitor Access Rules Worksheet for Shared Premises

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical worksheet for Sarawak premises managers to assign escort rules, badge colours and access zones to each visitor type, then test the rules against a delivery and a contractor visit.

Why a visitor access rules worksheet matters in shared premises

Shared premises — a multi-tenant office block, a shoplot with a common lobby, or an industrial park with several units — bring together people who do not share a single employer or a single set of habits. Visitors arrive for different reasons: a courier with a parcel, a technician servicing equipment, a client attending a meeting, or a family member dropping off keys. Without written rules, each person at the door makes a different judgement, and the result is inconsistent access that is hard to audit or improve.

A visitor access rules worksheet is a one- or two-page document that assigns every visitor type an escort rule, a badge colour and an access zone. It is not a legal document and it does not replace building regulations or tenancy agreements. It is an operational tool that turns vague expectations into checkable steps. The worksheet also gives you something concrete to discuss with a security-system supplier, because it defines what the access control system needs to support: which doors need readers, which zones need logging, and which visitors should never receive a credential.

This guide is written for premises managers in Sarawak who are planning ordinary security-system projects. It uses fictional examples and plain language. It does not cover regulated security requirements or legal obligations; those should be confirmed with the relevant authorities or your building management.

The worksheet columns: visitor type, escort rule, badge colour, access zone

Start with four columns. The first is visitor type — a short label such as Delivery driver, Contractor, Client meeting, or Staff guest. Keep the list to the types that actually arrive at your premises; a long list that nobody uses will be ignored.

The second column is the escort rule. Choose from three simple options: Escorted at all times, Escorted to destination only, or Unescorted in defined areas. Write the rule as a sentence so there is no ambiguity: “Escorted from lobby to receiving bay and back.” The third column is badge colour. Use a small set — for example, red for full escort, amber for escorted to destination, green for unescorted in defined areas. The colour should be visible at a glance and recorded in a sign-in log.

The fourth column is access zone. Name the physical areas a visitor may enter: lobby, reception, meeting room, loading bay, plant room, or staff-only corridor. For each visitor type, list the zones allowed and, just as importantly, the zones not allowed. A delivery driver may need the loading bay but not the staff pantry; a contractor may need the plant room but not the finance office.

When you later discuss an access control system with a supplier, this column becomes the basis for door groups and time zones. It also helps you decide whether a simple smart lock door access setup is enough for a small office, or whether a panel-based system is more appropriate for multiple doors and visitor types. Eyonic notes that smart locks work best when door condition, user roles and backup method are reviewed before installation, and that for business use they usually check whether a standalone smart lock or an access-control panel is more appropriate. That review starts with the zones you define here.

Assigning rules: a fictional example for a shared shoplot

Imagine a two-storey shoplot in Sarawak with a ground-floor retail area, a first-floor office shared by two tenants, and a rear loading bay. The premises manager drafts the following rows. These are fictional examples for illustration only.

Delivery driver: Escort rule — escorted from lobby to loading bay and back. Badge colour — red. Access zone — lobby and loading bay only. The driver signs in, receives a red badge, and waits for a staff member to accompany them.

Contractor (equipment service): Escort rule — escorted to destination only, then unescorted in the plant room. Badge colour — amber. Access zone — lobby, service corridor, plant room. The contractor signs in, shows a work order, and is escorted to the plant room; the escort returns to check progress at agreed intervals.

Client meeting: Escort rule — unescorted in defined areas after reception check-in. Badge colour — green. Access zone — lobby, meeting room, visitor washroom. The client signs in, receives a green badge, and is asked to remain in the meeting room area.

Staff guest (personal visitor): Escort rule — escorted at all times by the staff member. Badge colour — red. Access zone — lobby and a designated meeting corner only. The staff member signs the guest in and remains with them.

Notice that the badge colour follows the escort rule, not the visitor's importance. A client on a green badge is not more trusted than a contractor on amber; the colour simply tells staff what the rule is without needing to remember each case.

Testing the rules against a hypothetical delivery

A worksheet is only useful if it survives a real sequence of events. Walk through a hypothetical delivery. A courier arrives at 10:15 a.m. with three boxes for a first-floor tenant. The receptionist checks the worksheet: Delivery driver, red badge, lobby and loading bay only. The courier signs in and receives a red badge. The receptionist calls the tenant, who sends a staff member to the lobby. The staff member escorts the courier to the loading bay, accepts the boxes, and escorts the courier back to the lobby. The courier signs out and returns the badge.

Now test the exceptions. What if the tenant's staff member is unavailable for ten minutes? The worksheet should say whether the courier waits in the lobby or whether reception can accept the boxes on the tenant's behalf. What if the courier needs to use the washroom? The access zone column should state whether the visitor washroom is allowed on a red badge. What if the courier arrives after reception hours? The worksheet should name a backup contact and state that the delivery is refused or held at the door.

These details are not bureaucratic. They are the difference between a rule that staff can follow under pressure and a rule that gets quietly abandoned. Write the answers in a short notes column next to each visitor type, and review them after the first month of use.

Testing the rules against a hypothetical contractor visit

Now walk through a contractor visit. A technician arrives to service the air-conditioning in the plant room. The worksheet says Contractor, amber badge, lobby, service corridor and plant room. The technician signs in, shows a work order, and is escorted to the plant room. The escort returns to reception but checks back at agreed intervals — say every thirty minutes, as a local practice rather than a fixed standard.

Test the edge cases. What if the technician needs to leave the plant room to collect a part from a vehicle? The worksheet should say whether the amber badge allows a return through the lobby with a sign-out and sign-in, or whether a new escort is required. What if the technician finishes early and the escort is on a break? Name a backup escort. What if the technician needs to enter a zone not listed, such as the server room? The rule should be that any zone not listed is refused, and a manager must approve an addition in writing.

This is also the point to consider how the access control system supports the rule. If the plant room door has a reader, the technician might receive a time-limited credential instead of a physical key. If the door is a simple smart lock, the premises manager should confirm that user permissions and a backup entry method are planned properly, because smart lock door access works well for controlled convenience under those conditions. The worksheet does not choose the hardware, but it tells the supplier what the hardware must do.

From worksheet to system brief: what to hand to a supplier

Once the worksheet is tested, it becomes a concise brief for any security-system discussion. Group the rows by access zone and note which zones need controlled doors, which need only signage, and which need a log of entry and exit. Add a column for review frequency — daily, weekly or monthly — and a named owner for each visitor type.

If your premises already has CCTV, note where camera placement overlaps with visitor routes. A camera at the lobby entrance and another at the loading bay can support the sign-in process, but the worksheet should state who reviews footage and how long it is kept. If you are planning a new system, a site survey can confirm door types, power points and cable routes before any quote is finalised. Eyonic Security Systems plans, supplies and installs monitoring systems with camera placement, recording and remote viewing for homes, offices, shops and industrial sites, and a clear visitor worksheet helps that planning stay focused on the doors and zones that matter.

Keep the brief to two pages. Attach the worksheet as an appendix. Do not include actual access codes, personal identity numbers or detailed security configurations in a document that circulates widely; refer to them by role instead.

A short review checklist before you adopt the worksheet

Before you print the worksheet and put it at reception, run this checklist. It is a set of author heuristics, not an audited standard.

One: every visitor type that arrived in the last month has a row. Two: every row has an escort rule written as a full sentence. Three: every row has a badge colour from a set of no more than four colours. Four: every row lists allowed zones and at least one not-allowed zone. Five: the delivery test and the contractor test each produced a clear answer without needing a manager's judgement. Six: a backup contact is named for after-hours arrivals. Seven: the worksheet names who reviews it and how often. Eight: the worksheet does not contain actual codes, key locations or personal data.

If any item is missing, fix it before adoption. A worksheet that staff cannot apply at the door is worse than no worksheet, because it creates the appearance of control without the practice. Review the document after the first month, then quarterly, and update it when a new tenant moves in or a new visitor type appears.
