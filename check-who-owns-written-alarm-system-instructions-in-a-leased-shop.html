Who Owns the Written Alarm System Instructions in a Leased Shop?

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A document ownership and availability checklist for Sarawak premises managers leasing a shop, covering landlord, tenant and installer roles for written burglar alarm system instructions, with a suggested review date.

Why the question of ownership comes up at all

In a leased shop, the burglar alarm system often sits in an awkward place. The landlord may have fitted it before the tenancy began, the tenant may have added to it, and an installer may have serviced it under a contract that names neither party clearly. When a new manager arrives, or when the tenancy is renewed, someone asks a simple question: where are the written instructions, and who is allowed to hold them?

That question is worth answering on paper before it becomes urgent. Written instructions usually cover how the system is armed and disarmed by authorised staff, what the notification arrangements are, who to call, and what routine checks are expected. They are not the same as a service contract, an invoice, or a verbal briefing at handover. Treat them as a controlled document with a named owner.

This guide is a checklist, not legal advice. Tenancy terms vary, and a lease clause about fixtures is not the same as a clause about documents. The aim here is practical: agree who holds the instructions, who can request a copy, and when the arrangement is reviewed. Separate the three roles before assigning the document

Most confusion comes from treating landlord, tenant and installer as one party. Split them first, then decide what each one holds.

The landlord typically owns the building fabric and any alarm equipment installed as part of the premises. The tenant typically owns the day-to-day operation: who arms the system, who responds to a notification, and which staff are authorised. The installer holds the technical record of what was fitted and how it was set up.

A useful worksheet has one row per role and four columns: what this role holds, what this role may request, what this role must not change without approval, and who signs off. For example, the tenant may hold the operating instructions for daily use, while the installer holds the fuller technical file. Neither should be assumed to hold the other's copy.

Keep the worksheet short enough to fit on one page. If a role is unclear, write "to confirm" rather than guessing. The point is to expose the gap, not to fill it with an assumption.

Build a document availability checklist

Availability is different from ownership. A document can be owned by the landlord and still be available to the tenant on request. Write the checklist around availability so that a new manager can find what they need without a chain of phone calls.

Suggested checklist items, as an author heuristic rather than a standard:

One: a named holder for the operating instructions, with a job title rather than only a person's name.

Two: a stated location, such as an office file or a labelled folder, recorded in the handover pack.

Three: a request route, so an authorised person knows whom to ask for a copy.

Four: a version or date on the document, so two copies can be compared.

Five: a note of what the instructions do not cover, such as changes to the system layout.

Six: a review date, covered later in this guide.

Score each item as present, partial or missing. A partial score is useful because it shows where a follow-up question is needed. Do not treat the score as an audit result; it is a planning aid for the premises manager.

What belongs in the written instructions and what does not

A written instruction set should be readable by an authorised staff member who was not present at handover. It should describe the ordinary routine: how the system is armed at closing, how it is disarmed at opening, what a notification means, and which role is contacted next.

It should not be a technical manual for changing the system. Reset steps, zone changes and configuration details belong with the installer, not in a shop office folder. Keeping that boundary clear protects both parties: staff are not asked to make changes they are not trained for, and the installer retains the record of how the system was set up.

Where the shop also uses related systems, such as a smart lock door access arrangement at a staff entrance, the instructions should say who holds that separate document rather than merging everything into one file. Agree the handover moment and the review date

Documents drift when nobody owns the handover moment. Pick a trigger and write it down: a new tenancy, a change of premises manager, a service visit, or a renewal date. At that trigger, the named holder confirms that the instructions are still present, still dated, and still match the system in the shop.

A suggested review rhythm, offered as an example rather than a rule, is once at the start of the tenancy and once a year thereafter, plus any time the responsible manager changes. Put the next review date in the same worksheet as the roles, so it is visible rather than buried.

A short escalation path when the document is missing

Sometimes the checklist comes back with a missing item. Handle it in a fixed order so the request does not stall.

First, ask the named holder whether a copy exists in another location. Second, ask the installer whether a copy was issued at handover. Third, ask the landlord whether the document sits with the building file. Fourth, record the outcome and the date, even if the outcome is "not located".

Keep the escalation in writing and keep it factual. Avoid naming individuals in a shared log where a job title will do. If the missing document concerns the burglar alarm system, note which parts of the routine are affected, such as closing procedure or notification contacts, so the risk is visible to the person who approves the next step.

This is also the moment to check whether the shop's other systems have the same gap. A keyphone system or an access control system may have its own instructions held by a different party. The escalation path is the same; only the named holder changes.

Turn the checklist into a one-page record

Finish by compressing the work into a single record that a new manager could pick up. It should show the three roles, the documents each holds, the availability score, the next review date, and the escalation outcome if anything was missing.

Keep it plain. A table with short entries is easier to maintain than a long narrative. Date every version, and store it with the tenancy file rather than in a personal folder.

The wider lesson is that security documents are part of the premises record, not an afterthought. Whether the shop is reviewing a burglar alarm system, planning a CCTV site survey, or checking who holds the instructions for a smart lock door access setup, the same three questions apply: who owns it, who can get a copy, and when will it be checked again. Answer those on one page and the leased shop has a document trail that survives staff changes.
