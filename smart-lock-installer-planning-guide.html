Checking Door Compatibility Before Choosing a Smart Lock

Prepared for Eyonic Security System (Eyonic CCTV)

A practical guide for owners and facilities teams in Sarawak on checking door compatibility before choosing a smart lock, using an example rental unit survey to cover measurements, fit questions, battery alerts, permissions and an authorised fallback.

Why the door decides the smart lock, not the brochure

A smart lock is usually chosen from a product page, a showroom display or a recommendation from a neighbour. The door it will be fitted to is often the last thing anyone examines closely. That order is backwards. The lock has to match the door's thickness, the existing cut-outs, the way the door closes against the frame, and the material the door is made from. A model that suits a solid timber office door may not suit a hollow rental door, a glass door with a narrow stile, or a metal grille door. Starting with the door narrows the shortlist quickly and avoids the awkward situation where a lock is purchased and then cannot be fitted without modification.

This guide is written for owners and facilities teams planning security and ELV systems for small premises in Sarawak, and it uses a worked example of a rental unit survey. It is a planning resource, not an installation manual. Anything involving drilling, wiring, mortising or bypassing an existing lock should be handled by an authorised installer. The aim here is to help you gather the right information before anyone arrives with tools, so the conversation with an installer is short and specific. Eyonic Security Systems plans, supplies and installs security systems for homes, offices, shops and industrial sites in Sarawak, and smart lock door access is one of the areas where a short pre-check saves the most time.

A useful way to think about it: the lock is a small electronic device, but the door is a mechanical system with a history. It may have been rehung, patched, painted over, or fitted with a closer that pulls it slightly off alignment. Those details matter more than the number of unlocking methods listed on the box.

An example door survey for a rental unit

Imagine a small rental unit in a shophouse block. The main entrance is a single timber door with an existing cylindrical knob lock and a separate deadbolt above it. The door opens inward, the frame is painted metal, and there is a gap of a few millimetres between the door edge and the frame. The owner wants keyless entry for tenants but also wants a physical key available for the property manager. This is a realistic starting point, and it already raises several questions that a survey should answer.

A survey sheet for this door would record: door material and construction (solid or hollow), door thickness at the lock position, the backset and centre distance of the existing lock, whether the door is left- or right-handed, the width of the frame face, the condition of the hinges, whether a door closer is fitted, and whether the door currently latches cleanly without lifting or pushing. It would also note the power situation, because some locks rely on batteries while others are wired, and it would note who needs access and how often. Photographs of both faces of the door, the edge, and the frame are worth more than a paragraph of description. A site survey is a normal part of planning, and for a rental unit it is worth doing once with the property manager present so the access list and the fallback method are agreed in the same visit.

For this example door, the survey would likely flag that the existing deadbolt position may conflict with a new lock body, that the painted metal frame needs a clean strike plate fit, and that the tenant turnover means permissions must be easy to reset. None of those are reasons to abandon the idea; they are reasons to choose the right model and plan the fitting properly.

Measurements and fit questions to bring to the installer

You do not need to become a locksmith, but a handful of measurements make the first conversation far more productive. Measure the door thickness at the point where the lock will sit, not at the edge where paint and trim can distort the reading. Measure the backset, which is the distance from the door edge to the centre of the existing lock hole, and the centre distance between the knob and the deadbolt if both exist. Note the diameter of any existing holes. Check whether the door is flush or panelled, because a raised panel can interfere with a keypad or handle. Finally, check the frame: is there enough flat surface for a strike plate, and does the door sit flush when closed?

Fit questions are just as important as numbers. Does the door need to keep its existing key cylinder for the property manager, or can that be replaced? Is the door exposed to rain or direct sun, which affects both the lock body and the keypad? Is there a glass panel close to the lock that would make a thumb-turn reachable from outside? Does the door have a self-closing arm that might stop the bolt from engaging cleanly? These are the questions that separate a smooth installation from a return visit. For business premises, the choice between a standalone smart lock and a fuller access-control panel often turns on how many doors, how many users and how much audit detail is needed, and that is a decision to make with the installer rather than from a product listing.

Write the answers down and keep them with the survey photos. If you are comparing two units in the same building, the measurements may differ slightly, and having both sets on file prevents assumptions.

Battery alerts, power and the maintenance routine

Battery-powered smart locks are convenient because they avoid running cable to the door, but they introduce a maintenance task that has to be owned by someone. Before choosing a lock, ask how the lock reports low battery, whether the alert appears in the app, on the device itself, or both, and how much warning it gives. Ask what happens when the battery is fully flat: does the lock fail open, fail closed, or retain a mechanical key override? For a rental unit, the answer affects both tenant safety and the owner's liability, so it should be written into the handover notes rather than left to memory.

Power planning also covers the wider premises. If the door is part of a small office with CCTV security systems, alarm and door access, it helps to think about where the lock sits in the overall picture. A lock that depends on a hub or a network connection needs that hub to be on a sensible power arrangement, and the person who checks the system should know where it is. Remote CCTV viewing and remote lock management are often discussed together because both depend on a stable connection and a clear owner for alerts. Decide in advance who receives a low-battery or offline notification, and what they are expected to do. A simple monthly check, recorded on a shared calendar, is a reasonable author heuristic for a small premises; it is not a manufacturer requirement, and the actual interval should follow the lock's own guidance.

Keep a spare battery of the correct type on site, and record the date it was last changed. That single habit removes most of the surprise from battery-powered entry.

Permissions, roles and the rental turnover problem

A smart lock is only as good as its user list. Before installation, write down who needs access, what each person should be able to do, and how access will be removed. For the rental example, the list might include the tenant, a co-tenant, the property manager, a cleaner who attends weekly, and a maintenance contractor who attends occasionally. Each of those is a different role with a different duration. The lock should support separate credentials per person so that removing one person does not disturb anyone else, and so that the record shows who entered and when.

This is where planning pays off. If the lock only supports a shared code, every departure means changing the code and telling everyone. If it supports individual user slots with schedules, the cleaner's access can be limited to a day and time window, and the contractor's access can expire automatically. For business use, the same logic applies at a larger scale, and it is worth asking whether a standalone lock or an access-control panel better matches the number of doors and the reporting you need. Smart locks work best when the door condition, user roles and backup method are reviewed before installation, and that review is the moment to decide how permissions will be issued and revoked.

Agree the revocation process in writing. Who can add a user, who can remove one, and what happens at the end of a tenancy or when a staff member leaves? A short checklist kept with the handover documents turns a vague intention into something the next person can follow.

The authorised fallback and the handover

Every smart lock plan needs a fallback that does not depend on the electronics. That might be a mechanical key held by the property manager, a second entry door, or a documented procedure for a locksmith. The important word is authorised: the fallback should be available to named people through a known process, not left as an open bypass. Decide where the key is kept, who can retrieve it, and how its use is recorded. If the lock has a key override, test that it works before the installer leaves, and confirm that using it does not silently disable the electronic functions.

Handover is the point where all the earlier planning either holds together or falls apart. Ask the installer to demonstrate adding a user, removing a user, checking the battery status, and using the fallback. Ask for the admin credentials to be transferred to the owner or facilities lead, not left on a default. Confirm what happens during a power cut or network outage, and how the lock behaves when it reconnects. If the premises also has camera placement and recording, note whether the entrance is covered, because a door event and a camera view together are far more useful than either alone.

Finally, keep the survey sheet, the photos, the user list and the fallback procedure in one folder. When a tenant changes or a staff member leaves, the next person should be able to act without reconstructing the whole decision. If you are unsure whether a particular door is suitable, the sensible next step is to share the measurements and photos with an authorised installer and ask them to confirm the fit before any purchase.
