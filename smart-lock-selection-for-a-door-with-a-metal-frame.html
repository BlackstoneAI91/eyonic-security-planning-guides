Smart Lock Selection for a Door with a Metal Frame: A Compatibility Comparison

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical compatibility guide for Sarawak premises managers choosing a smart lock for a metal-framed door, covering frame type, door thickness, latch backset, user roles and backup entry before any order is placed.

Start With the Door, Not the Lock

A smart lock is usually chosen from a catalogue photo, but the door decides whether that choice is workable. Before comparing brands or features, walk to the door with a tape measure and a notebook and record four things: the frame material and profile, the door leaf thickness, the existing latch or lock cut-out, and how the door closes against the frame. A metal-framed door behaves differently from a timber door because the frame is often a hollow or folded steel section with a narrow face, and the lock body has to sit inside that section rather than in a solid stile. Write down the frame face width, the depth of the section, and whether there is a return flange that would obstruct a lock body or a handle. Photograph the frame edge, the existing lock, and the floor gap. These notes become the fixed requirement you hand to a supplier, and they stop the conversation from drifting into features before fit has been settled. If the door is part of a larger security plan, it helps to treat the lock as one element among several; Eyonic Security Systems plans, supplies and installs CCTV systems for homes, offices, shops and industrial sites in Sarawak, and a door entry decision often sits alongside camera coverage of the same entrance.

Frame Type and Fixing Method: The First Compatibility Check

Metal frames fall into a few broad families, and each raises a different question. A folded steel frame with a narrow face may not have room for a mortised lock body, which pushes the choice toward a surface-mounted or rim-style lock. A hollow section may need a reinforcing plate or a through-bolt arrangement so the lock is fixed to something structural rather than to thin sheet. A frame with an existing cut-out may limit you to a lock that matches that cut-out, or require patching and re-cutting, which is a fabrication job rather than a swap. Ask the supplier three direct questions: what frame face width does this lock need, how is it fixed to a metal frame, and what happens to the existing cut-out. If the answers are vague, the lock is not yet a candidate. This is also the point to decide whether the door needs a standalone lock at all. For business use, Eyonic usually checks whether a standalone smart lock or an access-control panel is more appropriate, because a door that already sits within a managed access system may be better served by a reader and a controlled strike than by a second, separate credential system.

Door Thickness and Latch Backset: Measure Before You Compare

Two measurements decide most of the remaining fit questions. Door thickness determines whether a lock body and its spindle will sit correctly through the leaf; too thin and the lock may not clamp securely, too thick and the spindle or cable route may not reach. Latch backset is the distance from the door edge to the centre of the spindle or latch, and it must match the lock's design or the door must be re-drilled. Record both in millimetres, and record them at the actual door rather than from a drawing, because a metal door may have a folded edge that changes the effective thickness at the lock position. Also note the handing of the door, the direction of swing, and whether the frame has a stop that the latch must clear. A simple table works well: door reference, frame type, leaf thickness, backset, existing cut-out, and notes on obstructions. Fill it in for every door you intend to fit, not just the first one, because a mixed building often has several door types and a lock chosen for one may not suit the next.

Match the Lock Type to the Door and the Traffic

With the measurements in hand, the lock options narrow quickly. Surface-mounted locks suit frames where a mortised body will not fit, and they are easier to remove or service, but they protrude and can interfere with a closing door or a narrow corridor. Mortised locks give a cleaner finish but demand enough frame depth and a cut-out that can be made accurately. Electronic deadbolts and lever sets each impose their own backset and thickness ranges. Beyond fit, match the lock to how the door is used: a staff-only door with light traffic has different needs from a main entrance used by visitors, deliveries and contractors. Write down who needs to enter, at what times, and whether they need a code, a card, a phone credential or a key. That list is the requirement the lock must satisfy, and it is more useful than a feature comparison. Smart lock door access works well for controlled convenience when user permissions and backup entry are planned properly, so the planning step is not optional paperwork; it is what makes the convenience safe to rely on.

User Roles, Permissions and Backup Entry

A lock that opens for everyone is not access control. Before ordering, list each user group and what it should be able to do: enter during business hours only, enter at any time, admit visitors, or administer the lock. Decide who holds the administrator credential and who can add or remove users, because that role is the one that matters when staff change. Then plan the backup method explicitly. Ask what happens when the battery is flat, when the network is down, when a phone is lost, and when the administrator is unavailable. A mechanical key override, an external power point, or a documented manual release are common answers, but the important thing is that the answer is written down and tested before the door is relied on. Keep a register that ties each code or credential to a named holder and a removal date, and review it when someone leaves. This is the same discipline you would apply to an access control system, and it is what separates a managed door from a door with a gadget on it.

A Pre-Order Comparison Worksheet

Use one page per door and fill it in before you request any quote. The worksheet has four blocks. Block one, the door: frame type and face width, leaf thickness, latch backset, handing, existing cut-out, and obstructions. Block two, the users: each group, its permitted times, and whether it needs to admit others. Block three, the lock: fixing method, credential types, administrator role, and battery or power arrangement. Block four, backup and handover: the override method, who holds it, how it is tested, and where the register is kept. Score each candidate lock against the blocks rather than against a brochure, and mark any block that cannot be answered as a gap rather than a pass. As an author's heuristic, a lock that leaves two or more blocks unanswered is not ready to order, and a lock that fits the door but has no tested backup should be treated as incomplete. When the door is part of a wider entrance plan, it is worth coordinating the lock decision with camera placement at the same door so that the coverage and the entry record tell a consistent story.

Where the Lock Sits in the Wider Security Plan

A metal-framed door is rarely the only thing at an entrance. The same doorway may need camera coverage, an alarm zone, and a record of who came through. Decide early whether the lock will stand alone or report into a broader system, because that choice affects the credential type and the administration burden. If the site already has an access control system, adding a separate smart lock creates a second user list to maintain; if there is no such system, a standalone lock may be the simpler starting point. Either way, document the decision and the reason, so the next person to manage the building understands why the door works the way it does. For sites in Sarawak, a supplier who can look at the door, the frame and the surrounding coverage in one visit will usually give a more grounded recommendation than a catalogue comparison, and the site survey is the natural moment to settle the measurements this guide asks for.
