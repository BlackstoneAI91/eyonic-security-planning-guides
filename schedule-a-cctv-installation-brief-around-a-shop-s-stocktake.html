Scheduling a CCTV Installation Brief Around a Shop's Stocktake

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical guide for Sarawak premises managers on timing a CCTV installation brief around a shop stocktake, covering access changes, stock location confirmation, customer notices and a written handoff to the installer.

Why the stocktake week changes the brief

A stocktake week is not a normal trading week. Shelves are counted, stock is moved, back-of-house areas are opened up, and staff who would normally be on the floor are working through lists. If a CCTV installation is scheduled in the same period, the brief has to say so plainly, because the two activities compete for the same floor space, the same staff attention and the same access points.

The practical answer is to treat the stocktake as a fixed constraint in the brief rather than a detail to be sorted out later. Write down the stocktake dates, the hours when counting happens, which areas are closed to customers, and which doors are in use. Then decide, before contacting any supplier, whether the installation work happens before, during or after that window. Each choice has a different communication burden, and the brief should name the choice.

Separate the three communication jobs

Most confusion in a stocktake-week installation comes from one person trying to hold three unrelated conversations. Split them into three named owners in the brief.

The first job is communicating revised access times. Someone must tell staff, the installer and any building management which doors are open on which day, and when the premises will be closed to customers. This owner needs authority to change the schedule, not just pass messages.

The second job is confirming affected stock locations. During a count, stock moves. Pallets sit in aisles, cartons are staged near the back door, and the stockroom layout changes for a few days. The person who owns this job confirms which areas hold stock on each install day, so camera placement decisions are not made against a floor that no longer exists.

The third job is preparing public customer notices. If part of the shop is closed, if a counter is relocated, or if a doorway is temporarily out of use, customers need a plain notice at the entrance. Keep the notice about service and access only. It does not need to describe the security system.

Name all three owners, with a deputy for each, in the brief itself.

Write the access and closure schedule as a table

A paragraph describing the week is easy to misread. A table is not. Build one row per day of the installation window and give it five columns: date, trading hours, doors in use, areas closed to customers, and the named person on site.

Fill it in with the stocktake in mind. If counting runs from early morning, say whether the installer can start before the count or must wait. If the back door is the only route for stock movement that day, record it, because that affects where cabling and equipment can reasonably go. If the shop closes early on one day, note the actual closing time rather than the usual one.

Keep the table to the installation window only. A two-week table is readable; a two-month one is not. Circulate it to the installer and to the three owners above, and ask for written confirmation that the dates are understood. A short reply confirming the dates is enough; you are checking that the same week is in everyone's diary.

This table is also the document you hand over at the start of a CCTV installation, so the crew is working from the same access rules as your staff.

Confirm stock locations before camera placement is fixed

Camera placement is usually decided at the survey, and the survey usually happens on an ordinary trading day. That is a problem if the stocktake changes where high-value or bulky stock sits. The brief should list the locations that matter, in plain language, and note whether each location is permanent or temporary.

For example, a brief might record that the rear stockroom holds fast-moving lines year-round, while a temporary staging area near the loading point exists only during count week. The first is a placement consideration; the second is a scheduling consideration. Mixing them up leads to a camera aimed at a spot that is empty for fifty weeks of the year.

Ask the stock owner to walk the floor with the survey and mark the permanent locations on a simple sketch. Keep the sketch free of detailed measurements and wiring notes; it is a placement aid, not an engineering drawing. The aim is a short list the installer can quote against, with each camera tied to a named view rather than a vague instruction to cover the back.

Decide what customers are told, and by whom

Customer notices during a stocktake-week installation should answer three questions: is the shop open, which entrance to use, and whether any service is affected. Anything beyond that invites questions staff cannot answer mid-count.

Agree the wording with the person who owns customer communication, and set a rule for when notices go up and come down. A notice that stays on the door a week after the work finishes is worse than no notice, because it trains customers to ignore them.

Also decide where staff send customer questions that the notice does not cover. A single named person, reachable at the counter, is usually enough. Record that name in the brief so it does not depend on who happens to be on shift.

Keep the notices factual and short. They describe access and service, not equipment, and they should not promise a completion date unless the schedule table already fixes one. If the date moves, the notice owner updates the notice the same day.

Hand the brief over with a short acceptance line

End the brief with a single page the installer signs or confirms in writing. It should list the agreed dates, the access table, the named owners, the permanent stock locations marked for camera placement, and the notice plan. Add one line for the installer to confirm receipt and one line for you to confirm the brief matches your understanding.

This page is not a contract and does not replace the supplier's own documents. It is a shared reference so that a change of plan is visible to everyone. If the stocktake dates move, the change is recorded against the same page rather than scattered across messages.

A short pre-start checklist

Run this checklist in the week before work begins. It is an author's heuristic, not a researched standard, so adjust it to your premises.

One: the access and closure table is filled for every day of the window, with a named person on site each day. Two: the three owners, and their deputies, are named and have confirmed. Three: permanent stock locations are marked on the sketch and temporary staging areas are noted as scheduling constraints only. Four: customer notice wording is agreed, with an owner and a removal date. Five: the installer has confirmed the dates in writing. Six: the acceptance page is filed where the premises manager can find it.

If any line is blank, the brief is not ready. A short delay before the stocktake is easier to manage than a crew arriving on the busiest counting morning with no agreed door to use.
