Auto Gate Acceptance Walk With a Checklist: Leaf Travel, Obstruction Response and Manual Release

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical acceptance walk for Sarawak premises managers taking handover of an auto gate, with a pass/fail checklist covering leaf travel, obstruction response and manual release, plus a signed sign-off page.

Why the acceptance walk is a separate step from the installation

An auto gate is finished when the installer says it is finished; it is accepted when you have watched it do the things you will rely on every day. Those are two different moments, and the gap between them is where most handover disputes live. The installer's final test usually confirms the gate opens and closes on command. Your acceptance walk confirms it opens and closes safely, predictably, and in a way your staff can operate and release without help.

Treat the walk as a scheduled appointment, not a five-minute courtesy at the end of a long day. Book it in daylight, with the gate area clear of vehicles, and with at least one person who will actually use the gate present. Bring a printed checklist, a pen, and a phone for photographs. The output of the walk is a single signed page: each line marked pass or fail, each fail described in one sentence, and a date by which the installer will return.

That matters for the walk because a gate is rarely a standalone item on a Sarawak compound: it usually shares a driveway with cameras, an intercom or a card reader, and the acceptance walk should note those interfaces even when they are tested on a different day.

Before the walk: what to have on the checklist header

The header of the checklist fixes the facts that are easy to forget three months later. Record the date, the site address as written on the quotation, the gate type (sliding or swing), the number of leaves, the motor model as printed on the unit, and the names of the installer and the person accepting on behalf of the premises. Add the quotation or job reference number so the signed page can be filed against the paperwork.

Then write down the operating modes the gate is meant to support. A shared driveway may need fully open, pedestrian opening if the gate supports it, and a hold-open state for moving vehicles. A shop compound may need the gate to stay open during trading hours and close at night. These are not installation details; they are your requirements, and the walk should test each one rather than assume the default behaviour is what you wanted.

Finally, agree the safety devices in scope before anyone presses a button. Common items include a pair of safety beams across the opening, a flashing lamp, and a force or obstruction setting in the controller. If a device is on the quotation, it belongs on the checklist. If it is not on the quotation, write that down too, so the absence is a recorded decision rather than a surprise later.

Step 1: leaf travel and the fully open and fully closed positions

Start with the simplest test and do it slowly. Operate the gate through a full open cycle and a full close cycle, and watch the leaf from the side rather than from the driver's seat. You are checking three things: that the leaf travels without binding or scraping, that it stops squarely at each end position, and that nothing in the path fouls the leaf at any point in the run.

Mark pass or fail for each of the following on your sheet. The leaf reaches the fully open stop without the motor straining or the controller cutting out. The leaf reaches the fully closed stop and the two leaves meet or the single leaf meets the post without a gap you could put a hand through. The gate does not drift back after stopping. The track or hinge area is clear of debris and the gate runs quietly enough that you would not hear it from the office.

Repeat the cycle at least three times in a row. A gate that works once and hesitates on the third attempt has a problem worth recording now. Note the time each full cycle takes, because a gate that has slowed noticeably compared with the first test is telling you something about alignment or load.

Step 2: obstruction response, tested deliberately

This is the step people skip, and it is the one that matters most on a shared driveway. With the gate moving, place a test object in its path and confirm the gate stops and reverses or stops and holds, according to the behaviour the installer has configured. Use a soft object and stand clear; never use a person, a child or a pet as the test.

Test the safety beams separately from the force setting. Break the beam while the gate is closing and confirm the gate responds. Then test the beam while the gate is opening, if the controller is configured to react in that direction. Record which device triggered which response, because a gate that reverses on the beam but not on contact is only half tested.

Ask the installer to show you the controller setting for obstruction sensitivity and to write the current value on the checklist. You are not expected to adjust it; you are expected to know it exists and to know that it was set deliberately. If the gate is on a slope, or if wind load is a factor on a wide leaf, say so during the walk so the setting can be discussed then rather than after a complaint.

A note on scope: this walk records what you observed and what the installer configured. It is not a substitute for the installer's own commissioning and safety checks, and any adjustment to the controller should be made by the installer, not by staff.

Step 3: manual release, power failure and the person who will use it

The manual release is the part of the system that gets used on the worst day, usually by someone who has never touched it. Test it during the walk, in daylight, with the installer present. Confirm where the release key or lever is kept, who holds it, and how the gate is returned to normal operation afterwards.

On the checklist, record the release method, the location of the key, and the name of the person responsible for it. Then have that person, not the installer, perform the release and the reset while you watch. A release that only the installer can operate is not a handover.

Simulate a power failure in the way the installer recommends, and confirm the gate behaves as described: it may hold position, it may be releasable manually, or it may have a backup supply. Write down which. If the site has a backup supply, note how long it is expected to carry the gate and who checks it.

This is also the moment to record the interfaces. If the gate is opened by a remote, a keypad, a card reader or an intercom, list each one and note whether it was tested today. Where a smart lock or access control system is part of the same entrance, the published guidance from Eyonic is that these work best when door condition, user roles and a backup entry method are reviewed before installation, and the same discipline applies to the gate: know your backup before you need it.

The pass/fail sheet you sign

Keep the sheet to one page. A worked example of the columns, using fictional entries, looks like this:

Item: leaf reaches fully open stop. Result: pass. Note: quiet, no strain, 14 seconds. Item: leaf meets post at close. Result: pass. Note: gap checked by hand, no daylight visible. Item: safety beam breaks during close. Result: pass. Note: gate reversed, installer demonstrated. Item: obstruction sensitivity value recorded. Result: pass. Note: value written on sheet by installer. Item: manual release operated by site staff. Result: fail. Note: key not yet cut; installer to supply and demonstrate on return visit. Item: power failure behaviour confirmed. Result: pass. Note: gate holds position, manual release required. Item: remote controls issued and counted. Result: pass. Note: two issued, serials recorded.

At the foot of the page, three lines: accepted by (name and signature), installer (name and signature), and date of return for open items. Anything marked fail gets a one-line description and an owner. Do not sign the page as complete while a fail line is open; sign it as received with open items, which is an honest record and a useful lever.

Photograph the signed sheet and file it with the quotation, the controller settings note and the release key record. If the same compound later adds cameras or an alarm, that file is the reference for what the gate was doing on day one.

After the walk: what to hand to the people who use the gate daily

The signed sheet is for you. The people who use the gate need something shorter: a single page with the release method, the name and number of the person who holds the key, what to do if the gate stops halfway, and who to call. Put it where the gate is operated, not in a filing cabinet.

If the gate shares an entrance with a card reader or intercom, add a line on that page about what to do when the reader fails, because the fallback is usually the gate release. If the site also runs CCTV security systems covering the driveway, note on the same page who reviews footage and how a request is made, so the gate and the cameras are handled by the same understood process rather than two separate ones.

Finally, set a review date. A short re-walk at three months, using the same sheet, catches drift in leaf travel and confirms the release key is still where it should be. That second walk takes minutes and turns a one-off handover into a maintained record.
