CCTV Site Survey Checklist Before Signing Installation

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A pre-signature CCTV site survey checklist for Sarawak premises managers, covering power points, cable routes, NVR location, lighting changes and sign-off owners so the quote matches the site.

Why the survey comes before the signature

A quotation is only as good as the walk-through behind it. When a premises manager signs before a proper survey, small site realities surface later: a camera position with no nearby power, a cable run that needs a longer conduit path than assumed, or a recorder cupboard that turns out to be a damp store room. Each of those becomes a variation, a delay or an argument about scope.

The practical answer is to treat the survey as a document, not a conversation. Walk the site with the installer, write down what you both saw, and attach it to the quotation. The signed survey becomes the shared reference for what was priced, what was excluded and who confirms each item.

Power points and cable routes: the two items that move quotes most

Before anyone discusses camera models, map the power and the paths. These two items are where a verbal quote and a site reality most often diverge.

Power points. For each candidate camera position, record whether a power outlet already exists within a sensible distance, or whether the position depends on power over the network cable. Note the outlet type and whether it is on a circuit you control. Do not assume an outlet in a neighbouring tenant's area is available to you.

Cable routes. Walk the actual path a cable would take, not the straight line on a floor plan. Record ceilings, wall construction, trunking needs, and any point where a route would cross a doorway, a shared corridor or a landlord-owned wall. Photograph each route with a note of the direction of travel.

A simple table works: position reference, power available yes or no, route description, route obstacles, and the name of the person who confirmed it. Keep the table with the quotation. If a route later changes, you have a written starting point rather than a memory.

For the reader comparing options, the useful question is not which camera is best but which positions are genuinely serviceable. A position that needs a long, awkward route is a cost and a maintenance risk, and it belongs in the survey notes before pricing.

Camera placement: write down what each view must show

Camera placement is a decision about purpose, not about covering the most wall. For each proposed camera, write one sentence describing what the view must show and who uses that view. Examples: the back door during closing, the counter during a cash handover, the vehicle approach to a gate.

Then record what could block or spoil that view. Shelving that may change height, signage, a future partition, foliage, or a light source that points into the lens. Note the height at which the camera would sit and whether anyone can reach it easily for cleaning or adjustment.

Add a column for privacy. If a camera view would look into a neighbouring unit, a public walkway or a staff rest area, note it and ask the installer how the view will be limited. You do not need to design the technical answer; you need the question on the record so it is addressed before installation.

Finally, mark which positions are essential and which are nice to have. If the budget tightens, the essential list is what you protect. This is also the point to note whether the site needs a mix of indoor and outdoor views, since weather exposure affects both housing and mounting choices.

Keep the placement notes in the same document as the power and route tables. A camera position that cannot be powered or cabled is not a placement decision yet.

NVR location, recording and remote viewing questions

The recorder is often decided last and regretted first. Choose its location during the survey, not on installation day.

Location questions to settle on site: Is the space ventilated and dry? Is it secure from casual access? Is there room to reach the back of the unit and the cabling? Is there a power point and a network connection nearby? Who holds the key to that space?

Recording questions to put in writing: what recording schedule is proposed, how many days of retention are expected, and what happens to recordings when storage fills. Ask for these as stated assumptions in the quotation, because retention depends on camera count, resolution and schedule together.

Remote viewing questions: who will be able to view footage away from the premises, on what device, and who approves adding or removing a viewer? Ask the installer to demonstrate remote viewing during handover and to show you how access is removed when a staff member leaves. Do not accept a verbal promise that it works; ask for a short demonstration as part of acceptance.

Lighting changes and the site conditions that shift over a year

A survey captures one moment. A year of operation brings different moments: monsoon afternoons, early closing in the dark, a new backlit sign, a change of shelving, or a renovation next door.

Record the lighting at each camera position at the time of the survey and note what you expect to change. Ask whether the position depends on daylight, on existing lamps, or on lighting the installer would add. Note any strong light source that faces the lens, and any area that is dark at closing time.

Also record seasonal or operational changes you already know about: a planned extension, a change of opening hours, a new delivery schedule, or a neighbouring unit due for renovation. These are not predictions of failure; they are known variables that belong in the survey notes so the design can account for them or the quotation can state that it does not.

A short line in the document is enough: condition observed, expected change, date expected, and who will confirm it. That single habit prevents the common surprise where a camera that worked in the dry season is judged against a different scene six months later.

Who signs off each item: a simple responsibility table

A survey without named owners is a wish list. Before signing the quotation, assign a person to each line. Use roles rather than names where staff change often, and add a name in the margin.

Suggested columns: item, decision needed, owner, date confirmed, and where it is recorded. Typical rows include power availability at each position, cable route approval, recorder room access, remote viewing user list, lighting assumptions, and the acceptance test that will be run at handover.

Two ownership questions deserve their own rows. First, who approves changes after signature, and what is the process for a variation? Second, who receives the handover pack, including the recorder location, the user list and the demonstration record? If the premises manager is not the person who will run the system day to day, both people should be at the survey or at least named in it.

This table is an author heuristic, not a standard. Adapt it to your organisation. The point is that every assumption in the quotation has a person behind it, so a later question has an obvious first call.

Turning the survey into a quote you can compare

Once the checklist is complete, use it to read quotations side by side. Compare what each quote assumes about power, routes, recorder location, retention and remote viewing access. A quote that is silent on a checklist item is not necessarily wrong, but the silence should be resolved before signature.

Ask each installer to mark up your checklist rather than sending a separate document. That keeps the comparison on the same rows. Where a quote excludes an item, ask for it to be written as an exclusion with a reason.
