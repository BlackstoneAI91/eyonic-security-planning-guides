Queue System Counter Display Configuration Checklist

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical checklist for Sarawak premises managers to configure queue system counter displays for clarity and visibility, covering placement, content, calling workflow and handover testing.

Why the Counter Display Deserves Its Own Checklist

The counter display is the part of a queue system that every visitor reads, yet it is often configured last, after the ticket dispenser and the calling software. That order of work invites problems: a screen mounted where reflections wash it out, a counter number that does not match the number printed on the ticket, or a calling sequence staff never agreed to. This guide is a configuration checklist for premises managers in Sarawak who are planning an ordinary service-counter setup and want the display to be legible, consistent and easy to hand over. It is written as a planning aid, not as a report of any particular installation. Treat the timings and scoring suggestions below as author heuristics and examples, not as established research. Where a brand claim appears, it is drawn from the supplied evidence and cited. Eyonic Security Systems is the brand behind this resource; its published service scope covers planning, supplying and installing monitoring systems with camera placement, recording and remote viewing for homes, offices, shops and industrial sites. That scope is useful context for how a queue display fits alongside other premises systems, but the checklist itself is generic and can be applied to any counter hall.

Step 1: Fix the Display's Job Before Choosing Hardware

Before anyone drills a bracket or orders a screen, write one sentence describing what the display must do. Common jobs include showing the next ticket number per counter, showing which counter is serving which number, and showing a waiting count. A display that tries to do all three at once on a small screen becomes unreadable from the back of the hall. Decide the primary job and let secondary information be smaller or rotate slowly. Then decide the viewing distance. A counter display read from three metres needs larger characters than one read from eight metres. As a working example, a rule of thumb some planners use is roughly one centimetre of character height for every three metres of viewing distance; test it in your own hall rather than treating it as a standard. Record the decision in a short configuration note: primary job, secondary information, maximum viewing distance, and the person who approves changes. This note becomes the reference when a technician asks what the screen should show, and it prevents the display from being configured by whoever happens to be on site. Keep the note with the rest of the premises documentation so a future manager can see why the layout was chosen.

Step 2: Place the Display Where Eyes Actually Go

Walk the arrival path yourself, from the entrance to the ticket dispenser to the seating area, and note where a waiting visitor naturally looks. The display should sit inside that natural sightline, not behind a pillar or above a doorway people walk through. Check for glare at the times of day the counter is busiest; a screen that faces a bright window may be unreadable in the afternoon even if it looks fine in the morning. Check the height: a display mounted too high forces people to crane their necks, and one mounted too low is blocked by standing visitors. Confirm that the display is visible from the seated waiting area, not only from the standing queue. If the hall has more than one waiting zone, decide whether one display serves both or whether a second screen is needed, and write that decision down. Finally, confirm power and cable routes before mounting, so the screen is not left with a loose cable across a walkway. This is the same discipline that applies to camera placement in a monitoring project: position is decided by sightlines and obstructions, not by where a fixing point happens to exist.

Step 3: Configure Content, Language and Numbering

Content configuration is where most confusion is created or avoided. Agree the numbering format first: does the ticket show a plain number, a prefix plus number, or a service letter plus number? The display must use exactly the same format, including leading zeros, so a visitor holding ticket 007 is not left guessing whether the screen showing 7 means them. Agree the language or languages shown. In a Sarawak service hall, a display that alternates between English and another language may serve visitors better than one that assumes a single language, but alternating text must be timed so each version is readable in full. Decide how long each message stays on screen; a rotating message that changes every two seconds is decorative rather than useful. Agree what the display shows when no counter is calling, and what it shows when the system is paused for a break. Avoid abbreviations that only staff understand. Have one person read the configured screen from the back of the hall and confirm every element is legible, then record the approved wording in the configuration note.

Step 4: Align the Calling Workflow With the Display

The display is only as clear as the calling workflow behind it. Agree how staff call the next ticket: from a counter terminal, a keypad, or a shared console. Agree whether a called ticket stays on screen until the visitor arrives or clears after a set interval, and what happens if the visitor does not appear. Agree whether a ticket can be recalled and how that recall appears on screen, so a recalled number is visibly different from a new call. Agree the audible cue, if any, and its volume relative to the hall's background noise. Write a short script for staff covering the normal call, the recall, the no-show, and the transfer to another counter. Test the script with two or three staff members before opening, using fictional ticket numbers, and note any step where the display and the spoken announcement disagree. That disagreement is the defect to fix. Keep the script with the configuration note so new staff learn the same sequence. If the counter also handles phone enquiries, note where the keyphone system sits in the workflow so a staff member is not calling a queue ticket and answering a call at the same moment.

Step 5: Test Visibility Under Real Conditions

A display that reads perfectly on an empty hall at midday may fail on a busy morning. Run a visibility test under conditions close to your busiest period: lights on as normal, blinds in their usual position, a few people standing in the waiting area. Stand at the farthest point a visitor might wait and read the screen aloud to a colleague, who checks it against the ticket. Repeat from a seated position and from the entrance. Note any angle where the number is unclear. If the hall has strong daylight, test at the time of day when the sun is lowest against the windows. Record the results in a simple table: position, readability pass or fail, and the fix proposed. This is the same acceptance discipline used when a monitoring system is handed over, where recording and remote viewing are checked rather than assumed. A display that has not been read from the back of the hall has not been tested.

Step 6: Handover, Documentation and Change Control

Configuration that lives only in a technician's memory disappears with the next service visit. Prepare a handover pack containing the configuration note, the approved screen wording, the staff calling script, the visibility test results, and the name of the person who approves changes. Confirm who holds the administrator login for the queue system and where it is recorded, without writing credentials into shared documents. Agree a change process: any alteration to numbering, language, timing or layout is requested in writing, approved by the named owner, and recorded in the note with a date. Agree a routine check, for example a weekly glance that the display is showing the correct date and the correct counter set, and a monthly read-through from the back of the hall. If the counter hall also has CCTV security systems covering the entrance or the service area, note in the handover pack how the queue display and the cameras are kept independent, so a screen failure does not affect recording and a camera adjustment does not disturb the display. A One-Page Configuration Worksheet

Use this worksheet to capture decisions before configuration begins. Section A, purpose: primary job of the display, secondary information, maximum viewing distance, named approver. Section B, placement: mounting position, height, glare check result, visibility from seated area, power and cable route confirmed. Section C, content: numbering format, language or languages, message rotation timing, idle message, break message. Section D, workflow: calling method, recall behaviour, no-show handling, transfer handling, audible cue and volume. Section E, testing: farthest reading position, seated reading position, entrance reading position, pass or fail per position, fixes proposed. Section F, handover: administrator holder recorded, change process agreed, routine check frequency, date of first review. Score each section as complete, partial or open, and treat any open item as a reason to delay go-live rather than a detail to fix later. The worksheet is deliberately short so it can be completed in one sitting with the counter supervisor and the technician in the same room. Keep the completed sheet with the handover pack and review it whenever the hall layout, the counter set or the staff roster changes.
