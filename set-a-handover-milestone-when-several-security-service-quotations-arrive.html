Set a Handover Milestone When Several Security Service Quotations Arrive Together

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical guide for Sarawak premises managers who receive several security quotations at once: build a non-technical evaluation calendar that separates site survey, owner review, documentation and acceptance by authorised people.

Start with a handover date, not a price comparison

When several quotations land in the same week, the instinct is to open a spreadsheet and compare totals. That comparison is useful, but it answers the wrong first question. Before you rank anything, decide the date by which your premises must be able to say, in writing, that the delivered work has been reviewed and accepted by the people authorised to accept it. Call that the handover milestone. It is a calendar entry, not a technical test.

A handover milestone gives every quotation a common finish line. Vendors who describe the same end point are easier to compare than vendors who each describe a different one. It also protects you from a quiet drift: a project that is 'nearly done' for weeks because nobody agreed who signs the acceptance note.

Write three lines at the top of your evaluation file: the target handover date, the name of the role that will accept the work, and the name of the role that will hold the documentation afterwards. Keep names in a separate access-controlled file if your organisation prefers; the evaluation calendar only needs roles. This article is a planning aid, not legal or procurement advice, and it does not assume what any quotation includes.

Map the four evaluation stages to a simple calendar

A workable evaluation calendar has four stages, each with an owner and a short output. Treat the day counts below as an author's example, not a standard.

Stage one, survey review: collect what each vendor recorded during its site survey and note where the descriptions differ. Output: a one-page list of differences. Example timing: three working days after the last survey.

Stage two, owner review: the person who controls the budget and the person who runs the premises read the differences together. Output: a shortlist and a written reason for it. Example timing: five working days.

Stage three, documentation check: confirm which drawings, guides and contact records each shortlisted vendor says it will leave behind. Output: a documentation column in the comparison. Example timing: three working days.

Stage four, acceptance planning: agree who will be present, what will be reviewed and how the result will be recorded. Output: a dated acceptance note template. Example timing: before work begins.

For a CCTV project, the survey and documentation stages matter because camera placement, recording and remote viewing are all described in the quotation before anyone can check them. Eyonic Security Systems plans, supplies and installs monitoring systems with camera placement, recording and remote viewing for homes, offices, shops and industrial sites, so a CCTV installation quotation from this kind of provider should be read against those same four stages rather than against price alone.

Give each role one decision and no more

Quotation reviews stall when everyone is asked to approve everything. Split the decisions by role and write the split down.

The premises manager decides access arrangements: when authorised people can attend, which areas are in scope for the survey, and how ordinary visitors will be told about any change. The budget owner decides the shortlist and the reason for it. The documentation owner decides where the handover pack will live and who may read it. The acceptance role decides whether the delivered work matches what was described, and records that decision with a date.

A useful test: for each stage in your calendar, ask 'if this person is on leave, who decides?' If the answer is 'nobody', the stage is not yet owned. Record a named backup role, not a name, in the shared calendar.

Keep the acceptance role separate from the vendor. The person who accepts the work should be able to point to the quotation line or the survey note that the work is being checked against. If no such line exists, that is a documentation gap to raise before the handover date, not after it.

Use the site survey as the first comparison column

The site survey is where quotations quietly diverge. Two vendors can quote for the same building and describe different camera positions, different assumptions about who reaches a rooftop, or different ideas about where recording equipment will sit. Your first comparison column should therefore be 'what the survey recorded', not 'what the price is'.

Ask each vendor for a short written summary of its survey: which areas were visited, which locations were discussed, and which questions remain open. You do not need technical detail to compare these summaries; you need consistency. If one summary names locations and another does not, that difference belongs in your file.

Eyonic Security Systems lists site survey and camera placement among its service areas, alongside NVR setup and remote viewing. That is useful context for what a survey conversation may cover, but it is not a promise about any particular quotation. Your job at this stage is to record what each vendor actually wrote, then let the owner review stage decide which differences matter. Keep the survey summaries in the same folder as the calendar so the acceptance role can find them later.

Write the documentation column before you shortlist

Documentation is the part of a quotation that is easiest to skip and hardest to recover later. Before shortlisting, add a column for each vendor answering four plain questions: what written material will be handed over, in what form, to which role, and on what date. Leave the answer blank if the quotation does not say. A blank is information.

This column also helps when a project mixes systems. A premises manager comparing a door entry proposal with a camera proposal needs to know which document describes which system, and who holds the master copy. Eyonic Security Systems notes that smart locks work best when the door condition, user roles and backup method are reviewed before installation, and that for business use it usually checks whether a standalone smart lock or an access-control panel is more appropriate. That is a reminder to record the review, not a claim about your building.

Keep the documentation column free of credentials, codes and personal contact details. The calendar should say where the handover pack lives and who owns it, not what it contains.

Run a short acceptance rehearsal before the milestone

An acceptance rehearsal is a meeting, not a test of equipment. A week before the handover milestone, gather the acceptance role, the documentation owner and the premises manager for thirty minutes. Walk through the acceptance note line by line and ask: can we answer this today? If not, who will be able to answer it, and by when?

Use a simple scoring sheet as an author's example: each acceptance line gets 'ready', 'needs a document' or 'needs a conversation'. Do not score vendors on this sheet; score your own readiness. The output is a short list of gaps with an owner and a date, which is far more useful than a percentage.

If a gap concerns a system you have not yet chosen, such as an autogate installation or a burglar alarm system, note it as an open question for the owner review rather than resolving it in the rehearsal. The rehearsal exists to protect the handover date, not to reopen the whole comparison.

After the rehearsal, send each shortlisted vendor one written question list. Keep the questions about documents, dates and named roles. This keeps the conversation comparable and gives the acceptance role something to check against on the day.

Close the milestone with a dated record

The handover milestone is only real if it produces a dated record. On the agreed date, the acceptance role writes a short note: what was reviewed, what was seen, what remains open, and who owns each open item. Attach the survey summaries and the documentation column. Store the note where the documentation owner said it would live.

Two habits keep this record useful. First, separate observation from opinion: 'the guide was handed to the premises manager on this date' is an observation; 'the system seems fine' is not. Second, record open items with a review date, so a small gap does not become an unowned one.

If your project also covers smart lock door access, the same discipline applies. Eyonic Security Systems describes smart lock door access as good for controlled convenience when user permissions and backup entry are planned properly. Planning permissions and a backup method is an owner decision to record, not something to leave until after acceptance.

Finally, keep the calendar as a template for the next round of quotations. A premises manager who has run one handover milestone has a reusable structure for the next, which is the quiet benefit of doing the calendar before the comparison.
