Auto Gate Acceptance Test Before Final Payment: A Worked Sequence for Sarawak Premises

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical acceptance sequence for premises managers in Sarawak: run manual release, obstacle reversal, remote range and battery backup tests, log defects, and only then release final payment on an autogate installation.

Why the acceptance test comes before the final payment

Final payment is your last piece of leverage on any security project. Once it is released, the installer's incentive to return for small fixes drops, and every adjustment becomes a separate service call. That is why the acceptance test should be scheduled as a formal event with a date, a named witness and a written record, not squeezed into the last ten minutes of an installation day.

This guide is written for premises managers in Sarawak planning an ordinary autogate project. It sets out a worked sequence you can run on the day, a defect log format, and a simple rule for deciding whether the gate is ready for sign-off. It is general guidance, not a technical manual. Anything involving wiring, motor internals, sensor alignment or control-board settings belongs to the installer, and you should not open panels or attempt adjustments yourself.

One framing point before the sequence. An autogate is rarely a standalone purchase. It usually sits alongside cameras watching the approach, a door or pedestrian entry point, and sometimes an alarm. Treat the gate acceptance as one part of a wider handover so that the same visit covers the related items. Before the visit: assemble the paperwork and the people

An acceptance test fails more often from missing paperwork than from a faulty gate. Gather the following before the installer arrives, and put it in one folder or shared drive:

1. The signed quotation or scope, including what was supplied and what was excluded. 2. The agreed operating description: how the gate is meant to open, how it is meant to close, and what safety features were promised. 3. The names of everyone who will hold a remote or key, and who authorises new ones. 4. A blank defect log (format below). 5. The name of the person authorised to sign acceptance, and a backup if they are unavailable.

Decide in advance who attends. At minimum: the installer's technician, the person who will operate the gate daily, and a manager who can sign. If the gate serves a shared compound, invite one representative of the other users so they hear the operating rules first-hand rather than second-hand.

Set the expectation with the installer that the visit ends with either a signed acceptance or a written defect list with a return date. Both outcomes are acceptable. What is not acceptable is a verbal "we'll come back and sort it" with no record.

Step one: manual release, before anything is powered

Start with the gate in its normal closed position and the power still on, then follow the installer's demonstration of the manual release. You are not being asked to perform a technical procedure; you are being asked to watch, then repeat it once under supervision so you know it works and know where the release mechanism is.

Record these points in the log:

- Where the manual release is located, and whether the location is accessible without moving a vehicle. - Whether the person who will operate the gate daily can reach it and operate it. - Whether the release was demonstrated with the gate partly open as well as fully closed. - Whether the gate, once released, can be moved by hand without excessive force.

If the release is behind a locked panel, confirm who holds the key and that a spare is accounted for. If the release point is obstructed by parked cars at peak times, note it as a defect with a suggested remedy, such as a marked no-parking area. Keep the remedy as a request to the installer, not something you implement.

This step matters because it is the fallback when power fails or the motor misbehaves. A gate that cannot be released manually is a gate that can trap a vehicle at the worst possible moment.

Step two: obstacle reversal and the safety walk-through

Obstacle reversal is the single most important safety behaviour to witness, and it should be tested in both directions: while the gate is opening and while it is closing. Ask the installer to demonstrate with an approved test object, and watch from a safe position outside the gate's path.

For each direction, record:

- Whether the gate stopped and reversed when the test object was placed in its path. - How far the gate travelled before reacting. - Whether the reaction was consistent across three attempts. - Whether any warning light, sounder or signage operates as described in the scope.

Then walk the site with the installer and note the physical context: where a child, a delivery rider or a pedestrian could plausibly be when the gate moves, and whether the warning signage is visible from those positions. You are recording observations, not designing the safety system. If something looks wrong, log it and ask the installer to explain how the installed configuration addresses it.

A useful discipline here is to test at the times of day the gate is busiest. Reversal behaviour that works in flat morning light may feel different at dusk, and the people using the gate at dusk are the ones most likely to be caught out.

Step three: remote range, battery backup and everyday operation

Now test the gate the way it will actually be used. Take the remotes to the positions from which people will normally open the gate: the driver's seat approaching from the road, the pedestrian gate, the guard post or reception window if there is one. At each position, record whether the remote worked first time, and whether it worked consistently across several attempts.

Then test the battery backup. Ask the installer to demonstrate the gate operating on backup power with the mains supply isolated in the approved way. Record how the gate behaved, and ask two questions: how long the backup is expected to last under normal use, and what the user should do when it runs low. Write the answers down in the installer's own words.

Finish with the everyday operation checks:

- Each issued remote or key operates the gate. - The number of remotes issued matches the handover list. - The person who will operate the gate daily can open, close and stop it without prompting. - Any intercom, keypad or entry device linked to the gate behaves as described.

If the gate project sits alongside cameras, use this same visit to confirm the approach view. Camera placement should give a clear picture of vehicles and people at the gate, and the acceptance visit is the moment to check that the view is not blocked by a pillar, a sign or a parked car.

The defect log: one column that decides the payment

Keep the defect log simple enough that it gets filled in on the day. A table with these columns works well:

1. Item number. 2. What was tested (for example, obstacle reversal while closing). 3. What was expected. 4. What was observed. 5. Severity: blocking or non-blocking. 6. Owner (installer, or premises manager for site-side items such as clearing a parking space). 7. Agreed return date. 8. Signed off by, and date.

Severity is the column that decides the payment. A workable rule, and this is an author's heuristic rather than an industry standard: any defect that affects safe operation, manual release, obstacle reversal or the ability to open and close the gate reliably is blocking. Cosmetic items, spare remote quantities and documentation gaps are non-blocking and can be listed for follow-up.

Apply the rule consistently. If the gate reverses correctly but the warning sign is missing, that is likely blocking, because the sign is part of how people are warned. If the paint on the control box is scuffed, that is not. Write the reason next to each severity call so that a reader who was not present can follow your reasoning.

At the end of the visit, both parties sign the log. If there are blocking defects, the acceptance is conditional and the final payment waits for the return visit. If there are none, sign acceptance and record the date.

After acceptance: handover, records and the wider system

Acceptance is not the end of the project; it is the point at which responsibility for day-to-day operation moves to your team. Close the loop with a short handover pack:

- The signed acceptance sheet and defect log, with any return visits recorded. - The remote and key register, naming each holder and the authorisation route for new ones. - The installer's written notes on backup duration and what to do when it runs low. - A one-page operating note for staff, covering normal use, the manual release location and who to call when something is wrong. - The service and warranty terms, with dates.

If the gate shares a site with cameras, alarms or door entry, keep the records together. A single project folder makes the next handover, audit or staff change far easier, and it stops gate documents drifting into one manager's email while camera documents sit somewhere else. Where a site also uses controlled door entry, the same discipline applies: permissions and backup entry should be planned and recorded rather than assumed, which is the principle behind smart lock door access done properly.

Finally, set a review date. A short walk-through a few weeks after acceptance, with the defect log in hand, catches the small things that only appear once the gate is in daily use. That review is cheap. Discovering a problem months later, after the final payment and the warranty conversation have both gone quiet, is not.
