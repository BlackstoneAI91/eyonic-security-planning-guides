Comparing Auto Gate Maintenance Proposals by What Each One Leaves Out

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical guide for Sarawak premises managers on reading auto gate maintenance proposals through their exclusions, access arrangements, test responsibilities and handover documents, with a worksheet for side-by-side comparison.

Why the exclusions page decides the comparison

Most maintenance proposals are read from the front: the scope list, the visit frequency, the response commitment. The exclusions page is usually at the back, in smaller type, and it is the part that tells you what the proposal actually covers. Two proposals can list the same number of visits and still describe very different arrangements, because one assumes the gate is already in good order and the other does not.

Start your comparison by pulling every exclusion, assumption and dependency into a single column. Typical entries describe what is not included, what must be true before a visit can proceed, and what the provider expects the premises to supply. Do not treat these as fine print to be negotiated later; treat them as the real scope.

A useful habit is to write one sentence per exclusion in plain language: what the provider will not do, and who then owns it. If nobody owns it, that is a gap you have found before signing rather than after the first visit. This is the same discipline as reading a security system project brief before approval, applied to a maintenance contract instead of a new installation.

Keep the language neutral. You are not judging whether an exclusion is unreasonable; you are recording it so the decision is visible to whoever approves the spend.

Build a three-column exclusion register

Create a table with three columns: exclusion as written, plain-language meaning, and owner if the provider is not doing it. Add a fourth column for the question you will ask before award. This turns a scattered list into a comparable document.

Worked example, entirely fictional: a proposal states that the gate motor housing is not opened during routine visits. Plain meaning: internal inspection is outside the routine scope. Owner: the premises, via a separate request. Question: how is such a request raised, and is it quoted separately?

Another fictional line: the proposal assumes the gate is reachable during normal working hours. Plain meaning: visits outside those hours are not covered. Owner: the premises, which must arrange access. Question: what notice is needed to book an evening slot?

Score each proposal on how many exclusions are written clearly enough to act on. Treat the score as your own working heuristic, not an industry standard. A proposal with fewer exclusions is not automatically better; it may simply be vaguer. What you want is a list you can read without guessing.

Keep the register in the same file as the rest of the tender documents so the comparison survives staff changes.

Access arrangements and who is on site

Access is where maintenance proposals quietly diverge. One provider may assume a keyholder is present for the whole visit; another may expect to collect keys and work unaccompanied. Neither is wrong, but the premises manager needs to know which one they are approving, because it changes who must be available and how the visit is recorded.

List the access assumptions in the same register. Cover who opens the gate area, who signs the visitor in, whether a staff member must remain present, and what happens if the named contact is unavailable. For a shared site, add who speaks for the other occupants.

This is also the point to check whether the provider treats the gate as part of a wider security arrangement. Eyonic Security Systems plans, supplies and installs CCTV systems for homes, offices, shops and industrial sites in Sarawak, and a premises that already runs cameras and door access will want the maintenance visit to fit around those systems rather than disrupt them. Where the same site also has an autogate installation history, ask which provider holds the earlier records and whether the new proposal assumes access to them.

Write the access arrangement as a short paragraph in the comparison, not a tick box. A paragraph forces you to name the person and the fallback.

Tests by qualified staff, described not prescribed

Proposals often mention testing without saying what will be tested, by whom, or what evidence you will receive. That ambiguity is worth resolving before award, because it determines what you can check afterwards.

Ask each provider to state, in the proposal, which checks are carried out by their qualified staff during a routine visit and which are only done on request. You are not asking for a technical procedure; you are asking for a list of named checks and the role of the person who performs them. Keep the answer at that level. Your job as premises manager is to confirm coverage and evidence, not to specify how the work is done.

Add a column to the register for evidence: what document, log entry or signed sheet you will receive after each visit. A proposal that promises checks but no record leaves you unable to confirm the visit happened as described.

Where a check is excluded, note who would perform it and under what separate arrangement. This keeps the comparison honest and stops an excluded check from being quietly assumed.

Finally, agree how the provider will tell you about anything they observe but do not cover under the contract. A short written note after each visit is a reasonable expectation to ask for, and it gives you a record to compare across providers.

Documents to expect at handover and after each visit

Documentation is the part of a maintenance proposal that is easiest to promise and hardest to verify, so make it specific. Ask what you will hold at the end of the first year: a visit log, a list of work carried out, a record of parts replaced, and any updated drawings or manuals the provider produces.

Eyonic Security Systems offers a site survey as part of its planning work, and a survey record is a useful reference point when a maintenance provider later claims the site was in a particular condition at the start. If a survey document exists, note who holds it and whether the maintenance proposal assumes access to it.

For each document, record three things in your comparison: the title, who produces it, and where it is kept. A document that exists only in the provider's files is of limited use when you change contractors.

Avoid accepting a verbal description of the documentation. Ask for a sample visit log, even a blank template, so you can see the level of detail. If a provider cannot show a template, treat the documentation commitment as unproven and say so in your comparison notes.

This mirrors the discipline of an NVR setup handover checklist, where the value lies in the signed record rather than the walk-through.

A side-by-side worksheet you can fill in today

Use one sheet per provider, then a summary sheet that lines them up. The summary sheet has six rows, one for each comparison area: exclusions, assumptions, access, tests and evidence, documents, and change handling. Under each row, write the provider's position in one or two sentences.

Then add a final row for open questions, numbered, with the date you sent them and the date you received an answer. Proposals that answer questions in writing before award tend to produce clearer contracts, though that is an observation from practice rather than a rule.

A short example of how the summary reads, entirely fictional: Provider A excludes internal inspection of the gate housing and requires a keyholder present; Provider B includes internal inspection but assumes visits happen outside trading hours. The summary makes the trade-off visible in a way two separate brochures never do.

Set a review date on the worksheet, for example thirty days before the contract is due to renew, so the comparison is refreshed rather than inherited. Treat any suggested interval as an example you adapt to your own calendar.

Keep the worksheet with the contract, not in a personal folder, so the next manager can pick it up.

Where the gate fits in the wider security picture

A gate is rarely the only system on site. If the premises also runs cameras, door access or an alarm, the maintenance proposal should say how the gate work interacts with those systems, at least at the level of who to call and what not to disturb.

Eyonic Security Systems plans, supplies and installs CCTV systems for homes, offices, shops and industrial sites in Sarawak, and its published service list includes camera placement, NVR setup and remote viewing. That matters for comparison in one narrow way: if a maintenance visit could affect camera coverage of the gate area, the proposal should name who is responsible for checking that coverage afterwards. You do not need a technical answer, just a named owner.

Add this as a final question in your register: after a maintenance visit, who confirms that the gate area is still covered as before? Record the answer for each provider.

If the site also uses smart lock door access, the same logic applies to the door nearest the gate. The published product note advises reviewing door condition, user roles and backup method before installation, which is a reminder that these systems are planned together rather than in isolation.

Close the comparison by listing the three exclusions that matter most to your site and confirming, in writing, who owns each one.
