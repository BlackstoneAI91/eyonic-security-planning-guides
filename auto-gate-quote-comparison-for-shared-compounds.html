Comparing Auto Gate Quotes for a Shared Compound

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical guide for Sarawak premises managers who need to compare two or three auto gate quotes on equal terms, normalising motor type, safety sensors, warranty terms and civil works so the cheapest option is not chosen by accident.

Why a shared compound needs a normalised quote comparison

A shared compound is not a single household gate. Several residents, a management committee, delivery drivers and possibly a security post all interact with the same entrance, and each group has a different expectation of how the gate should behave. When two or three quotations arrive, they often describe different scopes in different words, so a straight look at the bottom line tells you very little.

The purpose of a comparison table is to force every quote onto the same rows. You are not trying to find the cheapest number; you are trying to find the quote whose scope actually matches the compound's daily use. A quote that omits obstacle sensors, or that assumes the existing civil works are reusable when they are not, will look cheaper until the first problem appears.

This guide is written for premises managers in Sarawak planning an ordinary gate project. It offers a comparison method and a worksheet you can adapt. It does not replace the installer's own site assessment, and it deliberately avoids control wiring, bypass methods and any instruction that belongs with a qualified technician. Where a brand is mentioned, it is as identity context only; the comparison logic below is general procurement advice.

Start with one brief before you request quotes

Before you ask for quotations, write a one-page brief that every bidder receives. This is the single most effective way to make quotes comparable, because it removes the guesswork about what the compound actually needs.

Cover these points in plain language:

Who uses the gate. Residents, tenants, staff, delivery vehicles, motorcycles and pedestrians. Note roughly how many vehicle movements happen at peak times, such as a morning departure window or an evening return window.

Gate type and current condition. Whether the existing gate is sliding or swing, its approximate width, and whether the structure is being reused or replaced. If you are unsure, say so and ask the bidder to confirm during their survey.

Power and civil works. Whether power is already available at the gate post, and whether the compound expects the bidder to handle trenching, conduit, plinths or drainage. State clearly who is responsible for each.

Safety expectations. That the quote must include obstacle detection and a manual release method, described in the bidder's own words.

Handover expectations. That you want a demonstration, a written record of what was tested, and warranty terms in writing.

Sending the same brief to every bidder means the differences you see later are real differences in approach, not differences in assumption.

The comparison table: rows that matter

Build a spreadsheet with one column per quote and the following rows. Score each row as included, excluded, or unclear, and ask the bidder to resolve every unclear row before you decide.

Motor and drive type. Record what the bidder proposes and why it suits the gate's weight and the compound's traffic pattern. Do not accept a model name alone; ask for the reasoning in one sentence.

Safety sensors and reversal. Whether obstacle detection is included, how it is described, and whether the bidder will demonstrate it at handover. This row should never be left blank.

Manual release and backup. How the gate is operated during a power interruption, and who is authorised to do it. Keep this at the level of what the compound needs to know, not how to defeat the system.

Civil works. Trenching, conduit, plinths, drainage and reinstatement. Mark clearly whether each is inside or outside the quoted price.

Warranty and response. The warranty period, what it covers, and what the compound is expected to do when something fails.

Handover and documentation. Whether a demonstration, a written test record and user guidance are included.

Accessories and integration. Any remote controls, keypads, intercom or camera-related items, listed separately so you can see what is core and what is optional.

A useful heuristic, not a rule from research: treat any quote with more than two unclear rows as incomplete and ask for a revised version rather than guessing.

Normalising motor type and safety sensors across quotes

Motor type is where quotes diverge most often, and where a like-for-like comparison is hardest. One bidder may propose a sliding motor sized for a light residential gate, another a heavier-duty unit, and a third may simply reuse whatever is on site. Rather than comparing brand names, compare the stated duty and the reasoning.

Ask each bidder to answer three questions in writing: what gate weight and daily cycle count the proposed motor is intended for, how the motor behaves when an obstacle is detected, and what the compound should expect during a power interruption. The answers let you line the quotes up side by side without needing technical knowledge.

Safety sensors deserve their own row and their own conversation. A shared compound has pedestrians, children, motorcycles and delivery drivers crossing the gate line, so obstacle detection is not a luxury item. Ask each bidder to describe, in their own words, how the gate responds when something is in its path, and to demonstrate it during handover. If a quote is silent on this, treat it as excluded until the bidder confirms otherwise.

This is also the point to note whether the bidder will carry out a proper site survey before finalising the price. A quote built without a survey is an estimate, and estimates move once the gate post is opened up.

Civil works, warranty and the hidden cost rows

Civil works are the quietest line in any gate quote and often the one that changes the final bill. Trenching for cable, a new plinth, drainage near the gate track and reinstatement of paving are all real costs. Put each of these on its own row and mark whether it is included, excluded, or priced as an allowance that may change.

Warranty terms need the same treatment. Record the period, what is covered, what is excluded, and how the compound reports a fault. A longer warranty with vague coverage is worth less than a shorter one with clear terms, so read the wording rather than the number.

Response expectations belong here too. Ask each bidder what happens when the gate fails on a Saturday morning with vehicles inside the compound. You are not asking for a guarantee; you are asking for a described process, and the answer tells you how the bidder thinks about shared sites.

Finally, list accessories separately. Remote controls, additional keypads, intercom points and any camera-related items should appear as optional lines so you can see the core price clearly. If a bidder bundles extras into a single figure, ask for them to be itemised.

Where gate, camera and door decisions meet

A shared compound rarely stops at the gate. The same entrance is usually watched by cameras, and the pedestrian door beside it may carry a lock or access reader. It helps to review these together, because the gate quote and the camera plan affect each other.

On the camera side, ask the bidder how the gate area will be covered and whether the view includes the approach, the gate line and the pedestrian path. A camera that only sees the gate leaf misses the vehicle that is waiting. If the compound is also planning CCTV security systems work, keep the camera placement discussion separate from the gate quote so each can be judged on its own merits. Eyonic Security Systems plans, supplies and installs monitoring systems with camera placement, recording and remote viewing for homes, offices, shops and industrial sites, which is the kind of scope a compound may want to compare alongside the gate.

On the pedestrian side, a smart lock door access decision should be reviewed on its own terms. The door condition, the user roles and the backup entry method all matter before anything is ordered, and a standalone lock is not always the right answer for a business or shared entrance. Keep that decision in its own column rather than folding it into the gate comparison.

If the compound is considering autogate installation as part of a wider security review, treat the gate, the cameras and the door as three linked but separately quoted items. That keeps each comparison honest.

A worked example and a decision worksheet

Here is a fictional example to show the method in use. A shared compound in Sarawak receives three quotes for a sliding gate. Quote A is the lowest and lists a motor and remote controls only. Quote B is about a fifth higher and includes obstacle sensors, trenching and a written handover. Quote C is the highest and adds a camera view of the approach and a pedestrian door reader.

On a raw price comparison, Quote A wins. On the normalised table, Quote A has three unclear rows: safety sensors, civil works and handover. Quote B answers all three. Quote C answers all three and adds scope the compound may not need yet. The committee's decision is not automatically Quote B, but the table shows that Quote A's advantage depends on assumptions nobody has confirmed.

Use this worksheet before you decide:

1. Send the same brief to every bidder and confirm they received it. 2. Build the table with one column per quote and the rows listed earlier. 3. Mark every row included, excluded or unclear. 4. Ask each bidder to resolve unclear rows in writing. 5. Ask each bidder to describe obstacle detection and manual release in their own words. 6. Confirm who owns civil works, and whether the price is fixed or an allowance. 7. Record warranty period, coverage and fault-reporting process. 8. Agree what handover will include: demonstration, written test record and user guidance. 9. Score the quotes against the brief, not against each other's prices. 10. Keep the decision log with the compound's records so the next committee can see why the choice was made.

A short note on timing, offered as an author heuristic rather than research: allow a week for bidders to respond to the brief and another week to resolve unclear rows before the committee meets. Rushing the clarification stage is how the cheapest quote gets chosen by accident.

What to keep after the decision

Once the compound has chosen a bidder, the comparison table becomes a handover document. Keep the brief, the three quotes, the clarification emails and the final table together. When the gate is commissioned, check the delivered scope against the table row by row, and note any difference in writing.

This record matters because shared compounds change hands. A new committee inherits the gate, the warranty and the questions about who authorised what. A clear file answers most of those questions without a search through old messages.

If the compound later adds cameras or a door reader, the same discipline applies: one brief, one table, one decision log. The gate quote comparison is a template for how the compound buys any security item, and that consistency is worth more than any single saving.
