Testing a Queue Management System Before Opening Day: A Multi-Counter Service Desk Guide

Prepared for Eyonic Security System (Eyonic CCTV)

A practical pre-opening test plan for a small multi-counter service desk, covering ticket types, counter transfers, accessibility, staff downtime procedure and acceptance scenarios for owners and facilities teams in Sarawak.

Why a Pre-Opening Test Is a Flow Exercise, Not a Button Check

A queue management system can power on, print tickets and show a number on a screen while still failing the people it is meant to serve. The real question before opening day is whether a customer can arrive, understand what to do, get the right ticket, reach the right counter, and leave without confusion when something goes wrong. That is a flow problem, and flow problems are best found by walking through them deliberately rather than by trusting a vendor demo.

This guide uses a fictional example: a service desk in Sarawak with four counters, two service categories and a shared waiting area. Nothing here describes a real installation or a real customer. Treat the numbers, timings and scores as author heuristics and examples, not as research findings or guarantees. The aim is to give owners and facilities teams a structured rehearsal they can adapt to their own premises.

One useful habit is to separate the test into two layers. The first layer is the equipment: does the ticket dispenser, display, audio announcement and counter terminal behave as expected. The second layer is the human procedure: what does a customer do, what does a staff member do, and what happens when the system is unavailable. A test that only covers the first layer tends to pass while the second layer quietly fails on opening morning.

If your premises also run security and ELV systems, coordinate the queue test with those systems rather than treating them as separate projects. For example, camera placement over a waiting area and counter row should be agreed during the same walkthrough, so that the view of the queue is not blocked by a new display or signage later. Step 1: Define Ticket Types and the Customer's First Thirty Seconds

Before testing anything, write down the ticket types in plain language and check that a first-time visitor can choose correctly without asking staff. In the fictional service desk, imagine three ticket types: General Enquiry, Document Collection and Appointment. The test is not whether the labels look tidy on a screen; it is whether a person walking in for the first time picks the right one. Ask someone who has not seen the layout to stand at the entrance and talk through their choice out loud.

Run a short scripted scenario for each ticket type. For General Enquiry, the customer should be able to find the dispenser, read the options, take a ticket and understand roughly what happens next. For Document Collection, check whether the ticket type leads to the correct counter group. For Appointment, confirm how an appointment holder is recognised, because a queue system that treats appointments like walk-ins can create a second, invisible queue. Record where the tester hesitated; hesitation is the most useful output of this step.

Keep the first-thirty-seconds test small and repeatable. A simple checklist works: can the dispenser be reached, is the screen readable at standing height, is the wording free of internal jargon, and is there a visible fallback if the dispenser is out of paper or offline. Score each item pass, partial or fail, and treat partial as a prompt to fix rather than a pass. These scores are an author heuristic for organising the review, not a certified assessment method.

Finally, decide who owns each ticket type. If two counters can serve the same ticket, say so explicitly in the test script, because shared responsibility is where transfers and misrouting usually appear.

Step 2: Rehearse Counter Transfers and Handoffs

Transfers are the part of a queue system most likely to be improvised on opening day. In the fictional desk, imagine a customer takes a General Enquiry ticket but the query turns out to need a document check at another counter. The test should rehearse that handoff deliberately: who initiates the transfer, what the customer is told, whether a new ticket is issued, and whether the original ticket is closed so it does not distort the waiting count.

Write two or three transfer scenarios and act them out with staff playing both roles. Scenario one: a straightforward transfer to a named counter. Scenario two: a transfer where the destination counter is busy, so the customer must wait again. Scenario three: a transfer that is refused because the query belongs to a different service entirely. For each, note what the customer hears and sees. A transfer that is technically complete but leaves the customer unsure where to go has not passed.

Also test the reverse case: a customer who reaches the wrong counter first. Staff should have a consistent, short explanation and a clear next step. Avoid inventing policy during the test; if the correct behaviour is unclear, mark it as an open decision and assign an owner. The goal of the rehearsal is to expose gaps, not to prove that the team already knows the answer.

Keep a simple transfer log during the test: time, from counter, to counter, reason, and whether the customer needed to be redirected again. Reviewing that log afterwards is often more informative than the live test itself.

Step 3: Test Accessibility and the Waiting Environment

Accessibility testing is often reduced to a ramp check, but a queue system adds its own barriers. In the fictional desk, test the dispenser height from a seated position, the readability of the display from the far end of the waiting area, and whether the audio announcement is clear without being intrusive. Ask a tester to move through the space as a person with limited mobility, a person accompanying a child, and a person who cannot easily read small text would experience it.

Check the waiting area as a whole. Seating should not block the path between the dispenser and the counters. Signage should match the ticket type names exactly, because mismatched wording forces customers to guess. If the display shows a counter number only, confirm that the counter itself is clearly numbered and visible from the seating area. These are small details, but they decide whether the queue feels orderly or chaotic in the first hour.

Where the premises also use CCTV security systems, the waiting area is a good place to confirm that coverage and queue layout do not conflict. A display mounted where it blocks a camera view, or a signage board that creates a blind spot, is easier to correct before opening than after. Step 4: Write and Rehearse the Staff Downtime Procedure

Every queue system will eventually be unavailable, whether from a power interruption, a network fault or a simple paper jam. The pre-opening test should include a deliberate downtime drill, because staff who have never practised the fallback will improvise under pressure. In the fictional desk, the drill might be: the dispenser stops issuing tickets, and the team must switch to a manual method within a stated time.

Define the fallback in advance. Who decides to switch? Where is the manual ticket pad or written list kept? How are waiting customers informed? How are tickets reconciled when the system returns, so that nobody is served twice or skipped? Rehearse the switch and the return, not just the outage. The return is often messier, because the system may resume with a queue that no longer matches the room.

Assign a downtime owner and a deputy, and write the procedure on one page that can be kept at the counter. Test that the page is findable without asking the person who wrote it. A short drill with a stopwatch is enough; the timing is an example target you set for your own team, not an industry benchmark.

If your premises use smart lock door access for staff areas, include the downtime question there too. Smart locks work best when the door condition, user roles and backup method are reviewed before installation, and for business use it is worth checking whether a standalone smart lock or an access-control panel is more appropriate. A staff-area door that cannot be opened during a system outage is a downtime problem just as much as a stalled ticket dispenser.

Step 5: Build Acceptance Scenarios and a Sign-Off Sheet

An acceptance scenario is a short, repeatable story with a clear pass condition. For the fictional desk, write five or six of them and run each one at least twice, ideally with different staff on the counter. Example scenarios: a walk-in with a General Enquiry ticket is served and closed; an appointment holder is recognised and routed correctly; a transfer between counters completes without a second redirect; a customer using a wheelchair can take a ticket and reach the counter; and a downtime drill switches to manual and back without losing a customer.

For each scenario, record the expected result, the observed result, and the decision: accept, accept with fix, or retest. Keep the sheet short enough that it is actually completed. A one-page sign-off with named owners is more useful than a long document nobody reads. Treat the pass conditions as your own acceptance criteria, not as a standard imposed by any vendor.

Include a final walkthrough on the day before opening, focused only on the fixes from the earlier test. Confirm that signage, ticket wording and counter numbering all match, and that the downtime page is in place. Then stop testing and let the team rest; a tired team on opening morning is its own risk.

If the premises combine queue management with CCTV security systems, use the same sign-off to confirm that camera placement, recording and remote viewing were checked during the same period. Step 6: Plan the First Week of Live Monitoring

Opening day is not the end of testing; it is the start of a short observation period. Decide in advance what you will watch during the first week: how often customers hesitate at the dispenser, how many transfers happen per day, how long the longest wait is, and whether any ticket type is consistently mischosen. Keep the list short and review it daily rather than collecting data nobody reads.

Assign one person to note issues in a simple log and one person to decide fixes. Separate cosmetic issues from flow issues, and fix flow issues first. If a ticket type is consistently mischosen, the label is usually the problem, not the customer. If transfers cluster around one counter, the routing rule may need adjusting.

Finally, connect the queue review to the wider premises. If the waiting area is covered by CCTV security systems, confirm that remote CCTV viewing is available to the person who needs it, and that access to recordings is limited to named roles.
