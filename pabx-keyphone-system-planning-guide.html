Mapping Call Routing for a Small Office Keyphone System

Prepared for Eyonic Security System (Eyonic CCTV)

A practical guide for owners and facilities teams in Sarawak to map call routing on a small office keyphone system, with ring groups, no-answer paths, after-hours messages and a staff acceptance checklist.

Start With a Call Inventory, Not a Wiring Diagram

Before anyone talks about extensions, ports or handsets, write down every way a call can arrive at your office. For a small premises this usually means one or two main lines, a general enquiry number, a billing or accounts line, and perhaps a direct number for a manager. Add the internal calls staff make to each other, because those shape how you group extensions later. The goal of this first step is a plain list of call types, not a technical plan.

Next to each call type, note who is expected to answer it and what should happen if nobody does. A general enquiry might be handled by whoever is at the front desk, while a supplier call might belong to accounts. Write these expectations down even if they feel obvious, because the disagreements usually appear here rather than at the equipment stage. If two people assume they own the same line, that is a routing decision you need to settle before installation.

This inventory also helps you brief an installer clearly. If your office already runs other low-voltage systems, the same discipline applies: a CCTV site survey starts by listing what needs to be seen, and a keyphone plan starts by listing what needs to be answered. The routing map itself is a separate exercise, but the habit of listing requirements first is the same.

Draw the Example Call-Flow Map

A call-flow map is a simple diagram you can sketch on one page. Put the incoming line at the top, then a decision point, then the destination extension or group. Use arrows for what happens next. Keep the labels in plain language such as front desk, accounts, workshop or manager, rather than extension numbers, so staff can read it without training.

Here is an illustrative example for a small office with six staff. The main line rings the front desk extension for four rings. If unanswered, it rings a reception ring group containing the front desk and one administrative colleague. If still unanswered after another four rings, it diverts to a recorded message asking the caller to leave a name and number, and then to a shared voicemail box that the office manager checks. A separate accounts line rings two accounts extensions at once, then falls back to the same recorded message. Internal calls between staff go direct and never enter the public routing path.

The ring counts above are examples, not recommendations from research. Treat them as placeholders you adjust after staff try the flow for a week. The important part is that every path has an ending. A call that rings forever, or that diverts to a mailbox nobody checks, is worse than a clear message telling the caller when to expect a reply. Draw the map, date it, and keep it with your other premises documentation so it can be updated when staff change.

Design Ring Groups Around Real Staffing

A ring group is a set of extensions that ring together when a call arrives. It is useful when several people share responsibility for answering, such as a front desk plus an administrator. The design question is not how many extensions you can add, but who is genuinely available during the hours the group is active. A group of five people where three are usually out of the office will simply ring longer before anyone answers.

Map your staffing across a normal week before you choose group members. Note lunch cover, days when someone works off-site, and any part-time schedules. Then decide whether the group should ring all members at once or in a sequence. Simultaneous ringing suits a small team sitting near each other; sequential ringing suits a case where one person is the primary handler and another is the backup. Write the choice into the map with a short reason so a future office manager understands why it was set that way.

Also decide what happens when a group member is already on a call. Most systems can skip busy extensions, but the behaviour should be confirmed during handover rather than assumed. If your office also manages door access, keep the same people-focused thinking: smart lock door access works best when user permissions and backup entry are planned properly, and call groups work best when membership reflects who is actually present. Neither system improves simply by adding more names to a list.

Write the No-Answer and Busy Paths Explicitly

The no-answer path is where many small office plans become vague. Someone says the call will go to voicemail, but nobody decides which mailbox, who checks it, or how quickly. Put these details on the map as a short written rule beside each arrow. For example: front desk unanswered after two ring cycles, divert to shared mailbox, office manager checks twice daily on working days.

Busy paths deserve the same treatment. If both accounts extensions are on calls, should the third caller wait in a queue, divert to the front desk, or hear a message? Each option has a cost. A queue keeps the caller in line but may frustrate someone with a quick question. Diverting to the front desk spreads the load but may send billing questions to people who cannot answer them. Choose deliberately and note the reason.

Keep the rules few enough to remember. A map with fifteen branching conditions will not survive a busy Monday. Three or four clear rules per line are usually enough for a small office, and they are easier to test during acceptance. If a rule cannot be explained in one sentence to a colleague, simplify it before installation rather than after.

Plan After-Hours Messages and Escalation

After-hours behaviour should be decided by the business, not left to the installer's default. Start by listing your actual opening hours, including lunch closures and Saturday arrangements if they differ. Then decide what a caller should hear outside those hours. A short message naming the business, stating the hours, and offering an alternative contact route is usually more useful than a generic greeting.

Consider whether any calls need to reach a person outside hours. A small office may want an emergency path for facilities issues, but that path should be named and limited. If you create an after-hours escalation, write down who is on the list, in what order, and how the list is updated when someone leaves. An outdated escalation list is a common source of frustration, and it is entirely avoidable with a dated document.

Recorded messages should be reviewed by someone who speaks for the business, because the wording becomes part of how callers experience the office. Keep the script short, avoid promising response times you cannot meet, and re-record when hours change. Store the approved script with the call-flow map so the two stay consistent.

Use a Staff Acceptance Checklist Before Sign-Off

Acceptance is the point where the plan meets reality. Build a checklist of scenarios and have staff walk through each one using real handsets. The checklist below is an author heuristic for structuring that session, not a certified testing standard. Score each item as pass, adjust or fail, and record who tested it.

Example checklist items: a call to the main line reaches the front desk within the agreed ring count; an unanswered main-line call reaches the reception group; an unanswered group call reaches the recorded message and shared mailbox; the mailbox is accessible to the named person; the accounts line rings both accounts extensions; a busy accounts line follows the chosen rule; internal calls connect directly; the after-hours message plays outside opening hours; the escalation list matches the current staff roster; and every staff member can explain what to do when a caller asks for someone who is out.

Run the session during a normal working day so the test reflects real conditions. Note any item that needs adjustment and agree a date to retest. Keep the signed checklist with the call-flow map. When staff change or hours shift, the checklist gives you a quick way to confirm the routing still matches the business.

Keep the Map Alive After Handover

A call-flow map is only useful if it stays current. Assign one person to own it, review it at a set interval such as every six months, and update it whenever a staff member joins, leaves or changes hours. Small changes accumulate quietly, and a map that is a year out of date can mislead a new colleague more than having no map at all.

Store the map, the recorded message script, the escalation list and the acceptance checklist together in one folder, digital or physical. Label each document with a date and a version note. When you brief a supplier about changes, you can then describe the current state accurately rather than reconstructing it from memory.

If your office is adding other systems at the same time, keep the documentation habit consistent across them. A supplier handling camera placement, recording and remote viewing will also expect a clear brief, and the same principle applies to a keyphone plan: describe what the business needs, agree the rules, test them with staff, and keep the record. For teams in Sarawak planning several premises systems together, it helps to treat each one as its own small project with its own map and checklist, even when the same people are involved in all of them.
