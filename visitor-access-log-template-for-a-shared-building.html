Visitor Access Log Template for a Shared Building: A Practical Guide for Sarawak Premises Managers

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A working guide and template for recording visitor details, host, time in and out, and purpose in a shared building, with notes on linking the paper log to an access control system and planning the handover.

Why a shared building needs a different visitor log

A single-tenant office can get away with a receptionist who recognises everyone. A shared building cannot. When several tenants, a management office, cleaners, contractors and delivery riders all use the same lobby, lift and stairwell, the front desk becomes the only place where the building's visitor record is created. If that record is thin, the management office has no way to answer a simple question later: who was in the building on a given afternoon, and who were they visiting?

The purpose of this guide is to give premises managers in Sarawak a template they can adapt, plus the decisions that make the template work in practice. It is written for ordinary commercial buildings, not high-security facilities. The template is deliberately plain: visitor name, company, host name and unit, purpose of visit, time in, time out, and the staff member who admitted them. Everything else is optional.

One design rule matters more than the rest: the log should be quick enough to complete at a busy counter. A form that takes two minutes per visitor will be skipped during a lunchtime rush. A form that takes twenty seconds will survive. Keep the mandatory fields short and put the rest in a notes column.

Where the building already has electronic door control, the paper log and the system should not duplicate each other. The log records intent and identity; the system records which door opened and when. Together they answer different questions, and neither replaces the other.

The template: fields, and what each one is for

Use the following columns as a starting point. Adjust the labels to match how your building actually works, but resist adding columns that nobody will fill in.

Visitor name. Full name as shown on an identity document. This is the anchor for every other entry.

Company or organisation. Useful for contractors and suppliers; leave blank for personal guests.

Host name and unit. The tenant or staff member being visited. Without this, the log cannot be reconciled with tenant records.

Purpose of visit. A short phrase: meeting, delivery, maintenance, interview, viewing. Avoid free-text essays.

Time in and time out. Record both. A log with only an entry time cannot show whether a visitor is still inside.

Badge or pass number. If the building issues temporary passes, record the number so the pass can be traced if it is not returned.

Admitted by. The name of the guard or receptionist on duty. This creates accountability without implying blame.

Notes. Anything unusual: a visitor who arrived without an appointment, a delivery left at the counter, a contractor escorted to a plant room.

A worked fictional example helps. A courier arrives at 10:15 to deliver a parcel to Unit 7-2. The guard records the name, the courier company, the host unit, purpose as delivery, time in 10:15, and notes that the parcel was left at the counter because the tenant was out. At 10:22 the guard records time out. That single line answers a later query about a missing parcel without anyone having to guess.

Keep the log on a clipboard or in a bound book at the counter. Loose sheets go missing. If the building uses a digital form on a tablet, make sure it works offline, because a lobby tablet that depends on a weak connection will fail at the worst moment.

Deciding what the log should not contain

A visitor log is a record of movement, not a filing cabinet. The more sensitive data it holds, the more carefully it has to be stored, and the more damage a lost clipboard can do.

Avoid recording identity document numbers in full. If a building policy requires an identity check, record only that a check was done, not the number itself. Avoid recording vehicle plate numbers unless the building has a car park with its own access record. Avoid health information, and avoid any note about why a visitor needed assistance.

Set a retention period and write it on the cover of the log. A common approach is to keep completed logs for a fixed period, then dispose of them securely. The exact period is a management decision, not a legal instruction, and it should be agreed with the building owner or management committee rather than assumed.

Decide who may read the log. The guard on duty, the building manager, and the tenant whose visitor is recorded are the obvious readers. A cleaner or a contractor should not be able to flip through it. If the log is digital, apply the same rule through user accounts rather than trusting everyone with the master password.

Finally, tell visitors what the log is for. A short notice at the counter, in plain language, removes most of the friction at the point of signing.

Linking the paper log to an access control system

Many shared buildings run a paper log at the counter and electronic door control at the lobby, lift or car park. The two should be designed together, because the handoff between them is where records usually break down.

An access control system records which credential opened which door at what time. It does not record why a person was there or who they were visiting. The visitor log supplies that context. When a manager later needs to reconstruct an afternoon, the two records are read side by side: the log shows the visitor and host, the system shows the door events.

This is also where the choice of hardware matters. A smart lock door access setup can suit a single controlled door, and the brand's own review notes that smart locks work best when the door condition, user roles and backup method are reviewed before installation, and that for business use it is worth checking whether a standalone smart lock or an access-control panel is more appropriate. That is a sensible framing for a shared building: a standalone lock on a store room is a different decision from a panel controlling a lobby door with many users.

A short worksheet: test the template before you print it

Before committing a template to a hundred printed sheets, run it against three fictional scenarios and see whether the columns hold up. This is an author heuristic, not a formal audit method, but it catches most design gaps in ten minutes.

Scenario one: a delivery rider arrives during a lunchtime rush, does not want to sign anything, and the tenant is not answering the phone. Does the template let the guard record the visit quickly and note that the item was left at the counter? If the form demands a host signature, the guard will improvise.

Scenario two: a contractor arrives to service an air-conditioning unit on the fifth floor and will move between floors for two hours. Does the template have a way to record an escort and a badge number, and a time out that reflects when the contractor actually left?

Scenario three: a visitor signs in at 2:00 and the log shows no time out at the end of the day. What is the procedure? Someone should check the building, and the log should carry a note explaining the gap.

Score each scenario from one to three on speed, completeness and clarity. If any scenario scores one on speed, simplify the form. If any scores one on completeness, add a single field rather than a paragraph. Record the date of the test and who ran it, so the next manager knows the template was reviewed rather than inherited.

Keep the worksheet with the log. It is the evidence that the form was designed for this building, not copied from another one.

Handover: keeping the log useful when managers change

Visitor logs outlive the people who designed them. A building manager who leaves takes the unwritten rules with them: which tenants always sign for their own guests, which contractor is allowed to skip the counter, where the completed logs are stored. Write those rules down.

The handover pack should contain the current template, a completed example, the retention period and disposal method, the list of people allowed to read the log, and a short note on how the log relates to the door system. If the building has electronic access records, add the name of whoever administers user accounts and how a lost credential is revoked.

Where the building also runs CCTV security systems, the handover should note how footage requests are handled, because a visitor query often turns into a request to check a camera. The brand's site survey, camera placement, NVR setup and remote viewing services describe the kind of planning that keeps those records usable, but the log itself should simply state who to ask.

Run a short review each time a tenant moves out or a new tenant moves in. The host list changes, and a log that still names a departed tenant as a host will confuse the guard on the next shift. A fifteen-minute review at handover is cheaper than reconstructing a week of visits from memory.

Common gaps and how to close them

Most visitor logs fail in predictable ways. The gaps below are the ones worth checking before the next busy week.

No time out. The single most common gap. Make the time-out column impossible to miss, and have the guard do a quick sweep of open entries at shift change.

Illegible names. Ask visitors to print, and have the guard read the name back. A log full of unreadable scrawl is not a record.

Host not recorded. Without a host, the entry cannot be matched to a tenant. Make the host field mandatory and let the guard fill it in from the tenant directory.

Log stored in the open. A clipboard left on a counter is readable by anyone who walks past. Keep completed pages in a locked drawer or a restricted folder.

No link to the door system. If the building has electronic access, agree which events are captured and which are left to the paper log. Duplicating everything wastes effort; capturing nothing leaves gaps.

No review. A log that is never read is a ritual. A monthly glance at a sample of pages, checking for missing time-outs and blank hosts, keeps the standard visible without turning the counter into an inspection point.

The template is a tool, not a policy. Its value comes from the decisions around it: what is recorded, who reads it, how long it is kept, and how it connects to the building's other security arrangements.
