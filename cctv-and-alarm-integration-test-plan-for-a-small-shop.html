CCTV and Alarm Integration Test Plan for a Small Shop

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical test plan for Sarawak shop managers who need to confirm that CCTV and burglar alarm systems work together, including camera triggering on alarm, recording checks and a signed handover record.

What integration testing actually proves

A CCTV system and a burglar alarm system are usually bought as two separate decisions, often from the same supplier but commissioned by different technicians on different days. Integration testing is the step that confirms the two systems behave as one when something happens: an alarm zone is triggered, and the cameras record, mark or notify as agreed.

It helps to be clear about what this test does not do. It does not prove that the alarm will stop a break-in, and it does not prove that footage will be usable in every lighting condition. It proves that the wiring, configuration and handover instructions match what was agreed in writing. Treat it as an acceptance exercise, not a security guarantee.

For a small shop, the practical scope is narrow. You are checking a handful of zones, a handful of cameras, one recorder and one notification path. That narrowness is an advantage: you can run the whole sequence in a single session with the installer present and record the result on one page.

Agree the integration behaviour before the test day

The most common reason an integration test becomes an argument is that nobody wrote down what "working together" means. Before the test day, produce a short behaviour list and have both sides initial it. Keep each line concrete and observable.

A workable list for a small shop might include: which alarm zones should cause which cameras to record or flag; whether the recorder should send a notification, and to whom; how long the flagged recording is kept compared with ordinary recording; and what happens to the cameras when the alarm is disarmed. Add a line for the case where the alarm is triggered while the shop is open and staff are present, because that is a different scenario from an after-hours trigger.

Decide in advance who is allowed to run the test. Triggering an alarm may sound a siren, may notify a monitoring contact, and may disturb neighbouring units. If the shop is in a row of lots, tell the neighbours the time window. If a response service is connected, confirm how to place the system in test mode so a real response is not dispatched.

This is also the point to confirm the camera placement decisions that the alarm depends on. A camera aimed at the shutter is useful for the alarm trigger only if the trigger zone and the camera view actually overlap. Walk the shop with the zone list and the camera positions side by side, and mark any zone that has no useful camera coverage.

The test sequence, step by step

Run the sequence in a fixed order so that a failure at one step does not confuse the next. The following is an author's suggested sequence, not a standard or a certification.

Step one: record the baseline. Note the date, time, recorder clock and the normal recording state of each camera. A recorder clock that is wrong will make every later search harder, so check it first. Step two: test each alarm zone on its own. Trigger one zone, note the time, and confirm which cameras flagged or recorded. Reset, wait, then move to the next zone. Do not trigger two zones at once during acceptance. Step three: confirm the notification path. If the agreement includes an alert to a phone or a monitoring contact, confirm it arrived, and note how long it took. If remote viewing is part of the setup, open the viewing app at this point and confirm the flagged event is visible there. Step four: confirm retention. Ask the technician to show where the flagged recording is stored and how it is distinguished from ordinary footage. Confirm the retention period matches the behaviour list. Step five: test the disarmed state. Disarm the alarm and confirm the cameras return to their normal schedule without leaving the recorder in a test mode. Step six: test recovery. Ask what happens if the network drops or the recorder restarts, and have the technician demonstrate the expected behaviour rather than describe it.

A one-page results sheet you can sign

Keep the record short enough that it actually gets completed. A table with one row per zone and columns for zone name, trigger time, camera or cameras that responded, notification received, retention confirmed and pass or fail is enough for most small shops.

Add a second short block for the system-level checks: recorder clock correct, remote viewing confirmed on the manager's device, disarmed behaviour confirmed, recovery behaviour demonstrated, and the name of the person who holds the administrator account. That last item matters more than it looks. If the installer keeps the only administrator login, the shop cannot add a user when a supervisor changes.

Finish with three lines: tested by, witnessed by, and date. A witnessed test is harder to dispute later than a verbal assurance. Keep a copy with the alarm documentation and a copy with the CCTV handover folder, because the two systems may be serviced by different people over time.

If a row fails, do not sign it as passed with a verbal promise to fix it later. Mark it failed, note the agreed remedy and the date, and treat the sheet as open until the row is retested.

Where integration tests usually go wrong

A few failure patterns are worth watching for, and none of them require a large shop to encounter.

The first is a test run only in daylight with the shutters open. An alarm trigger at night, with the interior lights off, may produce footage that is technically recorded but not useful. If the shop has a night lighting pattern, ask for at least one trigger to be repeated under those conditions, or note explicitly that night behaviour was not tested.

The second is a test that confirms the siren but never confirms the recording. The audible part of an alarm is obvious; the recording flag is quiet. Insist on seeing the flagged event in the recorder's playback, not just hearing that it should be there.

The third is a change made after the test. If the technician adjusts a zone, moves a camera or changes the recording schedule after the sheet is signed, the sheet no longer describes the system. Ask for any post-test change to be noted on the same sheet with a new date.

The fourth is an unclear boundary between the two trades. If the alarm and the cameras were supplied under one project, the integration check belongs in that project's acceptance. If they were supplied separately, agree in writing which party is responsible for confirming the link, because each may reasonably assume the other has done it.

Planning the wider shop security project around the test

The integration test is easier to run when the surrounding project is sequenced sensibly. A site survey before quotation gives the installer the chance to note where alarm sensors and camera positions will sit relative to each other, and it gives you a chance to flag stock, signage or shelving that will block a view. Camera placement decided on the day of installation is camera placement decided under time pressure.

If the shop also has a gate, a door access decision or a keyphone at the counter, keep those items on a separate acceptance sheet with their own test sequence. Mixing every system into one long test day tends to produce a signature at the end of a tiring afternoon rather than a genuine check of each item. The same applies to a smart lock door access decision: the door condition, user roles and backup entry method should be reviewed before installation, and the acceptance test should confirm the backup method works, not just the convenient one.

For the CCTV side, the supplier's own installation service is the natural place to raise the integration question early, so that the behaviour list is agreed before the technician arrives rather than negotiated on the day. For the alarm side, the equivalent conversation belongs with the alarm installation scope, including the zone map and the keyholder list.

Finally, set a review date. A short retest three to six months after handover, or after any change of manager, catches the drift that happens when codes change, cameras are adjusted and nobody updates the sheet.

A short pre-test checklist

Before the technician arrives, confirm the following. The behaviour list is written and initialled. The zone names match the shop's rooms and doors. The test time window has been agreed and neighbours informed if a siren will sound. The monitoring contact, if any, knows a test is happening. The manager's phone is ready for any notification and remote viewing checks. The person who will hold the administrator account is present. A printed results sheet and a pen are on the counter.

During the test, keep to one zone at a time, watch the playback rather than accepting a description, and write down times as they happen rather than reconstructing them afterwards. After the test, file the signed sheet with both the alarm and CCTV documentation, note any open defects with a retest date, and record who holds which login.

None of this requires specialist knowledge. It requires a written list, a fixed sequence and a signature. That is usually enough to turn a vague assurance that the systems talk to each other into a record you can rely on when a staff member changes or a question arises months later.
