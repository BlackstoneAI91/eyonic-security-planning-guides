Ask How a CCTV Quote Describes a Weather-Exposed Camera Location

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A premises manager's guide to reading how a CCTV quote handles weather-exposed camera positions in Sarawak, with questions on assumptions, maintenance access and the tests an installer will evidence.

Why the weather-exposed camera line deserves its own question

A CCTV quote is usually read from the summary page inward: number of cameras, recorder, cabling, total. The line that describes a camera mounted where rain, sun and wind reach it directly is easy to skim past, because it often looks like every other camera line. That is the problem. A position under a roof overhang and a position on an open perimeter wall are different commitments, and the quote should say which one it is describing.

Sarawak conditions make this practical rather than theoretical. Long wet periods, strong afternoon sun on some elevations, and humid air all shape how a mounting position behaves over a year. You do not need to select hardware to ask a useful question. You need to know whether the person quoting has thought about that position as an exposed one, and whether the written scope reflects it.

Question one: does the quote name the position or just the camera?

Start with the simplest test. Read each camera line and ask whether it tells you where the camera goes in terms you could point to on a wall. "Camera 4 — rear perimeter" is weaker than "Camera 4 — rear perimeter wall, open to sky, above the loading area." The second version tells you the position is exposed, which is the fact you need.

A useful quote, in my view as a reading heuristic rather than an industry standard, should let a person who was not at the survey find the position. If the quote only lists quantities and a lump sum, ask for a marked drawing or a position list before you compare prices. Comparing two quotes where one names exposed positions and the other does not is not a like-for-like comparison.

Ask directly: which of the quoted positions are fully open to weather, and which sit under cover? Then ask that the answer be written into the quote or its attachment. A verbal answer at the counter is easy to lose when the job is handed to a different crew. The written version becomes the reference for the rest of the project, including any later dispute about what was agreed.

Question two: what assumptions sit behind the exposed position?

Every quote rests on assumptions, and exposed positions carry more of them. The surveyor assumes a mounting surface exists at a certain height, that a cable route can reach it, and that the position gives the view the brief asked for. On an open wall or a pole at the edge of a compound, each assumption is less certain than it would be inside a corridor.

Ask the supplier to list the assumptions attached to each weather-exposed position. Useful prompts include: what surface is the camera expected to fix to, is that surface part of the building you control, and does the view depend on something that could change, such as a future extension, a new sign or a neighbour's structure? You are not asking for engineering detail. You are asking what the price depends on.

A site survey is the normal place for these assumptions to be tested, and Eyonic's published scope lists site survey among its services. If a quote arrives without any record of a survey, ask when one will happen and whether the exposed positions will be confirmed on site before work is scheduled. A quote built on a survey is easier to hold to than one built on a phone description.

Question three: how will maintenance reach that position?

This is the question most often left until something needs attention. A camera on an open wall may be perfectly reachable from a walkway, or it may sit above a slope, a roof edge or a stack of stored goods. The quote should tell you how a technician is expected to get to it, because access affects how quickly any future visit can be done.

Ask what access the installer assumes: a ladder from ground level, a walkable roof area, a lift, or a route through a tenant's space. Then ask who provides that access on the day. If the route crosses another occupier's area, note it in the quote discussion rather than discovering it later. For a shop or office, also ask whether access needs to happen outside trading hours, and whether that changes the plan.

Keep this at the level of arrangements, not method. You are recording who opens which door and when, not how the work is performed. A short written note in the quote — "rear wall position reached via service lane, access arranged by premises manager" — is enough to prevent a wasted visit. If the answer is vague, treat the position as unresolved and ask for it to be settled before you approve.

Question four: which tests will be evidenced, and how?

A quote describes intent. Acceptance describes what actually happened. For weather-exposed positions, ask in advance which checks the installer will demonstrate and what record you will receive. This keeps the conversation practical and gives you something to sign against.

Reasonable things to ask about, phrased as questions rather than instructions: will the live view from each exposed position be shown to you at handover, will recorded playback from those cameras be demonstrated, and will remote viewing be tested on a device you nominate? Eyonic's published scope includes recording, remote viewing and NVR setup, so these are fair topics to raise when discussing what handover will cover. Ask for the demonstration to happen at a time when the scene in front of each camera is representative — daylight for an outdoor wall, for example.

Write the answers into the quote response as a short acceptance list. A simple example: three exposed positions, each with a live view check, a playback check and a remote viewing check, signed by the premises manager on the day. That list is your evidence trail, and it costs nothing to agree before work starts.

Question five: what changes if the position turns out to be unsuitable?

Sometimes the survey or the first day on site shows that an exposed position will not give the view the brief assumed. The quote should tell you how that is handled before it happens, so the decision is a process rather than an argument.

Ask what the supplier's normal step is when a quoted position proves unsuitable: a written variation, a substituted position agreed on site, or a pause until you approve. Ask who signs that change and whether the price is affected. You do not need a fixed answer for every scenario; you need to know the route. A quote that says nothing about change leaves the decision to whoever is on the ladder.

This matters most where the exposed position is the one covering an entrance, a gate or a boundary. If that view is the reason the project exists, a silent substitution is a real loss. Note in your own file which positions are essential and which are desirable, and share that ranking with the supplier when you discuss the quote. It gives both sides a clear order of priority when a position has to move.

A short worksheet for comparing two quotes

Use the same five questions against every quote you receive, and record answers in a single table so differences are visible. Columns: position reference, exposed or covered, assumption noted, maintenance access noted, tests to be evidenced, change route noted. A tick means the quote or its attachment says something specific; a blank means you need to ask.

Score nothing. The purpose is to see which quote has done the thinking, not to rank suppliers on a number. In my experience of reading documents of this kind, the quote that names positions and lists assumptions is usually the easier one to manage, because the conversation has already happened on paper.

Two closing points. First, keep the approved quote, the position list and the acceptance record together; they are the project's memory when staff change. Second, if you are planning a wider security project, the same discipline applies to other systems — the published scope from Eyonic covers CCTV security systems alongside related work such as access control and alarms, and each deserves its own written assumptions. For the CCTV installation itself, ask the questions above before you approve, and ask for the answers in writing.
