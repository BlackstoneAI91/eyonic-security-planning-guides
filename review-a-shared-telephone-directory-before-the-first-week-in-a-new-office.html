Reviewing a Shared Telephone Directory Before Your First Week in a New Office

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical guide for Sarawak premises managers on checking a shared telephone directory before opening a new office, covering public-facing and internal entries, change ownership, and feedback collection without exposing staff numbers.

Why the directory deserves a review before day one

A shared telephone directory is one of the few office documents that reaches both the public and every member of staff. Before the first week in a new office, it is worth treating it as a controlled document rather than a list someone typed in a hurry. The review has three aims: make sure callers reach the right place, make sure internal staff can find each other, and make sure nobody's personal number ends up somewhere it should not.

Start by separating the directory into two layers. The public layer is what appears on a website, a signboard listing, or a printed handout. The internal layer is the extension list, the after-hours contact sheet, and any shared document that staff open on their first morning. Each layer has a different audience and a different risk if it is wrong. A public entry that still shows a previous tenant's line misdirects customers; an internal entry that lists a personal mobile number creates a privacy problem that is hard to undo once the file has been shared.

Decide who owns the review before you start. One named person should hold the master copy, and that person should be the only one who publishes changes. If several people edit the same file, you will spend the first week reconciling versions instead of settling in. A short written note naming the owner, the review date, and where the master copy lives is enough to begin. This is a documentation task, not a technical one, and it can be completed before any equipment is switched on.

Checking the public-facing entries independently

Public entries are the ones you cannot easily correct once they are in circulation. Before the first week, ask someone who was not involved in drafting the directory to test each public entry. That person should call the main line, follow the recorded message or the reception answer, and note whether they reached the department the directory promised. A second check should confirm that the address, opening hours, and department names match what the office actually intends to publish.

Keep the test simple and record the result in a table with four columns: entry as published, what the caller expected, what actually happened, and who will fix it. Do not ask the tester to judge whether the number is "correct" in some abstract sense; ask only whether the call reached the intended place. This keeps the review factual and avoids arguments about wording.

Where the office uses a keyphone system, the public entry usually points to a main number that routes callers onward. The directory should describe that routing in plain language so a caller knows what to expect, for example that they will hear a menu or be answered by reception. If the office is planning a new or changed phone setup, the supplier will need a clear picture of how callers should reach each department. Auditing the internal extension list without exposing numbers

The internal list is where privacy mistakes happen most often. A shared spreadsheet that lists every extension alongside a personal mobile number can be copied, forwarded, or left open on a screen. Before the first week, decide which fields the internal directory actually needs. In most offices that is a name, a role, a desk or department, and an extension. Personal mobile numbers belong in a separate, restricted contact record held by the person who manages emergencies, not in the general directory.

Run a short audit with three passes. First, check that every desk the office plans to occupy has an entry and that no entry refers to a desk that no longer exists. Second, check that each entry names a role as well as a person, so the directory still works when someone is away. Third, check that the file's sharing settings match the audience; a document meant for staff should not be open to anyone with the link.

Record the audit as a checklist with a date and an owner. A simple scoring approach, such as marking each row complete, incomplete, or not applicable, is an author heuristic rather than a formal standard, but it gives the reviewer something concrete to sign. Keep the completed checklist with the master copy so the next review starts from a known position rather than from scratch.

Who handles changes, and how to keep one master copy

A directory fails quietly when nobody owns changes. Before the first week, write a one-page change procedure that answers four questions: who may request a change, who approves it, who edits the master copy, and how staff are told the change has happened. Keep the procedure short enough to fit on a single sheet and post it where the directory itself is stored.

A practical pattern is to separate the request from the edit. Anyone may request a change through a named channel, but only the directory owner edits the master file. The owner records each change with a date and a short reason, so a later reviewer can see why an entry looks the way it does. This is the same discipline that keeps other office documents trustworthy, and it costs very little once the habit is set.

Decide in advance how often the directory will be reviewed. A quarterly check is a common starting point, with an extra review whenever the office moves, a department is renamed, or a batch of new staff arrives. Put the next review date in the document header so it is visible to everyone who opens the file. If the office is also planning related systems, such as an access control system or a burglar alarm system, note in the same procedure who holds the contact records for those systems, so the directory and the security records do not drift apart.

Collecting feedback without sharing actual staff numbers

Feedback on a directory is easy to collect badly. If you circulate a copy of the directory and ask colleagues to mark errors, you have just created another uncontrolled copy. Instead, collect feedback against a numbered list of entries, or against role names rather than personal details. Ask reviewers to report the row number and the problem, not to paste the entry into an email.

A short feedback form works well. It asks for the row reference, the type of problem (wrong department, missing entry, outdated role, privacy concern), and the reviewer's suggested correction. The owner then applies approved changes to the master copy and confirms back to the reviewer. This keeps personal numbers out of inboxes and chat threads while still capturing the information you need.

Set a closing date for first-week feedback, for example the end of the second week, and treat anything received after that as a normal change request. Summarise the feedback in a short note: how many items were raised, how many were resolved, and which ones need a decision from a manager. That summary is useful evidence that the review happened, and it gives the next reviewer a starting point. Avoid publishing the raw feedback anywhere shared, since it may contain details that should stay with the owner.

A first-week review worksheet you can reuse

The worksheet below is an example structure, not a mandated form. Adapt the columns to the size of the office. Its purpose is to turn the review into a repeatable routine rather than a one-off scramble.

Section one, public entries: list each published entry, the tester's name, the date tested, the outcome, and the fix owner. Section two, internal entries: list each desk or role, whether an entry exists, whether a role is named, and whether any personal number appears. Section three, access and sharing: record where the master copy lives, who can edit it, and when the sharing settings were last checked. Section four, changes: record each request, its approval, the edit date, and how staff were notified. Section five, next review: record the date and the named owner.

Keep the completed worksheet with the master copy and give a copy to whoever manages the office's other systems. If the office is also commissioning CCTV security systems, the same owner can confirm that the directory lists the right role for after-hours contact, without publishing any individual's number. Handing the reviewed directory to the next owner

The final step is a handover, even if the next owner is simply you in three months. Write a short handover note that states where the master copy lives, who may edit it, when it was last reviewed, and which entries are still open. Attach the completed worksheet and the change procedure. This note is the difference between a directory that stays accurate and one that quietly rots after the first week.

If the office is moving into a shared building, confirm with the building manager which entries the landlord controls and which the tenant controls. A shared reception line, a building-wide after-hours number, or a landlord-supplied keyphone system may sit outside your master copy, and the handover note should say so plainly. Do not assume the landlord will update your internal list, and do not publish the landlord's staff numbers in your own directory without agreement.

Finally, schedule the first review before the office opens and the second one for a fixed date afterwards. A directory reviewed once and then forgotten is only slightly better than one never reviewed at all. The routine described here, with a named owner, a numbered feedback form, and a dated worksheet, is designed to survive staff changes and office moves. It asks for no special tools and no technical knowledge, only a decision about who is responsible and a habit of checking before publishing.
