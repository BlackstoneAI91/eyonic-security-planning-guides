NVR Setup Handover Questions for a Small Site

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical question sheet for Sarawak premises managers accepting an NVR handover: recording schedule, retention days, user accounts, playback steps and what to do when a camera goes offline.

Why the handover conversation matters more than the box

An NVR is the part of a CCTV security systems project that keeps working quietly after the installers leave. The cameras are visible, so people remember them. The recorder sits in a cabinet or back office, and its settings are easy to accept without checking. Months later, when someone asks for footage from a particular evening, the answer depends on decisions made in a short handover meeting.

This sheet is written for a premises manager in Sarawak who is about to sign off an ordinary small-site system: a shop, an office floor, a workshop or a small warehouse. It is not a technical manual and it does not replace the installer's own documentation. It is a list of questions to ask while the person who configured the system is still on site and can show you the answers on screen.

Treat the handover as a walkthrough, not a briefing. Ask for each item to be demonstrated rather than described. If a setting cannot be shown, write down that it was not shown. A short written record of what was demonstrated, dated and signed by both sides, is more useful than a folder of brochures.

Recording schedule: what is recorded, and when

Start with the recording plan, because everything else depends on it. Ask the installer to open the recording settings and show you, camera by camera, whether recording runs continuously, on motion, or on a schedule. Then ask why each camera is set that way.

Useful questions to bring:

Which cameras record continuously and which record only on motion or alarm triggers? What happens outside business hours — is the schedule different, and who decided that? If motion recording is used, what area of the image triggers it, and what is deliberately excluded? Are any cameras set to a lower resolution or frame rate than others, and what is the trade-off? Does the recording schedule match the hours the premises is actually occupied, including any shift patterns?

Write the answers as a simple table: camera name, location, recording mode, and the reason. A camera labelled only by a number is hard to discuss later. Ask for names that match the site, such as front entrance, counter, back door or loading area.

This is also the moment to connect the recorder plan to camera placement. A camera aimed at a spot that no longer holds stock, or blocked by a new sign, records something unhelpful no matter how the NVR is configured. If the handover reveals a view that does not match the site survey, note it as an open item rather than accepting it silently.

Retention: how many days of footage you actually have

Retention is the question most often answered vaguely. "About a month" is not an answer you can plan around. Ask for the number of days the system is configured to keep, and ask to see where that figure is set.

Questions to ask:

How many days of recording are kept before the oldest footage is overwritten? Is that figure the same for every camera, or do some cameras keep longer than others? What happens to retention if a camera is switched to continuous recording later? Has retention been checked against the actual disk space, or is it an estimate? Who is responsible for telling management if retention drops — for example after a settings change or a disk fault?

Ask for a demonstration: open playback, jump back to the oldest available day, and confirm the date shown. That single check tells you more than a verbal promise. Record the date you checked and the oldest footage available on that day.

Decide, as a manager, whether that window is long enough for your own review cycle. If your organisation reviews incidents weekly or monthly, a retention period shorter than that cycle creates a gap. This is a business decision, not a technical one, and it belongs in your notes with a named owner.

User accounts: who can see, export and change settings

Access to recordings is a governance question as much as a technical one. Ask the installer to list every account on the system, what role each has, and who holds the credentials.

Bring these questions:

How many user accounts exist, and which are administrator accounts? Who holds each account, by role rather than by name if the list will be shared widely? Can a standard user view live footage but not export clips, or is everyone an administrator? How is a leaver's account removed, and who is authorised to do it? Are accounts shared between staff, and if so, why?

Ask for the account list to be printed or written into the handover record, with the date. Then agree a simple rule: new accounts are created only on a named manager's approval, and removed when someone leaves. Keep that rule in the same document as the account list so the two stay together.

If the site also uses smart lock door access or an access control system, ask whether door events and camera footage are reviewed by the same people. Where they are, the account rules should match, so that one person's departure does not leave access open in one system and closed in another.

Playback and export: can you find a clip without help?

The real test of a handover is whether a manager can retrieve footage unaided. Ask the installer to step back and let you do it, using only the notes you have been given.

A simple exercise: choose a recent time, find the camera covering the main entrance, play the footage, pause on a moment, and export a short clip to a location you can find again. Then repeat the search using the mobile app if remote CCTV viewing is part of the system.

Questions to ask along the way:

What are the exact steps to search by date and time, written down in order? Where do exported clips go, and how are they named? Who is allowed to export, and where should exported files be stored? How long are exported files kept, and who deletes them? If remote viewing is enabled, which devices are authorised, and how is a lost phone handled?

Write the playback steps as a short numbered list and keep it with the handover record. A list written by the person doing the task, not copied from a manual, is usually the one that gets used. If remote viewing is configured, confirm that it was tested from outside the premises and note the date of that test.

When a camera goes offline: the first-response sheet

Every system will eventually show a camera as offline, a recording gap, or a disk warning. The handover should leave you with a first-response routine that does not require guesswork.

Ask these questions and write the answers down:

How does the system show that a camera is offline — on the recorder screen, in the mobile app, or both? Who is expected to notice, and how often is the system checked? What is the first thing staff should do, and what should they not touch? Who is contacted, and during which hours? What information should be noted before calling — camera name, time noticed, and what the screen showed?

Keep the routine short and non-technical. The aim is to record the fault accurately and hand it to the right person, not to encourage staff to reconfigure equipment. A simple log with columns for date, camera, symptom, who was told and when it was resolved gives you a history that is useful at the next service visit.

Pair this with a monthly check: open the recorder, confirm every camera shows a live image, and confirm the oldest available footage date. Note the result. That five-minute habit turns an offline camera from a surprise into a dated entry.

A one-page handover worksheet

Bring this sheet to the handover meeting and fill it in as you go. It is an example format, not a standard, and you should adapt the columns to your site.

Section A — Recording. Camera name and location; recording mode; reason for the mode; who approved the schedule. Aim for one row per camera.

Section B — Retention. Configured retention in days; oldest footage date checked on the day; who monitors retention; review cycle of the business.

Section C — Accounts. Number of administrator accounts; number of standard accounts; who approves new accounts; who removes leavers' accounts; date the list was last confirmed.

Section D — Playback. Numbered steps to find footage by date and time; where exports are stored; who may export; remote viewing devices authorised and date last tested.

Section E — Faults. How an offline camera is displayed; who checks and how often; who is contacted; what is written in the fault log.

Section F — Sign-off. Items demonstrated on the day; items not demonstrated and why; open items with a named owner and a target date; signatures and date.

A reasonable target, as an author's suggestion rather than a rule, is to complete sections A to D during the handover visit and to close any open items within a fortnight. If a section cannot be completed because the information is not available, record that plainly. An honest gap in the worksheet is easier to manage than an assumption that turns out to be wrong.

For sites still at the planning stage, the same questions work in reverse: they tell you what to ask before CCTV installation begins, so the handover sheet is mostly filled in by the time the system is ready.
