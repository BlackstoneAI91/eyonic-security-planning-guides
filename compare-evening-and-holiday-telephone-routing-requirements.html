Compare evening and holiday telephone routing requirements

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical guide for Sarawak premises managers comparing how calls are answered, escalated and documented after normal office hours and on public holidays, using a fictional schedule and a comparison table.

Start with a written schedule, not a product

Evening and holiday routing is easier to compare when you separate the schedule from the equipment. Write down the hours your premises actually operates, then mark the periods when nobody is at the front desk: weekday evenings, weekends, public holidays and any closed days specific to your business. For each period, record who is expected to answer, who answers if the first person does not, and where a message should end up. A fictional example: a Kuching shop closes at 6pm, a duty supervisor covers calls until 9pm, and after that an answering message asks callers to ring back the next morning. That is a routing requirement in plain language. It does not name a product, and it can be handed to any supplier for comparison. Keep the schedule in one document with a version date and an owner, so that a change of manager does not silently change who is supposed to pick up the phone.

Build a comparison table around who answers

Once the schedule exists, compare proposals by behaviour rather than by feature names. A simple table with four columns works: time period, first answering role, escalation owner and message destination. Fill one row per period for each proposal you are reviewing. Ask each supplier to describe, in writing, what a caller experiences in each row. If a proposal only says that evening routing is supported, ask for the row to be completed. This is where a keyphone system proposal and a hosted alternative can be compared on the same terms, because the table asks about outcomes rather than hardware. Keep the language neutral: you are recording what the supplier says will happen, not testing it yourself. Where a supplier cannot complete a row, note it as an open question rather than assuming the gap will be covered. The completed table becomes the basis for the next conversation and for any later acceptance discussion.

Decide who escalates a missed call

Escalation is the part of evening routing that is most often left vague. Decide in advance what should happen when the first answering role does not respond within a stated time. For example, a fictional office might say that if the duty supervisor has not answered within ten minutes, the call should go to the operations manager, and if there is still no answer, the caller should hear a message with the next working day's opening time. Write the time limits as examples chosen by your team, not as industry standards. Then ask each supplier how their proposal would express that chain and whether the chain can be changed later by an authorised person. Record who is allowed to request a change and who signs it off. This keeps escalation ownership visible and prevents a situation where everyone assumes someone else is responsible for the evening phone.

Check which messages need reviewing

Holiday routing usually depends on recorded or written messages, and those messages age badly. List every message a caller might hear outside office hours and give each one an owner and a review date. A fictional example: the festive closure message names the dates the office is closed, the date it reopens and a general instruction to call back then. Ask who updates that message before each holiday, who checks it afterwards, and how a correction is requested if the dates are wrong. Keep the list short and specific. Avoid putting personal mobile numbers or staff names into a public message; a role description such as duty supervisor is easier to maintain and easier to retire. Review the list at the same time each year, and note the review in the same document as the schedule so the two stay aligned.

Separate telephone routing from the wider security brief

Premises managers often plan telephone changes alongside other security work, and it helps to keep the two conversations distinct. Telephone routing is about who answers and where messages go; a CCTV security systems project is about camera placement, recording and remote viewing, and it is planned through a site survey. If your evening arrangements depend on someone checking a screen after hours, write that dependency down as a separate requirement and raise it with the relevant supplier. Do not assume that a telephone proposal covers monitoring, or that a camera proposal covers call answering. Keeping the two briefs apart makes each one easier to compare and easier to hand over.

Use a short worksheet before you sign anything

Before committing to a routing arrangement, run a one-page worksheet with the following checks. First, the schedule has a version date and a named owner. Second, every period has a first answering role and an escalation owner. Third, every out-of-hours message has a review date. Fourth, the proposal states who may request a change and who approves it. Fifth, the handover pack includes a plain-language guide for staff who will use the arrangement. Score each check as met, partly met or not met; treat the score as an author heuristic for your own discussion, not as a certification. Where a check is not met, write the question you will put to the supplier. This worksheet is deliberately short so that it can be completed in one meeting and revisited after the first holiday period, when you will know which rows of the comparison table held up in practice.

Plan the handover and the first review

The final step is agreeing what happens after the arrangement goes live. Decide who receives the written guide, who is allowed to ask for changes, and when the first review will take place. A fictional example: the office manager holds the schedule, the duty supervisor receives a one-page summary of evening expectations, and the first review is scheduled for the month after the next public holiday. Record the review outcome in the same document as the schedule so the history stays together. If the premises also uses a queue management system or access control system, keep those handovers separate and give each its own owner and review date. The aim is a small set of documents that a new manager could pick up and understand without needing to reconstruct the evening arrangements from memory.
