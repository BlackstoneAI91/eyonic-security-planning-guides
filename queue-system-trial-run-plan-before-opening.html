Queue System Trial Run Plan Before Opening: A One-Morning Test for Sarawak Premises

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical, timed trial plan for a queue management system before a service counter opens, with hypothetical arrival volumes, a staff script, a defect log and an acceptance worksheet for premises managers in Sarawak.

Why a trial morning beats a launch-day surprise

A queue management system changes how people move through a service area: where they take a ticket, where they wait, which counter calls them and what happens when a counter closes early. Those are operational habits, not just hardware settings. If the first time staff and visitors meet the new flow is opening day, every small confusion competes with real customers and a full waiting area.

A single quiet morning before opening is usually enough to expose the practical gaps. The goal is not to prove the system is perfect. It is to find the two or three things that would embarrass the counter on day one, fix them, and write down who owns each fix. Treat the trial as a rehearsal with a script, a clock and a defect log, not as a demonstration for management.

This guide is written for premises managers in Sarawak planning ordinary security and service-counter projects. It stays at the level of planning, documentation and handover. It does not cover wiring, configuration commands or vendor-specific settings; those belong to the supplier. Where a brand is mentioned, it is as identity context only, and any brand-specific statement is drawn from public approved notes rather than from a first-hand test.

One useful framing: the trial is a procurement document as much as an operational one. What you record on trial morning becomes the acceptance evidence you refer back to if something behaves differently after go-live.

Set the trial scope and pick a realistic morning

Choose a morning when the premises is quiet but not empty. A completely empty building hides the human friction; a busy trading morning hides the defects. A short window of roughly two to three hours, before the counter normally opens, is a reasonable author heuristic. Adjust it to your own opening pattern.

Write a one-page scope before the day. It should state:

- Which counters or service points are in scope for the trial, and which are deliberately excluded. - Which ticket types will be issued, and which will be simulated on paper only. - Who is allowed to press, call, transfer or close anything during the trial. - What counts as a pass, and what counts as a defect that must be fixed before opening. - Who signs the trial record at the end.

Keep the scope small. A trial that tries to test every ticket type, every display and every report at once produces a long list of half-observations. Two or three ticket types and two counters is usually enough to see whether the flow works.

If the same premises also runs other systems, resist the temptation to test everything on the same morning. A queue trial is about people flow. A separate session for cameras, alarms or door access keeps each record clean and each owner accountable.

Hypothetical arrival volumes for the trial morning

The numbers below are fictional examples for planning, not benchmarks. Replace them with your own estimates from past counter traffic, appointment bookings or staff recollection.

Example trial profile for a small service counter with two positions:

- 08:30 to 08:45: 6 arrivals, mostly single visitors, one ticket type. - 08:45 to 09:15: 18 arrivals, mixed ticket types, one visitor with a companion. - 09:15 to 09:30: 4 arrivals, including one person who arrives without reading the signage. - 09:30 to 09:45: a deliberate quiet gap, used to test what the display shows when nobody is waiting. - 09:45 to 10:00: 8 arrivals, with one counter deliberately closed to test transfer and re-call.

Recruit colleagues or willing participants to play visitors. Give each a simple role card: first-time visitor, visitor with an appointment, visitor who cannot hear the call clearly, visitor who arrives during the quiet gap. The role cards matter more than the headcount, because they force staff to practise the awkward moments rather than the smooth ones.

Record actual arrival times against the plan. The difference between planned and actual volume is itself a finding, and it tells you whether your staffing assumption for opening week is realistic.

A short staff script for the trial

A script keeps the trial consistent and gives new staff something to fall back on. Keep it to a few lines that anyone can read aloud.

Greeting and ticket: "Welcome. Please take a ticket from the machine and keep it with you. Your number will appear on the screen and be called at the counter."

Calling: "Number 014, please come to counter two." Staff should call once clearly, pause, then repeat once before moving to the next step in their own procedure.

Transfer: "Your query is handled at counter three. I am transferring your ticket now, and your number will be called there."

Counter closing: "This counter is closing. Please take a seat; your number will be called at the next available counter."

Accessibility: "If you cannot see or hear the display clearly, please tell me and I will assist you directly."

Quiet period: agree in advance what staff should do when no one is waiting, so the trial does not drift into improvisation.

After each scripted line, note whether the visitor understood it without a second explanation. That single observation is often the most useful output of the morning.

The defect log: what to record and how to score it

Keep one defect log for the whole trial. A simple table works: time, what happened, who was affected, which part of the flow, severity, owner, and target fix date.

Use a three-level severity scale as an author heuristic, not as an industry standard:

- Blocking: the visitor cannot complete the journey, or staff cannot serve them. Must be fixed before opening. - Confusing: the journey completes but requires explanation or repetition. Fix before opening if possible; otherwise add a temporary sign or script line. - Cosmetic: wording, display position or minor timing. Can be scheduled after opening.

Record the exact words used, not a summary. "Visitor did not know where to take a ticket" is less useful than "visitor walked to counter one first, was redirected, then found the ticket machine behind the pillar." The second version tells you what to move or sign.

At the end of the morning, count the blocking and confusing items. If the blocking list is not empty, the honest decision is to delay the counter go-live or run a second trial, not to hope opening-day staff will absorb the problem.

Where the queue trial meets the rest of the premises

A queue trial rarely stands alone. The counter sits inside a building that also has cameras, doors, alarms and phones, and the trial morning is a good moment to check that the surrounding systems support the flow rather than interrupt it.

Camera placement is worth a walk-through during the trial. If the waiting area or counter is meant to be covered, confirm that the view actually shows the ticket point and the seating area rather than the back of a display. This is a planning observation, not a technical test; the supplier confirms coverage. Door access and smart locks matter if the waiting area is behind a controlled door. The approved product note is modest and worth repeating: smart locks work best when the door condition, user roles and backup method are reviewed before installation, and for business use the choice between a standalone smart lock and an access-control panel should be checked first. A queue trial is a good time to confirm that a late visitor can still reach the counter without staff abandoning their post.

Phones belong in the same rehearsal. If a visitor calls ahead, or a counter needs to escalate to a supervisor, the keyphone routing should be part of the script. Test one escalation call during the trial and note how long it takes to reach a person who can help.

For the queue side itself, the destination page for the queue management system is the natural place to compare ticket hardware, display options and counter-calling workflow against your own peak pattern. Keep that comparison separate from the trial record so each document stays readable.

Acceptance worksheet and the go-live decision

Close the trial with a short acceptance worksheet signed by the counter lead and the premises manager. It should answer five questions:

1. Did every scripted visitor complete the journey without staff improvisation? Note the exceptions. 2. Were all blocking defects logged, owned and dated? 3. Did the display and calling behaviour match what staff were told to expect? 4. Did the escalation call reach a person who could resolve the issue? 5. Is the signage at the entrance clear enough for a first-time visitor?

Score each item as pass, pass with notes, or fail. Treat the scoring as a local decision aid, not a certified assessment.

If the worksheet shows a fail on question one or two, the sensible outcome is a short second trial focused only on the failed items, not a full repeat. If everything passes, record the date, the participants and the version of the script used, so that a later change to the flow can be compared against a known baseline.

Finally, hand the defect log and worksheet to whoever owns the counter after opening. A trial that ends with a signed record and a named owner is far more useful than one that ends with a verbal "looks fine". That record is also the document you return to when a supplier asks what was tested and what was accepted.
