Set Up an Alarm Service Question Log for a Tenant Who Cannot Attend the Visit

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical guide for Sarawak premises managers on building a question log when a tenant cannot attend an alarm service visit, covering high-level observations, who may request an inspection, and what written information should follow.

Start the log with a purpose statement and named roles

Before writing a single question, open the log with one or two sentences describing what it is for. A workable example: the log exists so that the premises manager, the tenant and the service provider can discuss one alarm service visit without anyone relying on memory or a phone call. Keep that statement neutral and free of technical detail.

Next, name the roles rather than the people. Typical entries are premises manager, tenant representative, appointed service contact and provider coordinator. For each role, record what that role is allowed to do: who may request an inspection, who may approve a date window, who receives the written summary afterwards. This matters most when the tenant is away, because the person physically present is not automatically the person who decides.

Keep the log about coordination, not about equipment. The purpose statement and role table together should fit on one page; if they do not, the scope has grown beyond a single visit.

Collect high-level observations only, never control details

The tenant who cannot attend can still contribute useful context. Ask for observations a visitor would notice: which entrance is normally used, whether the premises is occupied during the proposed window, whether furniture or stock has moved since the last visit, and whether any area has changed use. Write each observation as a dated line with the role who supplied it.

Draw a firm line around what does not belong in the log. Do not record control-panel details, wiring routes, device settings, access values or anything that would let a reader operate or interfere with the burglar alarm system. If a tenant volunteers such detail, note only that a technical question exists and route it to the provider through the agreed contact.

A simple two-column layout works: observation on the left, follow-up owner on the right. A fictional example from a shoplot in Sarawak: "Rear storeroom cleared last month, now used for packing" with the follow-up owner listed as the premises manager, who will confirm whether the provider needs to see that area. The log captures what changed; it does not attempt to interpret what the change means for the system.

Clarify who can request an inspection and on what authority

When the tenant is absent, the most common friction is not the visit itself but the question of authority. Add a short section to the log headed "Who may request an inspection" and answer it in plain terms. Record whether the tenant has delegated attendance to a named role, whether the premises manager may authorise access to common areas only, and whether any area requires separate permission.

Keep this as a coordination record, not a legal document. If the lease or a shared-premises rule governs who may approve access, note that the question has been referred to the proper owner and leave the answer field open until it returns. Do not paraphrase rules you have not seen.

A useful habit is a single status column with three values: open, answered, referred. Anything marked referred should name the role it was sent to and the date. This keeps the log honest when several questions are waiting on different people, and it prevents a well-meaning staff member from treating an unanswered question as an approval.

Agree what written information should follow the visit

Decide before the visit what the tenant will receive afterwards, and write that expectation into the log. A reasonable list for an ordinary service visit includes a dated summary of what was inspected at a high level, a list of questions that remain open, and the name of the role to contact for follow-up. Nothing in that list requires technical disclosure.

Where a site survey is part of the wider project, the same principle applies: the written output should describe scope and next steps rather than settings. Eyonic's published service list includes site survey and camera placement alongside alarm and access work, which is a reminder that a single premises often has several threads running at once. Keep the alarm log separate from those threads so each has its own owner and its own written record.

Add a line to the log stating where the written summary will be filed and which role is responsible for filing it. A fictional example: the summary goes into the premises file maintained by the manager's assistant, with a copy offered to the tenant representative on return.

Use a short question worksheet with an owner and a due date

A question log only helps if each line has somewhere to go. Use a five-column worksheet: question number, question in plain words, role who can answer, date raised, status. Keep questions short enough that a reader who was not in the original conversation can understand them.

Fictional examples from a small office in Sarawak: "Can the visit window move to the following week?" owned by the tenant representative; "Which entrance should the service contact use?" owned by the premises manager; "Who receives the written summary?" owned by the provider coordinator. None of these require technical knowledge, and all of them can be answered by message if the tenant is away.

Set a review rhythm rather than a deadline promise. An author heuristic that some managers find workable is to review open lines twice before the visit window and once afterwards, but treat that as a suggestion to adapt, not a rule. Close a line only when the answer is written down; a verbal answer that nobody recorded will be lost by the time the tenant returns.

Keep the log safe and hand it over cleanly

Treat the completed log as a premises record with the same care as any other operational document. Store it where the named roles can reach it, avoid duplicating it across personal devices, and keep a single current version so nobody works from an outdated copy. If the log is shared with a provider, share only the sections relevant to the visit.

At handover, whether to the returning tenant or to a new manager, attach a one-paragraph cover note: what the visit covered, which questions were closed, which remain open and who owns each. This is the same discipline that helps when several security quotations arrive together, where a clear record of who approved what prevents later confusion.

If the premises also runs CCTV security systems or an access control system, resist the urge to merge every log into one. Separate logs with separate owners stay readable. The alarm question log should end with a short sign-off line naming the role who reviewed it and the date, so the next reader knows the record was checked rather than merely stored.

Review the log after the visit and before the next one

A question log earns its keep in the gap between visits. Shortly after the service visit, reopen the log and mark each line: answered, still open, or no longer relevant. Add a brief note on anything the tenant should know when they return, phrased as coordination rather than advice.

Then start the next log from the closed one. Carry forward only the lines that remain open, with their original dates, so the history stays visible. A fictional example: a question about which entrance the service contact should use was answered for one visit but may need revisiting if the building's arrangements change, so it is carried forward with a note to confirm rather than assumed.

This review step is also the moment to check that no sensitive detail crept in during hurried updates. If it did, remove it and note the correction. The goal is a short, current, readable record that any authorised role can pick up, whether or not the tenant is in the building.
