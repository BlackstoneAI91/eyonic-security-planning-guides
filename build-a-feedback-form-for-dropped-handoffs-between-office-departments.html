Build a Feedback Form for Dropped Handoffs Between Office Departments

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical guide for Sarawak premises managers on designing a short feedback form that records what a caller reported, which department should have owned the handoff, and how to review the pattern each month during ordinary security-system projects.

Why a dropped-handoff form is worth the effort

A dropped handoff is any enquiry that leaves one department and never clearly arrives at another. In a security-system project, that might be a caller asking about camera placement who is passed from reception to facilities and then hears nothing back. The caller rarely knows which team failed; they only know nobody replied. A short feedback form turns that vague frustration into a record you can act on. The form is not a complaints channel and not a performance review. It is a routing record. Its purpose is to capture three plain facts: what the caller said they needed, who was expected to take ownership next, and whether that ownership actually happened. Keep the form to one screen. If it takes more than two minutes to complete, staff will fill it in from memory days later, and the detail that matters will be gone. Treat the form as a small operational document that sits alongside your other project paperwork, not as a new system that needs its own training programme.

What the caller reports: the first field on the form

Start with the caller's own words, not your department's summary. A field labelled "what the caller asked for" should be filled in as close to verbatim as possible, with personal details left out. For example: "asked when someone would visit to look at camera positions for a shop in Kuching" is useful. "Wants CCTV" is not. The second field is the channel: phone, walk-in, email or a message left with a colleague. The third is the time and date the enquiry arrived, because a handoff that looks dropped after one day may simply be a weekend. Avoid asking staff to guess intent. If the caller mentioned a site survey, record that phrase; if they mentioned remote viewing on a phone, record that. This keeps the form descriptive. It also means the record stays useful when the person who took the call is on leave. Where a project involves planned work such as a site survey or camera placement discussion, the caller's phrasing often signals which stage they think they are at, and that is exactly the detail a summary tends to lose.

The expected routing owner: naming a role, not a person

The middle of the form is where most handoff records fail. Teams tend to write "passed to facilities" without saying who in facilities was expected to act. Use a role field instead of a name: reception, facilities coordinator, project administrator, or the nominated contact for the supplier. Then add a single question: who was expected to reply to the caller next? One role only. If two roles could plausibly own it, the handoff is already ambiguous, and the form should record that ambiguity rather than hide it. A short optional field for "what the caller was told to expect" is also worth including, for example "told someone would call back within two working days". That gives the monthly review something concrete to check against. Keep the form free of account numbers, access details or anything a caller would not expect to be written down. The aim is a routing trail that any authorised colleague can read, not a dossier. Where a supplier is involved, record the supplier by role and the department that holds the relationship, so the trail does not depend on one person's memory.

A worked example using an ordinary office project

Consider a fictional office in Sarawak planning a modest security upgrade. A caller rings reception to ask about camera placement for a small reception area and is told the facilities team will respond. Reception notes the call but does not pass it on, and the caller rings again four days later. On the second call, the person who answers completes the form: caller asked about camera positions for the front desk area; channel, phone; date and time of first call; expected owner, facilities coordinator; what the caller was told, a callback within two working days. Nothing in that record diagnoses equipment or discusses settings. It simply shows that a routing step was missed. The monthly review can then ask a narrow question: did facilities receive the first enquiry, and if not, where did it stop? That is a far more useful conversation than a general complaint that calls get lost. The same pattern applies to questions about a keyphone system change, a queue management system enquiry, or a request to discuss smart lock door access for a shared room. The form does not need a different version for each topic; it needs consistent fields.

Designing the form so people actually complete it

A form that lives only in a shared drive will be forgotten. Put it where the handoff happens: a short cut on the reception desk, a bookmark in the shared mailbox, or a printed pad beside the phone. Use plain labels and no more than eight fields. Suggested fields, as an author's heuristic rather than a standard: date and time received; channel; caller's request in their own words; department that received it; expected routing owner; what the caller was told to expect; whether a reply was confirmed; and a free-text note. Add one checkbox for "caller contacted us again about the same request", because repeat contact is the clearest signal that a handoff dropped. Do not add a satisfaction score. It invites staff to interpret the caller's mood rather than record the facts. Review the form with the people who fill it in after the first month and remove any field nobody uses. A shorter form completed consistently beats a thorough form completed rarely.

The monthly review: reading the pattern, not the person

Once a month, gather the completed forms and look for patterns by routing owner and by request type. A simple tally works: how many records, how many show a confirmed reply, how many show repeat contact. Then ask three questions. First, are there request types that repeatedly land in the wrong department? Second, are there roles that appear as the expected owner but rarely confirm a reply? Third, are there times of day or week when handoffs stall? Keep the review to thirty minutes and write down one or two changes, each with an owner and a review date. Avoid turning the session into an assessment of individual staff; the form records routing, and routing is usually a process problem. If a supplier relationship is involved, the review may produce a short list of questions for the next conversation with them, such as which department should hold the main contact. Where the office is planning broader work, the same discipline helps: a CCTV security systems project, for instance, benefits from knowing which enquiries reached the right team before a site visit was arranged.

Keeping the form useful over time

A feedback form decays quietly. Fields get renamed, the bookmark disappears, and within a few months the records are too patchy to review. Protect it with three small habits. First, name one person as the form's custodian, responsible for the monthly review and for any changes to the fields. Second, date-stamp every revision so old records can still be read in context. Third, retire fields that stop earning their place. When a new office or department joins, brief them on the form in the same session as their other handover paperwork, so it does not arrive as an afterthought. If your organisation works with an external provider on security matters, the form can also record which department holds that relationship, which keeps the trail intact when staff change roles.
