Keyphone Extension and Hunt Group Planning Sheet: A Guide for Sarawak Premises Managers

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical planning sheet for Sarawak premises managers who need to define each desk, its extension and its ring group before requesting a keyphone quote, so suppliers price against a fixed requirement instead of a vague brief.

Why the Planning Sheet Comes Before the Quote

A keyphone quote is only as precise as the brief behind it. When a supplier is asked to price a system for an office with no desk list, the quote has to assume a number of extensions, a call-routing pattern and a handset type. Those assumptions are where later disagreements begin: the installed system may have too few ports, the wrong ring behaviour, or handsets in rooms that were never meant to have them.

The planning sheet reverses that order. You decide, desk by desk, what each position needs, then hand the finished sheet to the supplier as a fixed requirement. The supplier can still advise on model and capacity, but the scope is yours. This is the same discipline that makes a site survey useful for other systems: a survey records what exists, and a requirement sheet records what is wanted. Eyonic Security Systems lists site survey among its services, and the same logic of measuring before specifying applies to voice as much as to cameras.

Keep the sheet to one or two pages. If it runs longer, it is probably describing the whole building rather than the phone requirement.

Building the Desk and Extension List

Start with a walk through the premises and list every position where a handset is expected. For each one, record four things: the room or location, the person or role who sits there, whether the position needs to make outside calls or only internal ones, and whether a headset or a second handset is required.

Give each position a proposed extension number and keep the numbering in blocks that mean something. A common approach is to reserve a block for reception and front-of-house, a block for each department, and a separate block for shared or corridor phones. Numbering by block makes later additions easier to slot in without renumbering existing desks.

Mark positions that are planned but not yet staffed. A new office wing or a counter that opens later should appear on the sheet as a reserved extension, not be left out and added after the quote. Suppliers price capacity, and capacity is easier to justify when the future positions are visible on the page.

Finally, note any position that does not need a desk phone at all. If a role is mobile or works from a shared terminal, saying so explicitly prevents the supplier from padding the count.

Defining Hunt Groups and Ring Behaviour

A hunt group is a set of extensions that ring together or in sequence so that an incoming call reaches whoever is free. The planning sheet should name each group, list its members in ring order, and state what happens when nobody answers.

For each group, write down the trigger: which incoming line or number routes to it. Then write the ring pattern in plain words, for example that the group rings the first two members together for a set number of rings, then moves to the next pair, then falls to voicemail. Avoid the word "normal" as a description; state the sequence.

Decide the overflow destination for every group. A group that rings forever is a group that annoys callers. The overflow might be a reception extension, a shared voicemail box, or an after-hours message. Whatever it is, it belongs on the sheet.

Also record the exceptions. If the accounts group should not ring during a stocktake, or if a manager's extension should never be part of a public group, note it. Exceptions are the part of the brief most often lost between the meeting and the installation.

Matching the Phone Plan to the Rest of the Premises

The phone plan does not sit alone. Reception staff often also manage visitor entry, and the way calls and doors interact affects where handsets and door-release controls are placed. If the premises uses an access control system or smart lock door access at the main entrance, note on the sheet which desk is expected to release the door and whether that release is a phone function or a separate button.

Eyonic's published review of smart lock door access notes that smart locks work best when the door condition, user roles and backup method are reviewed before installation, and that for business use the choice between a standalone smart lock and an access-control panel is usually checked first. The same review discipline applies here: decide who releases the door and how, before the hardware is ordered, rather than discovering at handover that the release button is on the wrong side of the wall.

If the premises also runs a queue management system at a service counter, note whether counter staff need to take calls while serving. That single question can change the handset and headset requirement for the whole front-of-house block.

A Worked Example: A Small Office in Sarawak

The following example is fictional and is included only to show how the sheet is filled in.

A two-floor office has reception, four administrative desks, a manager's room, a meeting room and a small accounts section. The sheet lists reception as extension 101, the four admin desks as 111 to 114, the manager as 121, the meeting room as 131 and accounts as 141 and 142. Two future desks in a planned extension are reserved as 115 and 116.

Two hunt groups are defined. The main group rings reception and the admin desks in pairs, then overflows to a shared voicemail box after a stated number of rings. The accounts group rings 141 and 142 only, and overflows to the manager's extension during office hours. The meeting room phone is excluded from all groups so that meetings are not interrupted.

The sheet also notes that reception releases the main entrance door, and that the release is a separate button rather than a phone key. That one line tells the supplier that the phone system does not need to carry door-release wiring, which keeps the quote focused on voice.

What to Send With the Quote Request

Send the desk and extension list, the hunt group definitions, the overflow destinations and the exceptions as one package. Add a short cover note stating the number of positions now, the number reserved for later, and any positions that need headsets.

Ask the supplier to respond against the sheet rather than in free prose. A useful response confirms each group and each overflow, states the capacity of the proposed system against your position count, and flags anything on the sheet that the proposed model cannot do. If a supplier proposes a different grouping, ask for the reason in writing so you can compare it with the other quotes on the same basis.

Keep the sheet as a living document. When a desk moves or a group changes, update the sheet and the date on it. At handover, the sheet becomes the test script: someone walks to each listed position, calls each group, and confirms the overflow behaves as written. That is a far better acceptance test than listening to a handset ring once and calling it done.

Where the Keyphone System Fits in the Wider Security Plan

Many premises managers in Sarawak are planning several systems at once: cameras, alarms, door access and voice. The keyphone system is usually the one with the least written specification, because it is familiar and seems simple. That familiarity is exactly why it benefits from a sheet.

Group the planning work by reader intent. If your immediate question is how calls should route, this sheet is the tool. If your question is how the entrance is controlled, the door access review is the better starting point. If your question is what the supplier will actually install and how it will be tested, the handover checklist is the companion document. Keeping these as separate sheets, each with its own date and owner, stops one decision from quietly changing another.
