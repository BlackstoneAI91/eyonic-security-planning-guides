Camera Placement Worksheet for a Stairwell and Back-of-House Corridor

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A room-by-room worksheet for Sarawak premises managers to map sightlines, mounting heights and blind spots in stairwells and service corridors, producing a marked-up floor plan an installer can quote from.

Why vertical circulation and service corridors need their own worksheet

Stairwells and back-of-house corridors are the parts of a building that staff use constantly and visitors rarely see. They are also the parts where a camera is hardest to place well: a stairwell is a tall, narrow shaft with landings that block the view above and below, and a service corridor is often long, dim and lined with doors, trolleys and stored goods. A camera chosen for a shop floor and mounted the same way in a stairwell will usually show a strip of wall and the top of a handrail.

This worksheet treats those two spaces as their own placement problem. It is written for premises managers in Sarawak who are planning an ordinary security-system project and want to hand an installer a marked-up floor plan rather than a verbal description. The aim is not to design the system yourself. It is to record what you want covered, where a camera could physically go, and which gaps you accept, so that a supplier can quote against a fixed picture.

Eyonic Security Systems plans, supplies and installs monitoring systems with camera placement, recording and remote viewing for homes, offices, shops and industrial sites, so a marked plan is the kind of input that fits a normal survey conversation. Keep the worksheet factual: locations, heights, obstructions and the coverage you are trying to achieve.

Before you draw: the four measurements to take at each location

Walk the stairwell and corridor with a tape measure, a torch and a notebook before sketching anything. At each candidate mounting point, record four things.

First, the mounting height in metres from the finished floor, and whether the surface is concrete, plasterboard, metal or a ceiling grid. Second, the horizontal distance to the furthest point you want covered, because that distance plus the height sets the angle the lens must accept. Third, the obstructions between the two: landing soffits, handrails, pipework, signage, light fittings and door swings. Fourth, the light situation at the times that matter, including whether the corridor is lit overnight or goes dark when the last person leaves.

Add one more note that is easy to forget: where a cable could reasonably run from that point back to the recorder. A perfect camera position with no viable cable route becomes a different, worse position once the installer prices it. If you are unsure, mark two candidate points and let the site survey settle it.

Stairwell worksheet: landings, flights and the entry door

Treat the stairwell as a stack of small zones rather than one space. Fill in a row for each of the following, using your own floor plan.

Entry door at the bottom: mark the door, its swing, and the point where a person first becomes visible. A camera above and behind the door looking down the first flight usually captures faces on entry, but check that the door leaf does not block the view when open.

Each landing: note the landing depth and whether a camera mounted at the landing ceiling can see the flight below and the flight above. In most stairwells it cannot do both, so decide which direction matters more and write that decision down.

Each flight: record the number of steps and the headroom. A camera looking along a flight sees the top of heads on the way up and faces on the way down; note which you need.

Top exit: mark the final door and whether it opens onto a roof, a plant room or another floor. This is often the least observed point in the building.

For each row, write the coverage you want in plain words, for example "faces of anyone entering from the ground floor door". Avoid writing camera model numbers at this stage; the coverage statement is what the installer prices against.

Back-of-house corridor worksheet: doors, junctions and long runs

A service corridor is mostly a series of doors and junctions, so build the worksheet around them.

List every door in order along the corridor, including store rooms, staff rooms, washrooms, riser cupboards and the door back into the public area. For each, note whether it is normally locked, and whether you need to see the door itself or the approach to it. Seeing a door face is easy; seeing who approached it usually needs a camera further back along the corridor.

Mark each junction and corner, because these are where a single camera can cover two directions at once, and also where a blind spot is most likely. Note any point where the corridor changes width, steps up or down, or turns.

For long straight runs, record the total length and the ceiling height. A corridor longer than a typical lens can resolve usefully may need two cameras facing each other rather than one camera stretched to cover everything. Write down the distance at which you would still want to recognise a person, and mark that point on the plan.

Finally, note trolley routes, cleaning equipment storage and any stacked goods. These move, and a camera view that is clear on survey day can be blocked a month later.

Marking blind spots and accepted gaps on the plan

Once the candidate points are marked, shade the areas that no candidate camera can see. This shaded map is the most useful page you will give an installer, because it turns an argument about coverage into a drawing.

Use three categories. Covered: a candidate camera has a clear line to the area. Marginal: the area is visible but at an angle, at long range, or only when a door is closed. Accepted gap: you have decided not to cover it, and you write down why, for example a locked riser that is checked daily by staff.

Be honest about marginal areas. A stairwell camera that technically sees a landing through a gap between flights is not the same as one that sees it clearly. Marking it marginal tells the installer to propose a second point or a different lens, which is exactly the conversation you want before a quote is issued.

Keep the plan at a consistent scale and label every camera point with a letter, so the installer can refer to point C in their quotation and you can compare quotes line by line.

Turning the worksheet into a brief the installer can quote from

A marked plan is only half the handover. Add a one-page brief that sits in front of it, covering the following.

Scope: which stairwells and corridors are included, and which are explicitly out of scope for this phase. Coverage intent: the plain-word statements from your worksheet rows. Recording: how many days of footage you expect to keep, and whether the corridor cameras need a different retention from the public-area cameras. Viewing: who needs to watch live, from where, and whether that includes remote viewing on a phone or a branch office. Constraints: working hours, areas that cannot be entered during trading hours, and any surface that cannot be drilled.

Recording and remote viewing are normal parts of a planned system, and NVR setup is where retention and user accounts get fixed, so put your expectations in the brief rather than leaving them to be assumed. If the same project touches a controlled door, note it separately; smart lock door access works best when user permissions and a backup entry method are planned before installation, which is a different decision from camera coverage.

When the brief and plan are ready, ask for a walk-through during the site survey and record any change to the plan in writing. For the installation phase itself, the CCTV installation service page is the natural place to check what the supplier expects to receive from you before work is scheduled.

A simple scoring pass before you sign the brief

Before the brief goes out, score your own worksheet out of ten on each line below. This is an author heuristic for catching gaps, not a researched standard.

Every stairwell landing has a row, even the ones you decided not to cover. Every corridor door is listed with its lock status. Each candidate point has a height, a surface type and a cable route note. Each coverage statement is written in plain words rather than a product name. Blind spots are shaded and accepted gaps have a stated reason. The brief states retention days and who needs remote viewing. Working-hour constraints are written down.

A score below about seven on any line usually means the installer will have to ask you a question, which is fine, but better asked before the quote than after. Keep the scored worksheet with the signed brief so that a later manager can see what was decided and why.
