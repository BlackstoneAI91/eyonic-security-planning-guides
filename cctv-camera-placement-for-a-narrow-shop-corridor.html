Camera Placement Worksheet for a Narrow Retail Corridor

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical worksheet for Sarawak premises managers to map shelf height, aisle width and counter sightlines to candidate CCTV camera positions, with a column for rejecting blocked views.

Why a narrow corridor needs its own placement worksheet

A narrow retail corridor is a constrained space. Shelving on both sides, promotional signage and stock trolleys can block a camera's view. A generic plan that works in an open shop floor may leave you with footage of the tops of shelves rather than the aisle. This worksheet helps you record the physical facts of your corridor and test each candidate camera position against them before you commit to a CCTV installation.

Use it as a working document. Fill it in on site, take photos, and keep it with your project file. The aim is to give an installer a clear picture of what you want to see and where equipment can realistically go. Worksheet part 1: measure the corridor and record obstructions

Start with a simple sketch of the corridor. Mark the entrance, the counter, the exit, and any side openings. Then measure and record the following in a table. Use a tape measure and note everything in metres or centimetres, but be consistent.

Aisle width: measure the clear walking width at three points: near the entrance, at the middle, and near the counter. Note the narrowest point.

Shelf height: record the height of the tallest shelving unit on each side. If shelves are adjustable, note the current height and whether it is likely to change.

Signage and displays: list any hanging signs, standees, or promotional boards that could block a camera's line of sight. Note their height and position.

Counter sightline: stand at the counter and look down the corridor. Note what you can see and what is hidden by shelves or displays. Repeat from the entrance looking toward the counter.

Lighting: note windows, skylights, and light fixtures. Record whether the corridor is bright or dim at different times of day. This matters for camera placement because strong backlight can silhouette people.

Stock movement: note where trolleys or boxes are parked during deliveries. These are temporary obstructions that can become permanent if not planned for.

Keep this part factual. Do not guess; measure. The worksheet is only as good as the data you put in.

Worksheet part 2: list candidate camera positions

With the measurements in hand, list every position where a camera could physically be mounted. For a narrow corridor, typical candidates include:

- Above the entrance, looking down the aisle. - At the far end, looking back toward the entrance. - Above the counter, angled to cover the till and the customer side. - On a side wall at mid-corridor, if the wall is clear. - Near a side opening, if you need to see who enters that area.

For each candidate, record the mounting height, the direction it would face, and the approximate distance to the main area of interest. Also note any immediate obstructions, such as a shelf that would block the lower half of the view.

This is also the point to consider whether a camera would capture areas you do not want to record, such as a neighbouring shopfront through a window. Note these privacy considerations on the worksheet so they can be addressed during the site survey.

Worksheet part 3: reject positions blocked by stock or signage

Not every candidate position will work. This part of the worksheet is a rejection column. For each candidate, answer these questions:

1. Is the view blocked by shelving at the planned mounting height? If yes, note the height of the obstruction and whether a higher or lower mount would clear it.

2. Is the view blocked by a hanging sign or display? If yes, can the sign be moved, or is it fixed?

3. Does the position look directly into a bright window or light source? If yes, note the time of day when this occurs.

4. Would the camera be vulnerable to being knocked or covered by stock? For example, a low mount near a stocking area.

5. Does the position capture private areas or neighbouring premises? If yes, note the concern.

If a candidate fails any of these checks, mark it as rejected and write a short reason. Do not discard the position entirely; sometimes a small change, such as moving the camera 30 centimetres to one side, solves the problem. Record the alternative.

The goal is to arrive at a shortlist of positions that are both practical and useful. A camera that sees only the top of a shelf is not helping you.

Worksheet part 4: match positions to what you need to see

A camera position is only useful if it answers a question you actually have. For a narrow retail corridor, common questions include:

- Did a customer enter and leave? (Entrance view) - Was a product picked up and taken past the counter? (Aisle view) - Was the till area secure during a transaction? (Counter view) - Did someone enter through a side door? (Side opening view)

For each question, write down which candidate position would answer it. If no position answers a question, note that as a gap. You may need an additional camera or a different approach.

This is also where you decide whether you need continuous recording or event-triggered recording. That is a conversation for the installer, but your worksheet should note the times of day when coverage matters most, such as opening hours, delivery windows, or cash-up times.

Worksheet part 5: prepare for the site survey

Your completed worksheet is a briefing document for a CCTV site survey. A site survey is when an installer visits to verify measurements, check cable routes, and confirm power availability. Eyonic's service list includes site survey as a service, so you can expect this step if you engage them.

Before the survey, review your worksheet and highlight the positions you have shortlisted. Be ready to explain why you rejected others. Take photos of the corridor from each shortlisted position so the installer can see what you see.

During the survey, ask the installer to confirm:

- Whether the shortlisted positions are reachable with cabling. - Whether power is available at or near each position. - Whether the mounting height is suitable for the camera type. - Whether any privacy masks or angle adjustments are needed.

Record the answers on the worksheet. This becomes your record of what was agreed.

If your project also includes an autogate or other perimeter security, keep that separate. This worksheet is about the retail corridor. For gate-related planning, a different checklist applies.

Worksheet part 6: acceptance and handover notes

After installation, use the worksheet to check that each camera delivers the view you planned. This is not a technical test; it is a visual check. Stand in the corridor at different times of day and look at the live view or recorded footage. Ask:

- Can I see the entrance clearly? - Can I see the aisle at the narrowest point? - Can I see the counter and the customer side? - Is any view blocked by stock or signage that was not there during the survey?

If a view is not as expected, note it and raise it with the installer. The worksheet gives you a shared reference.

Also record who will have access to the recordings and how long they will be kept. That is an operational decision for your business, not a technical one. Keep it in the same file so that the handover is complete.
