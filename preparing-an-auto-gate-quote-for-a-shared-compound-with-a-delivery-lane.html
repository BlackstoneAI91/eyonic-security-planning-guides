Preparing an Auto Gate Quote for a Shared Compound With a Delivery Lane

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical guide for Sarawak premises managers on preparing an auto gate quote for a shared compound with a delivery lane: what to measure, how to separate pedestrians, how to describe delivery windows and what acceptance documents to request.

Start With a One-Page Compound Brief

A shared compound is a set of overlapping users, not a single gate. Before you ask anyone for a quote, write a one-page brief that a contractor can read in five minutes. Keep it factual and short.

Cover these points:

Who uses the compound: tenants, staff, residents, delivery drivers, waste collectors, service technicians. Which entrances exist and which one is the main vehicle entry. Where the delivery lane runs, and whether it crosses a pedestrian path. The hours when the compound is busiest. Who currently opens and closes the gate, and who holds the remote controls. Any known problems, such as vehicles queuing onto the road or drivers walking through the vehicle lane.

This brief becomes the cover sheet of your quote request. It also gives the installer a reason to visit rather than price from a photograph. A site survey is a normal first step for this kind of work, and Eyonic Security Systems lists site survey among its services.

Keep the brief descriptive. Do not include instructions for wiring, gate control logic or how to override anything. Those belong to the installer, and your job is to describe the site and the users.

Measurements to Ask the Installer to Record

A quote is only as good as the measurements behind it. Ask the installer to record the following on the survey sheet, and ask for a copy with the quote. These are ordinary site measurements, not technical instructions.

Gate and approach:

Gate type (swing or sliding) and the clear opening width. The full travel of each leaf or the runback space a sliding gate needs. Ground level and slope across the opening, and whether water pools near the gate line. The distance from the gate to the nearest public road edge, so queuing vehicles do not block traffic.

Delivery lane:

Lane width and the largest vehicle type expected. Turning space at the end of the lane, and whether a vehicle can turn without reversing into the pedestrian area. Any overhead obstruction, drain cover or change in surface along the lane.

Pedestrian route:

Where people walk now, and whether that path crosses the vehicle lane. Whether a separate pedestrian gate or side opening exists.

Power and equipment location:

Where the gate motor and control equipment would sit. Whether a power point is available nearby, and who is responsible for it.

Ask the installer to note anything that would change the price, such as extra civil work, a longer cable run or a second remote set. A quote that lists assumptions is easier to compare than a single total.

Separating People From Vehicles in the Design

The most useful design decision in a shared compound is often the simplest: keep people out of the vehicle lane. Ask the installer to propose a pedestrian route that does not require someone to walk through the gate opening while a vehicle is moving.

Questions worth putting in the quote request:

Will pedestrians have a separate gate, side door or marked walkway? How will a delivery driver on foot reach the office or receiving point without crossing the vehicle path? Where will visitors wait if the gate is closed? Is there a safe place for a person to stand while the gate is moving?

For the pedestrian door, the choice between a standalone smart lock and a wider access-control arrangement is worth discussing early. Eyonic notes that smart locks work best when door condition, user roles and a backup method are reviewed before installation, and that for business use it usually checks whether a standalone smart lock or an access-control panel is more appropriate. That review belongs in the quote stage, not after the door is drilled.

If the compound also needs door access for staff or tenants, the access control discussion can run alongside the gate quote so the two do not conflict. Writing Delivery Windows Into the Quote

Delivery lanes fail quietly when nobody writes down the timetable. Add a short delivery schedule to your quote request so the installer can size the gate and the approach properly.

A simple table works:

Delivery window, for example early morning, midday and late afternoon. Vehicle type expected at each window, such as a van, a one-tonne lorry or a container truck. Whether the driver needs to enter the compound on foot. Who opens the gate, and whether that person is on site at that hour. What happens if a delivery arrives outside the window.

Use fictional examples when you draft this, then replace them with your own site's real pattern. For instance: "Weekday mornings, two vans between 8am and 10am; driver walks to the office; gate opened by the duty supervisor." That sentence tells the installer more than a paragraph of description.

Ask the quote to state how the gate will behave during a delivery window, in plain language, and who is responsible for letting a vehicle in. Do not ask for or accept instructions on how to bypass the gate or its controls. If the compound needs a record of who entered and when, raise that as a separate requirement and let the installer propose the option.

Adding Cameras to the Same Site Review

A gate quote is a good moment to review camera placement, because the gate and the delivery lane are where vehicles and people meet. Doing both in one site visit usually produces a more coherent plan than treating them as separate projects.

Ask the installer to note, during the same survey:

Which camera positions would see the gate opening, the delivery lane and the pedestrian route. Where lighting changes during the day, and whether headlights at night would wash out a view. Whether a camera would look into a neighbouring property, and how that would be handled. Where the recorder would sit, and who would have access to it. Whether remote viewing is wanted, and for which staff roles.

A Quote Comparison Worksheet

When two or three quotes arrive, compare them line by line rather than by total. The worksheet below is an author heuristic, not a scoring standard; adjust the weights to suit your compound.

For each quote, record:

Scope: does it cover the gate, the pedestrian route, the delivery lane approach and any cameras? Measurements: are the gate width, runback, slope and lane dimensions stated? Assumptions: what does the installer assume you will provide, such as power or civil work? Delivery windows: does the quote reflect your actual timetable? Safety testing: is a test of the gate's safety behaviour included before handover? Handover: who receives the remote controls, and how are they recorded? Documentation: is there a written list of what was installed and what was tested? Warranty and support: what is covered, and who is contacted for a fault?

A quote that answers eight of these nine points is easier to trust than a cheaper one that answers three. Where a quote is silent, ask a written question and keep the answer with the quote.

Acceptance Documentation and Handover

The last stage is the least glamorous and the most useful. Before final payment, ask for a short handover pack. It does not need to be long, but it should let a new premises manager understand the site a year later.

Ask for:

A list of equipment installed, with locations noted in plain language. The measurements recorded at survey, updated if anything changed during work. A record of the safety test carried out at handover, and who witnessed it. The number of remote controls or access credentials issued, and to whom. A named contact for faults and the process for reporting one. A short note on the delivery lane and pedestrian route as built. If cameras were included, a note on who reviews footage and how long it is kept.

Walk the site with the installer before signing. Open and close the gate, walk the pedestrian route, and drive a vehicle through the delivery lane at a quiet time. Confirm that the gate behaves as described during a delivery window.
