Camera Placement for a Loading Bay Entrance: A Placement Worksheet

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical worksheet for Sarawak premises managers planning camera placement for a loading bay entrance, covering plate reading, door coverage, blind spots and a mounting-height table.

Start With What the Bay Must Prove

A loading bay entrance is a hard camera subject because it asks one lens to do several jobs at once. Vehicles arrive and leave, plates need to be legible, doors open and close, and people walk between the vehicle and the door carrying goods. Before choosing a camera, write down what a recording of the bay should be able to show six weeks from now. Typical answers fall into four buckets: identifying a vehicle by plate, confirming that a door was closed or locked, seeing who entered or left through that door, and showing the condition of goods at the point of handover. Each bucket points to a different view, and one wide camera covering the whole bay will usually do none of them well. Treat this as a written requirement list, not a shopping list. The list becomes the brief you hand to a supplier and the yardstick you use at acceptance. Keep the list short and specific: one line per thing the bay must prove, with the location it applies to.

Plate Reading Is a Geometry Problem, Not a Camera Spec

Reading a number plate depends on the angle and distance between the camera and the plate, not on the number of megapixels alone. A plate seen at a shallow angle is foreshortened, and no resolution recovers characters that were never captured square-on. In practice this means the plate camera should look along the direction of travel rather than across it, and it should be mounted so the plate passes through the frame at a workable distance. Walk the bay with a tape measure and mark where a vehicle stops to be checked, where the gate or roller shutter sits, and where the driver's window ends up. Those three points define the plate camera's field. If vehicles approach from two directions, that is two plate views, not one clever angle. Night-time is the other half of the problem: headlights can wash out a plate, so note where light falls at the time of day the bay is busiest. Write the chosen position on a simple sketch with the distance to the stop point. That sketch is far more useful to an installer than a description, and it keeps the conversation about geometry rather than brand names.

Door Coverage and the Blind Spots That Catch People Out

The door between the bay and the store or warehouse is where goods cross the boundary, so it deserves its own view rather than being a corner of the plate camera's frame. A camera above and slightly to one side of the door, angled down, shows faces on exit and the door leaf itself. A camera directly overhead shows the top of heads and tells you little about who passed. The classic blind spots at a loading bay are predictable. The first is the strip immediately in front of the door, hidden by an open leaf or a roller shutter as it rises. The second is the gap between a parked vehicle and the wall, where someone can stand out of view. The third is the area behind the vehicle once it has reversed in. For each, decide whether the risk matters to your operation and, if it does, whether a second camera or a repositioned one closes it. A useful habit is to stand at each blind spot during the survey and ask whether a person standing there could be identified from the proposed camera position. If the answer is no, the placement is not finished.

A Mounting-Height Table to Work From

Mounting height is a trade-off between identifying faces and protecting the camera. The figures below are author heuristics for ordinary single-storey and low-bay premises, offered as a starting point for discussion during a CCTV site survey, not as fixed standards. Confirm the final heights with your installer against your own building. Door-entry camera, looking down at the doorway: roughly 2.4 to 3.0 metres, close enough to show faces, high enough to be out of casual reach. Plate camera at the vehicle stop point: roughly 1.5 to 2.5 metres, kept low so the plate is seen closer to square-on rather than steeply from above. Wide overview camera covering the whole bay: roughly 3.5 to 5.0 metres, high enough to see over parked vehicles but not so high that everything becomes a small shape. Perimeter or fence-line camera: roughly 3.0 to 4.0 metres, angled along the fence rather than across it. Write your chosen height next to each camera on the sketch, along with the reason. A height without a reason tends to drift during installation.

Light, Weather and the Sarawak Setting

A loading bay in Sarawak deals with strong sun, heavy rain and, at many sites, a roofed area that is dark by mid-afternoon. Three practical points follow. First, check where the sun sits at the hours the bay is busiest; a camera facing a low sun will flare, and a small change of angle or a different mounting point often fixes it. Second, note whether the camera position is under cover or exposed. An exposed position needs a housing suited to rain, and the cable route needs to be planned rather than improvised. Third, look at the light at night. If the bay is lit by floodlights, check that the camera is not looking directly into them. If it is dark, plan lighting as part of the placement decision rather than assuming the camera will cope. None of this is exotic, but it is easy to skip when the focus is on camera count. A short walk of the bay at dusk, before any order is placed, will tell you more about night performance than any catalogue.

Recording, Retention and Who Watches the Bay

Placement decisions have consequences downstream. A plate camera and a door camera produce different pictures, and the recording and retention plan should match what each view is for. Decide how long the bay footage needs to be kept, based on how far back you might need to check a delivery or an incident, and write that down as a requirement. Then decide who needs to see the bay live and who only needs to review recordings. Remote viewing is useful for a manager who is not always on site, but it should be a deliberate decision with named users rather than an open link. Eyonic CCTV's service scope includes recording and remote viewing alongside camera placement, so these are reasonable topics to raise in the same conversation as the placement sketch. Keep a short note of who has access and why, and review it when staff change. That note is the difference between a system that is managed and one that is merely installed.

The One-Page Placement Worksheet

Bring the threads together on a single page you can hand over. For each camera, record: a name (plate, door, overview, perimeter), the job it must do, the mounting position described in words, the mounting height, the direction it faces, the light conditions at its busiest hour, and the blind spot it is meant to close. Add a column for the reason the position was chosen, because that is what protects the plan when someone suggests moving a camera later. Then add a short acceptance note: at handover, stand at the stop point with a sample plate and confirm it is legible on playback, walk through the door and confirm faces are identifiable, and check each named blind spot from the recorded view. If a view fails, the worksheet shows exactly which camera and which requirement is affected. This is also the point to discuss the wider installation scope, since camera placement is one part of a CCTV installation and the cabling, recording and viewing arrangements need to line up with it. A supplier working from your one-page worksheet can quote against a fixed plan instead of assumptions.
