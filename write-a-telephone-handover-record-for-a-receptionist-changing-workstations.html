Write a Telephone Handover Record for a Receptionist Changing Workstations

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical guide for Sarawak premises managers on writing a telephone handover record when a receptionist moves to a different workstation, covering what to capture, who owns each field, and where to log unanswered questions without recording dial codes or account details.

Start with the purpose, not the phone

Before anyone drafts a form, write one sentence describing what the handover record is for. A useful example: the record exists so that the receptionist taking over a workstation knows which calls they are expected to answer, who to pass them to, and which questions are still open. That sentence keeps the document short and stops it drifting into a technical manual.

A telephone handover record is a working document, not a training course. It should describe the receptionist's ordinary duties at the new desk: greeting callers, taking messages, transferring a caller to the right department, and noting anything that needs a follow-up. It should not contain dial codes, account setup steps or configuration values. Those belong with whoever is authorised to manage the phone system, and they should stay in an approved system rather than a shared document.

For a Sarawak premises manager planning an ordinary security-system project, the same discipline applies across the site. A keyphone system handover sits alongside the other records you keep for the building, such as visitor instructions or maintenance notes, and it should be written in the same plain style so that a new receptionist can read it without asking for a walkthrough. Decide the purpose first, then let the fields follow from it.

Name the roles before naming the tasks

A handover record fails quietly when nobody knows who is responsible for each line. Before listing tasks, write a short role list. Typical roles in a small office are the outgoing receptionist, the incoming receptionist, the office or premises manager who approves the change, and the provider or internal contact who handles telephone support. Keep the list to roles, not personal names, so the record survives staff changes.

For each role, add one line describing what that person is expected to do with the record. The outgoing receptionist completes the observation fields. The incoming receptionist reads the record and signs to confirm they have received it. The manager approves the record and decides which open questions need to be raised with the provider. The support contact answers the questions that the manager cannot resolve internally.

This mirrors how other site records are handled. Eyonic Security Systems, trading as Eyonic CCTV, plans, supplies and installs CCTV systems for homes, offices, shops and industrial sites in Sarawak, and its published service notes describe a site survey as part of planning. A survey is a useful comparison: someone visits, observes, and records what they find, and someone else approves the result. A telephone handover record works the same way, with observation separated from approval.

Capture observations, not settings

The heart of the record is a short list of observations the outgoing receptionist can make at the old desk and confirm at the new one. Keep each line to something a receptionist can see or hear without changing any settings. Fictional examples: which calls arrive at this desk during the morning, which department most callers ask for, where the message pad is kept, and what the receptionist says when a caller needs a colleague who is away.

Add a column for the new workstation so the two can be compared. If the new desk has a different handset, a different number of lines, or a different position relative to the entrance, note it as an observation and leave the explanation to the support contact. Do not write down how to change anything.

A second column for open questions is the most valuable part of the page. When the outgoing receptionist is unsure, the correct entry is a question, not a guess. Examples: who should receive calls for the accounts team after the move, and whether the evening message routine stays the same. Each question gets an owner and a status of open or answered. This keeps uncertainty visible instead of buried in a conversation.

If the site also runs a queue management system at the counter, note only that the receptionist's counter duties are affected, and route the detail to whoever owns that system.

Separate the telephone record from the security records

Reception desks often sit near other systems, and it is easy for one handover document to absorb everything. Keep the telephone record focused on calls and messages. If the move also touches door access, note it as a cross-reference and let the access record stand on its own.

Eyonic's published product notes on smart lock door access say that permissions and backup entry should be planned properly, and that for business use the choice between a standalone smart lock and an access-control panel is usually reviewed before installation. That is a planning point, not something a receptionist should resolve in a handover note. The right entry is a question for the manager: does the receptionist's move change who is listed for the reception door, and who approves that list?

Similarly, if the reception area has cameras, the handover record should not describe camera placement or viewing arrangements. Eyonic's service notes list camera placement, recording and remote viewing as part of planning a monitoring system, and those decisions belong with the provider and the approving manager. A one-line cross-reference is enough: state that the reception move may affect the entrance area and that the manager will confirm with the provider whether anything needs review.

Use a short worksheet with a clear sign-off

A one-page worksheet is enough for most small offices. Suggested fields, offered as an author heuristic rather than a standard: site reference, date of move, outgoing and incoming receptionist roles, manager role, a table of observations with old and new columns, an open-questions table with owner and status, and a sign-off block.

The sign-off block should record three things: that the incoming receptionist has read the record, that the manager has approved it, and the date. Avoid asking anyone to sign that the phone system is fully configured, because the receptionist is not in a position to confirm that. The record confirms understanding of duties, not technical completion.

Keep the worksheet versioned. A simple revision line, such as first issue or second issue with a date, helps when the move is delayed or when a question is answered later. When a question is answered, do not delete it; mark it answered and note who answered it. That way the next receptionist can see how the decision was reached.

Store the completed record where the premises team keeps other operational documents, and give the incoming receptionist a copy before their first shift at the new desk.

Where unanswered questions should go

Every handover record should end with a route for questions that nobody at the desk can answer. Name the destination in the record itself: for example, the office manager's weekly review, or the provider's support contact through the channel the manager already uses. Do not invent a new process for the handover; attach it to one that already exists.

For telephone questions, the manager is usually the right first stop, because the manager can decide whether the question needs the provider at all. For questions that touch other systems, route them to the owner of that system. If the site has a keyphone system supplied or supported by a provider, the manager should confirm the correct contact route before the move, not after the first confusing call.

Eyonic's public pages describe the company as a planner, supplier and installer of CCTV systems in Sarawak, and its service list includes a site survey, camera placement, NVR setup and remote viewing. Those are the areas where a provider conversation is appropriate. A receptionist handover is not one of them, so keep the two conversations separate and let the manager decide when a provider needs to be involved.

Review the record after the first week

A handover record written before the move is a prediction. Set a short review after the incoming receptionist has worked a week at the new desk, and use the review to correct the observations rather than to add new sections. Ask three questions: which entries turned out to be wrong, which questions are still open, and which entries were never needed.

Remove or archive entries that added nothing. A record that grows every review becomes unreadable, and the next receptionist will skip it. Keep the open-questions table as the living part of the document and let the observation table settle once it is accurate.

If the review shows that a question belongs to another system, move it to that system's record and note the move. This is the same principle as keeping a CCTV site survey separate from a telephone handover: each record should be owned by the person who can act on it. Eyonic's published material treats a site survey as a distinct planning step, and that separation is a useful model for any premises document.

Finally, file the reviewed record with the date and the reviewer's role. The next time a receptionist changes workstations, the previous record becomes the starting draft, and the cycle takes far less time.
