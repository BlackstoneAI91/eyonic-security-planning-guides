Recording Entrances in an Auto Gate Enquiry: A Property Manager's Guide

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical guide for Sarawak premises managers on listing entrances, permissions and authorisation checks in an auto gate enquiry, with a scoped property request and a simple worksheet.

Start with a scoped property request, not a wish list

An auto gate enquiry is easier to answer when it describes the property rather than the outcome you hope for. Before contacting any provider, write a short scoped property request: the site name or a neutral reference, the type of premises, the number of separate entrances that vehicles or people use, and which of those entrances the enquiry actually covers. Keep it factual. A fictional example: a two-storey shoplot in a Sarawak town has one main vehicle entrance from the road, one side pedestrian entrance, and a rear service entrance shared with a neighbouring unit. The manager decides the enquiry covers the main vehicle entrance only, and notes the other two as out of scope for now. That single decision prevents a provider from quoting for work nobody asked for, and it gives you a clean baseline if the scope later grows. Record the date and a version label on the request, because entrance lists change when tenancies change. If your organisation already keeps a room or asset list, reuse its naming so the enquiry and the internal record match. This is planning, not a technical specification, and it should stay readable by someone who has never visited the site.

List each entrance with the details a provider will ask for

For every entrance in scope, record a small set of neutral facts. Useful fields include: a plain name for the entrance, who normally uses it, whether it is shared with another occupier, whether the surface is level or sloped, and who currently holds the authority to approve changes there. Add a line for what the entrance is expected to do in ordinary daily use, such as letting staff vehicles in during opening hours. Avoid describing how any gate or barrier currently operates, and do not record codes, key locations or opening routines in the enquiry document. Those belong in whatever approved system your organisation already uses, not in a procurement file that may be emailed around. A fictional example of a completed line: "Rear service entrance, shared with Unit B, used by delivery staff, approval contact is the landlord's representative, currently outside the scope of this enquiry." That level of detail lets a provider ask sensible follow-up questions without inviting anyone to guess. Where an entrance is shared, say so plainly, because shared entrances usually need a different conversation about who authorises work and who receives the finished documentation.

Separate landlord permissions from your own approval

Property managers often hold operational responsibility without holding the authority to approve physical changes. Write down both, side by side, for each entrance. One column names the person or role who can approve the enquiry going forward; another names the landlord, management corporation or owner representative whose consent is needed before work at that entrance. Where the two are the same, note it. Where they differ, note what each party has actually confirmed in writing, and leave the field blank rather than assuming. A fictional example: a manager of a rented industrial unit confirms internally that the operations director approves the enquiry, but records that written consent from the landlord is still outstanding for the main vehicle entrance. The enquiry can still be sent, with that gap stated openly, so the provider knows which parts are provisional. This is an authorisation check, not legal advice; if the lease terms are unclear, the right step is to ask the party who owns that question. Keeping permissions visible in the document also protects whoever signs later, because the record shows what was known at the time the enquiry was issued.

Decide what belongs in the enquiry and what stays internal

An enquiry is a scoped request, not a full project file. A useful test is to ask, for each line, whether the provider needs it to respond sensibly. Entrance names, usage, shared status and approval contacts pass that test. Internal commentary about staffing, budget discussions or past disputes does not. Keep a separate internal note for anything sensitive, and refer to it only by a neutral reference in the enquiry. If the property also has other security interests, such as CCTV security systems covering a vehicle approach, mention them only as context and keep them out of the auto gate scope unless you intend to procure them together. Mixing unrelated wishes into one enquiry makes comparison harder and can obscure which entrance the provider is actually being asked about. A short scope statement at the top of the document helps: "This enquiry covers the main vehicle entrance only. Other entrances are listed for context and are not part of this request." That sentence alone resolves most ambiguity, and it gives you a defensible answer if a quotation later arrives covering more than you asked for.

Use a one-page entrance worksheet

A worksheet keeps the enquiry consistent across sites and across staff changes. Suggested columns, offered as an author heuristic rather than a standard: entrance reference, plain description, who uses it, shared or exclusive, approval role, landlord consent status, in scope yes or no, and the date last checked. Fill it in before drafting the enquiry, then attach or summarise it. A fictional example row: "Entrance 2, side pedestrian gate, staff and visitors, exclusive, approval by facilities lead, landlord consent not required, out of scope, checked 12 March." Score nothing and rank nothing; the worksheet is a record, not an evaluation. Its value appears later, when a provider asks which entrances were considered and why some were excluded. Review the worksheet whenever a tenancy starts or ends, because a shared entrance can quietly become exclusive, or the reverse, and the approval roles change with it. Keep one current version and archive older ones with their dates rather than editing over them. If your organisation runs several premises, the same worksheet format lets you compare enquiries without re-reading every document, and it makes handover to a colleague a matter of passing one page rather than reconstructing a conversation.

Plan the handoff before the enquiry goes out

Decide in advance who receives the provider's response, who checks it against the worksheet, and who signs off any next step. Name roles, not just individuals, so the process survives a staff change. A simple handoff note can record: the enquiry version sent, the date, the named contact for follow-up, and the internal person responsible for confirming that every in-scope entrance was addressed. Where the property also uses an access control system at a separate door, keep that conversation distinct and route it to whoever owns that scope, rather than folding it into the gate enquiry. A short pre-send checklist

Before the enquiry leaves your desk, run a quick check. Is every entrance named in plain language that a reader who has never visited the site would understand? Is each one marked in scope or out of scope, with a reason? Are the approval role and the landlord consent status recorded separately, with blanks left where nothing has been confirmed? Has anything sensitive, such as access values or internal commentary, been kept out of the document? Is there a version label and a date? Is the named follow-up contact a role as well as a person? These are author heuristics, not an audited standard, and a score is not the point; the point is that each question has an answer you can point to. A fictional example of a failed check: an enquiry lists three entrances but marks none of them out of scope, so the provider cannot tell whether all three are wanted. Fixing that takes one sentence. Finally, keep the sent version and the worksheet together in one place, so that when a response arrives, the comparison is between the provider's answer and your own record, not between two memories of a conversation.
