Planning Staff and Visitor Access for a Shared Office

Prepared for Eyonic Security System (Eyonic CCTV)

A practical guide for owners and facilities teams in Sarawak on mapping roles to doors, running an approval and revocation workflow, handing over visitor access, and reviewing audit records with privacy in mind.

Start With a Role-to-Door Worksheet, Not a Product List

Access planning for a shared office goes wrong when it begins with hardware. A better starting point is a single sheet of paper with two axes: the people who need to move through the office, and the doors they need to pass. List every role you can name — director, permanent staff, contract staff, cleaner, courier, visiting client, auditor, landlord's maintenance crew — down one side. List every controlled opening across the top: main entrance, reception door, staff room, server or records room, store room, rear service door, car park gate. Then mark each cell as full access, time-limited access, escorted only, or no access. This worksheet becomes the reference document for everything that follows, from the installer's site survey to the day a staff member resigns.

The value of the worksheet is that it forces decisions before anyone quotes equipment. A shared office usually has more roles than a single-tenant office, because different companies or departments may occupy the same floor and share a reception, a meeting room or a pantry. Writing the roles down exposes awkward cases early: the cleaner who arrives at six in the morning before any staff, the client who needs the meeting room but not the corridor behind it, the part-timer who works two days a week. Each of those cases has a different answer, and the answer should be recorded rather than remembered. Keep the worksheet versioned with a date, because access needs drift quietly as teams change.

Deciding Which Doors Deserve Electronic Control

Not every door in a shared office needs a reader, a keypad or a lock. A useful filter is to ask three questions of each opening: does it protect something valuable or sensitive, does it sit on the path a visitor would naturally take, and does it need a record of who passed through? A records room, a server cupboard and the main entrance usually answer yes to at least two. An internal pantry door often answers no to all three, and adding control there creates friction without benefit.

Where control is justified, the next decision is the type of control. A standalone smart lock door access setup suits a single door with a modest number of users, where convenience matters and the door itself is in good condition. Eyonic's own product guidance notes that smart locks work best when the door condition, user roles and backup method are reviewed before installation, and that for business use it usually checks whether a standalone smart lock or an access-control panel is more appropriate. That distinction matters in a shared office: a panel can manage many doors and many roles from one place, while a series of standalone locks means each door is administered separately and revocation becomes a manual chore.

Building an Approval and Revocation Workflow

Access control is a process, not a purchase. The process has four stages: request, approve, issue and revoke. Write down who performs each stage and what evidence is kept. A simple pattern for a small office is that the department head requests access for a named person, the office manager approves against the role-to-door worksheet, the installer or administrator issues the credential, and the same office manager is responsible for revocation on the person's last working day. Naming a single owner for the whole cycle prevents the common gap where a leaver keeps a working card because nobody was sure whose job it was.

Revocation deserves more attention than it usually gets. Build it into the exit checklist alongside returning keys and laptops, and set a target such as same-day removal of the credential as an example of good practice rather than a rule drawn from research. Temporary access should carry an expiry date from the moment it is issued, so a contractor's credential stops working without anyone needing to remember. Where the system supports schedules, use them for cleaners and part-timers so their access is limited to the hours they actually work.

Keep a simple register: who holds which credential, which doors it opens, when it was issued, when it expires and who approved it. Review the register against the worksheet on a fixed cycle — quarterly is a reasonable example for a small office — and reconcile it with your staff list. The register is also the document you consult when someone asks why a particular door opened at a particular time, so its accuracy has direct operational value.

Handling Visitors Without Losing the Thread

Visitors are the hardest part of shared office access because they are temporary, they arrive unpredictably, and they often need to move through spaces that staff treat as routine. The practical answer is a handover: the visitor's access is sponsored by a named staff member, it is limited to the areas the visit requires, and it ends when the visit ends. Reception should be able to see who is expected, who is sponsoring them and where they are going, without needing to phone around the building.

For low volumes, a paper or spreadsheet visitor log with a badge is entirely adequate, provided the badge is collected on departure and the log records time in and time out. For higher volumes or where the visitor must pass through a controlled door unaccompanied, a temporary credential with an expiry is cleaner. Either way, the sponsor carries responsibility for the visitor while they are on the premises, and that expectation should be written down and explained to staff rather than assumed.

Eyonic's work for a public office in Kuching focused on arrival flow, staff areas, visitor movement and dependable daily operation, which is a useful reminder that visitor handling is a flow problem before it is a hardware problem. Walk the visitor route yourself: where do they arrive, where do they wait, which door do they pass through, where do they go if the sponsor is delayed? If any step depends on someone being at their desk, the flow has a weak point. Fix the flow first, then choose the equipment that supports it.

Coordinating Access With CCTV and Remote Viewing

Access control and CCTV answer different questions, and a shared office usually needs both. Access control tells you who was authorised to pass a door and when a credential was used. CCTV security systems show what happened at the entrance, the reception desk and the corridor. Neither replaces the other, and planning them together avoids the situation where a camera points at a door but the door has no record of use, or a door logs an entry that no camera can put in context.

When planning camera placement, treat the controlled doors as priority views. A camera covering the main entrance should show the face of a person approaching, not the top of their head, and it should include enough of the surrounding area to show whether someone was alone or accompanied. Reception, the visitor waiting area and any door on the route to sensitive rooms are natural candidates. Eyonic's scope covers camera placement, recording and remote viewing for offices alongside other premises types, so the survey can address both access and coverage in one pass.

Remote CCTV viewing is genuinely useful for a shared office where the person responsible for access is not always on site, but it should be planned with the same discipline as the rest of the system. Decide who holds viewing access, on which devices, and what they are expected to do with what they see. NVR setup should include a check that recordings are actually being written and can be retrieved, because a system that records nothing is worse than no system at all — it creates false confidence. Test retrieval before handover, not after an incident.

Reviewing Audit Records Without Overreaching

An access system generates a record of movement, and that record is sensitive. Before you enable detailed logging, decide what you will review, how often, and who is allowed to look. A workable default for a small office is that the office manager reviews exceptions — failed attempts, out-of-hours entries, doors held open — rather than reading every event. Reviewing everything is neither practical nor respectful, and it tends to stop happening after the first busy week.

Privacy-conscious review means collecting only what you need and keeping it only as long as you need it. If the system retains a year of door events but your review cycle is monthly, ask whether the retention period is justified. Restrict access to the audit trail to named individuals, and record when someone exports or reviews it. Tell staff what is logged and why; a short written notice is usually enough and prevents the atmosphere of suspicion that grows when monitoring is discovered rather than explained. Visitors should be told what is recorded at the entrance.

Be careful not to overstate what the data proves. A credential used at a door shows that the credential was used, not necessarily who was holding it. That is one reason shared or loaned cards undermine the whole system, and one reason the register from the earlier section matters. Treat audit records as one input to a conversation, not as a verdict.

A Handover Checklist and Where Professional Support Fits

Before the system goes live, run a handover that proves each part of the plan works. A practical checklist, offered here as an example rather than a standard, might include: every role on the worksheet tested against every door it should and should not open; the revocation step rehearsed on a test credential; the backup entry method tested with the person who would actually use it; visitor credentials issued and expired on a trial basis; camera views checked at the times of day that matter; and recordings retrieved successfully. Score each item pass or fail and fix the failures before signing off.

Handover should also include the human side. Whoever administers the system needs to know how to add a user, remove one, change a schedule and find the audit trail, and they need that knowledge written down for the day they are unavailable. Keep the role-to-door worksheet, the credential register and the handover results together in one folder, physical or digital, so the next person to inherit the office can understand the setup without reconstructing it from scratch.
