A Burglary Alarm Handover Checklist When a Shop Changes Managers

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical handover checklist for shop managers in Sarawak taking over a burglary alarm system, covering ownership, notifications, installer-run tests and staff sign-off, with a briefing on linked CCTV security systems.

Why a manager change needs a formal alarm handover

When a shop changes managers, the alarm system often changes hands informally. The outgoing manager may hand over a keypad code, a fob or a phone number, and everyone assumes the rest is understood. That assumption is where confusion starts: who is now the named account holder, which phone numbers receive alerts, which staff members are authorised to arm and disarm, and what happens if the alarm activates after hours. A written handover turns those assumptions into a record that both managers, the staff and the alarm provider can work from.

This checklist is for premises managers in Sarawak planning an ordinary security-system handover. It is not a technical manual and it does not cover wiring, bypassing or system programming. Those tasks belong to the authorised installer or service provider. The goal here is to make sure the human side of the handover is complete: ownership, notifications, testing and confirmation. A burglary alarm is only useful if the right people know how to use it and who to call when it behaves unexpectedly.

Start with ownership and account details

Before any testing, confirm who owns the alarm account and who is authorised to make changes. In a shop, the account may be held by the business owner, the landlord or the company that operates the premises. The new manager needs to know which of these applies, because it affects who can request a change of contact details, add or remove users, or approve a service visit.

Ask the outgoing manager to list the following in writing: the alarm provider's name and the account reference, the date the system was last serviced, the names of any staff who currently hold user credentials, and the location of any written instructions or user guides. If the shop uses a smart lock door access system alongside the alarm, note who administers those permissions too. Eyonic's product guidance notes that smart locks work best when door condition, user roles and backup method are reviewed before installation, and the same discipline applies at handover: review who has access and how a backup entry is handled before the new manager takes over.

Do not rely on a verbal list. Write it down and have both managers sign it. If the alarm provider holds the record, ask for a copy of the current contact list so the new manager can compare it with what the outgoing manager believes is correct.

Map who receives notifications and who responds

A burglary alarm is only as good as its notification path. The new manager should know exactly which phone numbers receive alarm alerts, who is expected to respond at different times of day, and what the escalation order is if the first contact does not answer. This is not a technical setting; it is an operational decision that belongs to the business.

Build a simple notification table with three columns: time period, first contact, second contact. For example, during opening hours the first contact might be the shop supervisor and the second the new manager. After closing, the first contact might be the manager on duty and the second the business owner. Include the alarm provider's monitoring centre or service line if one is used, but keep the table focused on people the shop controls.

If the shop also uses remote CCTV viewing, note who is authorised to view footage and under what circumstances. Remote viewing is a useful feature, but it should not be handed over as an open-ended permission. The new manager should confirm which staff members can access the viewing app and whether that access is individual or shared. Shared logins make it difficult to know who viewed what, so prefer individual accounts where the system supports them.

Arrange an installer-run test and walkthrough

The most reliable way to confirm a handover is to have the installer or authorised service provider run a test with the new manager present. This is not a do-it-yourself exercise. The installer should demonstrate arming and disarming, show how a zone is triggered in a safe and controlled way, confirm that notifications reach the correct phones, and explain what the keypad or app displays when an event occurs.

Ask the installer to walk through the alarm zones on a simple floor plan. The new manager should be able to point to each zone and say what it covers: the front shutter, the back door, the stockroom, the safe area. If the shop has a CCTV system, the installer can also confirm camera placement and show how recorded footage is retrieved. Eyonic's site survey and camera placement services are part of this planning stage, and a handover is a good time to check that the original placement still matches how the shop is used today.

Keep the test practical. The goal is not to test every sensor in every mode, but to confirm that the new manager can operate the system confidently and knows who to call if something does not behave as expected. Record the date, the names of those present and any issues found. If a sensor needs attention, log it as an open item rather than leaving it as a verbal note.

Brief staff on the linked CCTV and access systems

A manager handover is also a staff briefing opportunity. The new manager should explain to the team what the alarm does, what they are expected to do at opening and closing, and how the alarm relates to the CCTV security systems and any door access controls in the shop. Keep the briefing practical and non-technical: who arms the system, who checks the cameras, who locks the back door, and who to tell if something looks wrong.

If the shop uses smart lock door access, the briefing should cover user permissions and the backup entry method. Eyonic's guidance notes that smart locks are good for controlled convenience when user permissions and backup entry are planned properly. At handover, the new manager should confirm which staff have access, whether any former staff still have active permissions, and how a backup entry is handled if a lock battery fails or a credential is lost. These are operational questions, not installation instructions.

For CCTV, the briefing should cover who reviews footage, how long recordings are kept, and what the process is for requesting a review. If the shop has remote viewing, remind staff that access is for authorised users only and that footage should not be shared outside the business without approval. The new manager should know how to contact the installer for NVR setup questions or recording adjustments.

A handover worksheet the new manager can sign

Use this worksheet as a simple record. It is an author's checklist, not a formal standard, and it can be adapted to the shop's size and complexity. Tick each item only when it has been confirmed in writing or demonstrated by the installer.

Ownership and account: alarm provider and account reference recorded; account holder identified; last service date noted; user guide location noted.

Users and permissions: current user list reviewed; former staff removed or flagged; smart lock permissions checked if applicable; backup entry method confirmed.

Notifications: notification table completed for opening hours and after hours; first and second contacts confirmed; remote viewing access list reviewed.

Installer test: arming and disarming demonstrated; zone walkthrough completed; notification test completed; CCTV retrieval demonstrated if applicable; open issues logged.

Staff briefing: opening and closing expectations explained; CCTV review process explained; door access rules explained; contact list for service and support shared.

Sign-off: outgoing manager, new manager and installer representative sign and date the worksheet. Keep one copy on site and send one to the account holder. If the shop later adds or changes systems, such as a new alarm package or an additional camera, update the worksheet rather than starting a separate file.

Close the handover with a short review

A week or two after the handover, the new manager should review the worksheet and confirm that everything still holds. Did notifications reach the right people during a real opening and closing? Did any staff member report confusion about the alarm or the cameras? Were any open issues from the installer test resolved? This short review catches small gaps before they become habits.

If the shop plans further work, such as additional camera placement, a new access-control panel or a change to the alarm zones, treat it as a new project with its own survey and handover. Eyonic's work with premises in Sarawak includes site survey, camera placement, NVR setup and remote viewing, and each of those elements should have a named owner after the handover. The checklist is not a one-time document; it is a living record that the new manager can update as the shop changes.

For questions about alarm packages, CCTV installation or access control, the next step is to speak with the provider directly. Keep the conversation focused on what the shop needs operationally, and ask for the handover record in writing so the next manager change is easier than this one.
