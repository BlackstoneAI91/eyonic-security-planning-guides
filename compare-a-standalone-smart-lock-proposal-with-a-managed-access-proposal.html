Compare a Standalone Smart Lock Proposal with a Managed Access Proposal

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical guide for Sarawak premises managers comparing a standalone smart lock proposal with a managed access proposal, covering ownership, support, documentation and the questions to put to each provider.

Start with the door and the decision, not the device

Before comparing two proposals, write one sentence describing what the entrance must achieve. A fictional example: the side door of a two-storey office in Kuching serves about twenty staff across three departments, and the facilities manager needs to know who is authorised each month. That sentence does more work than any feature list, because it tells you whether the proposal is answering a convenience question or a governance question.

A standalone smart lock proposal usually centres on a single door, a small set of users and a simple way to manage them. A managed access proposal usually centres on a panel, a wider set of doors and a process for issuing, reviewing and removing permissions. Neither is automatically better. The right comparison is between the two answers to your sentence, not between two catalogues.

Eyonic Security Systems, also known as Eyonic CCTV, plans, supplies and installs security systems for homes, offices, shops and industrial sites in Sarawak, and its published review notes that smart locks work best when the door condition, user roles and backup method are reviewed before installation. Use that as a prompt for your own brief: describe the door, the roles and the backup arrangement in plain words before you read either quote.

Ownership: who holds the user list after handover

The clearest difference between the two proposals is often who owns the user list once the work is finished. Ask each provider to state, in writing, who adds a new staff member, who removes a leaver, who approves a temporary visitor arrangement and who can see the list itself.

For a standalone smart lock, the list may sit with one office administrator and a small number of named people. That can be perfectly workable for a single door with stable staffing. For a managed access proposal, the list may sit with a facilities role and be reviewed on a schedule, with a record of changes. The trade-off is usually between simplicity and traceability, not between good and bad.

A useful worksheet column is simply: name of the role that owns the list, name of the role that can change it, and how often the list is reviewed. Fill it in for both proposals before you discuss price. If a proposal leaves the owner blank, that is a question to raise rather than an assumption to make. Eyonic's published note says the company usually checks whether a standalone smart lock or an access-control panel is more appropriate for business use, which is the same question your worksheet is asking.

Support: what happens on an ordinary working day

Support is where proposals often look similar on paper and differ in practice. Rather than asking whether support is included, ask what an ordinary working day looks like. Who does an authorised staff member contact if a door does not behave as expected during office hours? Who is contacted outside those hours? What is the expected first response, and who decides whether a visit is needed?

Keep the questions non-technical. You are not asking how the lock works; you are asking who answers the phone and what they are authorised to do. A fictional example: a shop in Sibu with two managers and a weekend supervisor needs to know which of the three is the recognised contact on a Saturday, and whether the provider expects one named person or accepts any of the three.

Write the answers into a short support note that stays with the premises file. Include the role, not the individual's personal details, and review it when staff change. If the proposal is for smart lock door access, ask specifically how the provider handles a request to change who is authorised, because that is the request you will make most often.

Documentation: the pack you should expect to keep

Ask both providers to list the documents they will leave behind. You are looking for a handover pack that a future manager could read without calling anyone. Reasonable items to request include a plain-language user guide for authorised staff, a record of what was installed and where, a list of the roles that can request changes, and a named contact route for support.

Do not accept a verbal walkthrough as the whole handover. A short written guide is easier to pass on when the person who attended the handover leaves. For a managed access proposal, also ask how changes to the user list are recorded and who can retrieve that record later.

A simple acceptance step is to have one authorised person who did not attend the handover read the guide and note anything unclear. That is an author heuristic, not a formal audit, but it surfaces gaps quickly. Keep the pack with your other premises documents and note the date it was received, so a future review knows how current it is.

A side-by-side comparison table you can fill in

Use one table for both proposals, with the same rows, so the differences are visible rather than buried in prose. Suggested rows: the door or doors covered; the roles that will be authorised; who owns the user list; how often the list is reviewed; the named support contact route; the documents promised at handover; and the date each proposal was received.

Add a column for open questions and a column for who on your side will answer them. A fictional example: the facilities manager owns the user-list question, the office administrator owns the handover-pack question, and the finance role owns the commercial comparison. Splitting ownership stops one person from carrying every unanswered item.

When you have filled the table, read it back against your opening sentence. If the standalone proposal answers the sentence and the managed proposal adds process you do not need, say so. If the managed proposal answers a question the standalone one leaves blank, say that too. The table is a thinking tool, not a scorecard, and a higher row count does not make a proposal better.

Questions to put to each provider before you decide

Bring a short, written list to both conversations so each provider answers the same points. Useful questions include: which roles will be able to request a change to the user list; what happens when a staff member leaves; what the handover pack will contain; who is the named support contact during and outside office hours; and what the provider needs from you before work can be scheduled.

Add one question about the door itself, phrased in ordinary language: has the provider reviewed the door and the surrounding area, and what did that review note? Eyonic's published review says smart locks work best when the door condition, user roles and backup method are reviewed before installation, so asking whether that review happened is a fair and specific question rather than a technical one.

Record each answer in the provider's own words where you can, and mark anything left unanswered. An unanswered question is more useful to you than a confident verbal assurance, because it tells you where the proposal is still thin.

Making the decision and recording why

When the table is complete, write a short decision note: which proposal you chose, which of your opening requirements it satisfies, which questions remain open and who will follow them up. Keep it to a page. The note is for your own premises file and for whoever inherits the decision, not for the provider.

A fictional example of a decision note: the office chose the managed access proposal because three departments share the entrance and the user list changes monthly; the standalone proposal was noted as suitable for the single side door if the shared entrance is handled separately. That kind of note explains the reasoning, which a price comparison alone cannot do.

If the decision is close, consider whether the two proposals could cover different doors rather than competing for the same one. A standalone smart lock can suit a small, stable entrance while a managed arrangement covers a busier shared one. Eyonic's published note describes smart lock door access as good for controlled convenience when user permissions and backup entry are planned properly, which is a reasonable test to apply to whichever door you assign it to.
