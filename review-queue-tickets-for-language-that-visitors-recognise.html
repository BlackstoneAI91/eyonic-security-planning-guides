Review queue tickets for language that visitors recognise

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A plain-language test for category names and counter prompts at a Sarawak service centre, using two fictional draft labels and a simple scoring worksheet.

Why ticket wording deserves its own review

A queue ticket is a small piece of public writing that a visitor reads under time pressure, often while standing, sometimes while holding a bag or a child's hand. If the category name on the ticket does not match the words on the wall, the visitor has to guess whether they chose correctly. That guess is the moment to design for.

This guide sets out a plain-language test you can run before a queue management system goes live, or before you reprint categories after a service change. It is written for premises managers in Sarawak planning ordinary security and building-system projects, where a queue system may sit alongside a keyphone system or an access control system at the same front desk.

The test uses a fictional service centre so nothing here depends on a real client. The aim is modest: compare two draft labels, record which one visitors recognise faster, and keep a written note of the decision. It is not an accessibility compliance check, and it does not replace advice from qualified staff on any regulated requirement.

Start from the words already on your walls

Before writing new category names, collect the language that already exists in the building. Photograph the noticeboard, the directional signs, the form headings and the counter labels. List every phrase a visitor might read on the way to the desk.

In the fictional example, a service centre in a mixed-use building has three counters: one for payments, one for new applications and one for general enquiries. The wall sign says "Pembayaran / Payments", the form says "Application for New Account" and the counter label says "Enquiries". A visitor holding a payment slip has three different phrases to reconcile.

The practical rule is that the ticket category should reuse the phrase the visitor has already seen, not a tidier internal name. If your back office calls something "Account Servicing", that is an internal label. The ticket should carry the public phrase.

This is the same discipline that helps when you brief other building systems. A site survey for CCTV security systems works better when the brief uses the words staff and visitors actually use for each area, rather than floor-plan codes.

Write two draft labels and compare them

Draft two versions of each category name. Version A uses your current internal wording. Version B uses the phrase from the wall sign. Keep both short enough to read on a small ticket.

Fictional example for the payments counter:

Version A: "Financial Transactions" Version B: "Payments / Pembayaran"

Fictional example for the applications counter:

Version A: "Onboarding" Version B: "New application"

Now run a simple recognition check with colleagues who do not work at that desk. Show each label on its own, ask what the person would expect to do at that counter, and write down the answer in their words. Do not explain the label first. The gap between what you intended and what they said is your evidence.

Treat this as an author heuristic, not research. Five or six colleagues is enough to expose an obviously confusing label; it is not a statistically meaningful sample and should not be presented as one. Record the date, who took part by role (not name) and the exact wording tested, so a later reviewer can see what changed.

Test the counter prompt, not just the category

The category name gets the visitor to the right queue. The counter prompt gets them to stand in the right place and to know when it is their turn. Both need the same plain-language treatment.

Write the prompt as a short instruction in the visitor's terms. Compare:

Draft prompt A: "Proceed to assigned service point upon display of reference." Draft prompt B: "Please go to counter 2 when your number shows."

Draft B names the action, the place and the trigger. It also avoids the word "reference", which may mean a ticket number, an account number or a receipt to different readers.

For a bilingual setting, decide deliberately which language leads and keep the order consistent across the ticket, the display and the counter sign. Mixing order between surfaces is a common source of hesitation.

Keep a written record of the final prompt text and who approved it. That record is useful later when a display is replaced or a new counter is added, and it belongs in the same handover folder as your other building-system documentation.

Build a short scoring worksheet

A worksheet turns a subjective argument into a comparable note. Score each draft label on four criteria, using a simple 1 to 3 scale where 1 means unclear and 3 means clear.

Criterion one: matches a phrase already visible in the building. Criterion two: understood by a colleague outside that desk without explanation. Criterion three: short enough to read on one line of a ticket. Criterion four: consistent with the counter prompt and the display.

Fictional result: "Payments / Pembayaran" scores 3, 3, 3, 3. "Financial Transactions" scores 1, 2, 2, 1. The worksheet does not decide for you, but it shows where the weakness sits.

Add two columns that are easy to forget: who owns the wording, and when it will next be reviewed. A label that nobody owns drifts as soon as a counter is repurposed.

This is an example scoring scheme, not a validated instrument. Adapt the criteria to your own service and say so in the note, so a future reader does not mistake it for an external standard.

Keep the review separate from the equipment decision

Wording review and equipment selection are different decisions, and mixing them slows both down. The wording test tells you what the ticket should say. It does not tell you which queue management system to buy, and a good label will not compensate for a poorly placed display.

Where the two do meet is in the requirement you hand to a supplier. Once you have approved labels, write them into the requirement as fixed text, along with the counter prompts and the language order. That way a supplier quotes against your wording rather than a default template.

Record the decision and hand it over

Close the review with a one-page record. Include the categories tested, the two draft versions, the worksheet scores, the final approved wording, the approver's role and the review date. Keep it where the next person will look: with the front-desk procedures, not buried in a project folder.

Add a short note on what was not tested. If you did not check the wording with visitors who use a wheelchair, who read slowly or who use a language other than the two you tested, say so plainly. That honesty is more useful than an implied claim of completeness, and it gives the next reviewer a clear starting point.

Finally, set a trigger for re-review rather than a date alone. A new counter, a renamed service or a change of building entrance are all reasons to reopen the wording. A calendar date catches the rest.

Done this way, the review is small, repeatable and easy to defend: a written test, a recorded decision and a named owner. That is a reasonable standard for a ticket that a visitor reads in five seconds.
