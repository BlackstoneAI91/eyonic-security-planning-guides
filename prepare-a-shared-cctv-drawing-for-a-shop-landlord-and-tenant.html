Preparing a Shared CCTV Drawing for a Shop Landlord and Tenant

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical guide for Sarawak premises managers on building one agreed CCTV drawing that separates existing devices, requested camera locations and consent to change, so landlord and tenant sign off the same plan before any CCTV installation work begins.

Why one shared drawing beats two separate briefs

A shop unit usually has two parties with an interest in the cameras: the landlord who owns the building fabric and the tenant who runs the business inside it. When each writes their own brief, the installer receives two overlapping wish lists and no single record of what already exists. The result is a quote that quietly assumes things nobody agreed to.

A shared drawing fixes this by putting three different kinds of information on one page, each visually distinct. First, existing devices: what is already mounted, who owns it, and whether it stays. Second, requested locations: where each party would like a camera, marked as a request rather than a decision. Third, consent notes: who must agree before a requested location becomes a committed one.

The drawing is not a technical schematic. It is a coordination document. Keep it at the level of rooms, walls, doors and approximate positions, and leave lens choice, cabling routes and mounting hardware to the supplier's own survey. That separation matters because the drawing's job is to record agreement, not to instruct anyone on how to install.

Set up the drawing's three layers before anyone marks a camera

Before a single camera position is discussed, agree the drawing's structure. A simple approach is three layers on one floor sketch, each with its own legend colour and its own owner column in a side table.

Layer one, existing devices. For each item already present, record a short reference, the room, whether it is landlord-owned or tenant-owned, and its status: retained, removed, or undecided. Do not record serial numbers, login details or access credentials on a shared document. A reference code is enough for the parties to talk about the same device.

Layer two, requested locations. Each request gets a number, a room, a one-line purpose in plain words (for example, "view of the rear service door"), and who requested it. Marking a request is not approval. Say so in the legend, because the most common misunderstanding in shared drawings is a request being read as a commitment.

Layer three, consent and constraints. This is where the drawing earns its keep. Note who must consent to a change affecting the building fabric, who must consent to a change affecting the tenant's fit-out, and any location that is explicitly out of scope. Keep the notes factual and short.

A worked example: a fictional unit in a row of shops has two existing cameras at the front. The tenant requests one at the stockroom door; the landlord requests one covering the rear lane side of the unit. Both appear as numbered requests, not as approved positions, until the consent column is filled in.

Separate existing devices from requested ones in the handoff table

The drawing itself is only half the handoff. The other half is a table that travels with it, so a reader can work through the document without needing to interpret symbols. Keep the table to six columns and one row per item.

Columns: reference, layer (existing or requested), room, plain-language purpose, owner or requester, and status. Status values should be a short fixed list, for example proposed, agreed, deferred, or out of scope. A fixed list prevents the slow drift into vague wording that makes a drawing hard to compare between revisions.

Two rules keep the table honest. First, an existing device's owner is recorded as it actually is, not as either party assumes it should be. If ownership is unclear, write "to confirm" rather than guessing. Second, a requested location never moves to agreed without a named person and a date in the consent column.

This is also where you record what the drawing deliberately leaves out. If a location is excluded because it would look into a neighbouring unit or a shared corridor, note the exclusion and the reason in one line. Exclusions are decisions, and decisions belong in the record.

When the table and drawing are ready, they form a single package. The installer quotes from the package, and any later change is a new revision of the package rather than a verbal adjustment on site.

Agree who consents to what before the CCTV site survey

Consent is the part most shared drawings get wrong, usually because it is left implicit. Make it explicit with a short consent matrix attached to the drawing. List the change types down one side and the parties across the top, and mark who must agree for each type.

Typical change types for a shop unit include: adding a camera to an existing mounting point, adding a new mounting point on the building fabric, changing what an existing camera views, relocating the recorder, and removing a device. For each, the matrix records whether the landlord, the tenant, or both must agree, and whether the installer needs to confirm feasibility first.

Keep the matrix about agreement, not about method. It should not describe how anything is fixed, wired or configured. Its only job is to answer the question "who says yes?" before work is scheduled.

Bring the matrix to the CCTV site survey. A survey is far more productive when the person walking the premises already knows which locations are consented, which are pending, and which are excluded. The survey can then focus on feasibility and coverage rather than reopening settled questions.

A useful habit: at the end of the survey, ask the supplier to mark on your drawing which requested locations they consider straightforward and which need further discussion. That single annotation turns the survey into a documented step rather than a conversation.

Keep revisions comparable so quotes can be compared

A shared drawing will change. The value of the document depends on those changes being traceable, so build a revision discipline from the first version.

Give every revision a number, a date, and a one-line summary of what changed. Keep the previous version. When a requested location moves from proposed to agreed, that is a revision. When a location is dropped, that is also a revision. The summary line should be specific enough that a reader can tell the difference between two versions without opening both.

This discipline pays off at quote stage. When two suppliers quote against the same revision number, differences between their proposals are easier to attribute: one may have priced a location the other excluded, or assumed a recorder position that the drawing places elsewhere. Without a shared revision, those differences hide inside prose.

A short comparison table helps. List each requested location, then a column per supplier showing whether it is included, excluded, or noted as needing further survey. Add a final column for questions to raise. This keeps the comparison about scope rather than about presentation.

For the installation stage itself, the same drawing becomes the reference for what was agreed. A supplier providing CCTV installation in Sarawak can work from a fixed list of consented locations, and the parties can check the finished work against the drawing rather than against memory.

A short pre-handoff checklist for the premises manager

Before the drawing package leaves your desk, run a five-minute check. These are author heuristics, not an industry standard, but they catch the errors that most often cause a second round of questions.

One: every item on the drawing appears in the table, and every row in the table appears on the drawing. Two: no requested location is marked agreed without a named person and a date. Three: existing devices have an owner recorded, even if that owner is "to confirm". Four: exclusions are written down with a one-line reason. Five: the revision number and date are on both the drawing and the table, and they match.

Then check the document for information that should not be shared. A drawing circulated between landlord, tenant and supplier should not carry access credentials, personal contact details, or anything that describes how to reach or operate the equipment. Keep those in a separate controlled record held by the responsible party.

Finally, decide who holds the master copy. One named role should own the current revision, and everyone else should work from a copy clearly marked with its revision number. When the package is complete and consistent, it gives every party the same picture of what exists, what is wanted, and who has agreed to what.
