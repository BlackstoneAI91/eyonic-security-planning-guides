CCTV Installation Planning Around Staggered Loading-Bay Hours

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical planning guide for Sarawak premises managers who need camera placement, recording and handover decisions to match two staggered loading windows and a shared yard.

Start with the loading timetable, not the camera list

A loading bay is not one scene. It is a sequence of scenes that repeats on a timetable. Before anyone climbs a ladder, write down the timetable in plain language: which bay opens first, which vehicle types arrive, where they stop, which door they use, and when the yard is shared between two windows. For a small warehouse with two staggered windows, that might mean an early window for inbound stock and a later window for outbound collections, with the shared yard busy in between.

This timetable becomes the spine of the whole plan. Every camera position, recording setting and acceptance test should be traceable back to a line on it. If a camera cannot be justified by a line on the timetable, it is probably covering something nobody has agreed to review.

A CCTV site survey is the right moment to walk the yard at the actual times of each window, not at a quiet mid-morning. Note where drivers stand, where paperwork changes hands, where vehicles turn, and where the yard is shared. Build a camera placement worksheet keyed to each window

Use one row per camera and one column per question. Keep it on a single page so it can be read at the gate.

Example columns: camera reference; bay or yard zone; which loading window it serves; what the view must show; what it must not show (neighbours, staff rest areas, public road beyond the boundary); light conditions at that window; mounting height and angle; recording stream and retention; acceptance test reference.

A worked example for a fictional two-window warehouse might read: Camera A covers Bay 1 door and the approach lane during the early window; Camera B covers Bay 2 door and the shared yard during the later window; Camera C covers the vehicle gate and the pedestrian door together so that gate and door activity can be read in one view. Camera D might cover the parts store door that sits between the two bays.

Two practical rules help. First, place cameras so that a person or vehicle is seen approaching, not only arriving. Second, avoid a view where a vehicle body blocks the door it is serving. Camera placement is a design decision, and it is worth testing candidate positions with a temporary camera or a phone at the same height before committing.

Note lighting changes across the two windows

Staggered windows often sit at different times of day, so the same yard can look completely different between them. Record, for each window, whether the scene is in direct sun, shade, deep shadow under a canopy, or lit by yard lamps. Note the direction of the sun at each window and whether headlights from arriving vehicles point into a camera.

This matters because a view that reads clearly at one window may wash out or go dark at the other. Write the lighting note next to the camera row, and agree with the installer which settings will be checked at each window during commissioning. Where a window falls after dark, confirm that yard lighting is on and that the camera view is still usable with vehicles moving through it.

Keep the lighting note factual: time, sky condition, lamp status, and what the view looked like. Do not guess at lux figures unless someone measures them. The goal is a record that lets a reviewer understand why a setting was chosen.

Set an acceptance-footage test for each bay

Acceptance is not a walkthrough. It is a short, repeatable test that produces footage someone can review against the worksheet.

Example test per bay, run during the actual window: a vehicle enters the yard, stops at the bay, a person steps out, walks to the door, and returns. Capture the whole sequence. Then review it and ask four questions: can you read the vehicle approach, can you see the door activity, can you identify the time and camera, and is the footage clear enough for the purpose you agreed?

Record the result as pass, pass with note, or fail, with a one-line reason. Keep the test clips in a named folder so the handover packet has evidence. If a view fails, the fix might be an angle change, a lighting adjustment, or a different camera position, and it should be retested at the same window rather than at a convenient time.

This is also the moment to confirm recording behaviour. NVR setup should be checked for stream quality, retention period and time accuracy, because a clip that cannot be found or trusted is not useful evidence.

How gate and door activity interacts with camera views

The vehicle gate and the pedestrian door are the two points where the loading timetable meets access. Plan them together. A camera that sees the gate but not the person walking from gate to door leaves a gap; a camera that sees the door but not the approach leaves another.

Where a site uses controlled entry, the access decision and the camera view should be reviewed side by side. Smart locks work best when the door condition, user roles and backup method are reviewed before installation, and for business use it is worth checking whether a standalone smart lock or an access-control panel is more appropriate. That review belongs in the same conversation as camera placement, because the door that opens is the door the camera must see.

Keep the two workstreams distinct in the plan: one line for the camera view, one line for the access method, and a note on how they align. If gate automation is part of the upgrade, sequence it so that gate testing and camera acceptance do not compete for the same yard at the same window.

Agree who reviews footage, and for how long it is kept

A handover checklist should name people, not roles alone. Write down who reviews footage day to day, who approves a review request, who exports a clip, and who can change recording settings. Add a deputy for each, because a single name is a single point of failure.

Then agree retention in plain terms: how many days of recording the site expects to hold, where exports are stored, and who deletes them. Retention is a site decision, so record the decision and the date rather than assuming a default. Note also how remote CCTV viewing is used: which devices, which users, and what happens when someone leaves. Access should be removed on the same day as the departure, and the checklist should show that step.

A short handover packet works well: the timetable, the camera worksheet, the lighting notes, the acceptance results, the retention decision, and the named reviewers. Keep one copy with the site and one with the installer.

A closing checklist for the staggered-window plan

Before sign-off, confirm each of these in writing:

1. The loading timetable is written down and matches the windows actually observed. 2. Every camera row names the window it serves and what the view must show. 3. Lighting notes exist for both windows, including after-dark conditions where relevant. 4. An acceptance clip has been captured and reviewed for each bay during its own window. 5. Recording retention and time accuracy have been checked. 6. Gate and door views have been reviewed alongside the access method. 7. Named reviewers, a deputy, and an export process are recorded. 8. Remote viewing users are listed, with a removal step for leavers.

Treat the checklist as an author's heuristic, not a standard. Adapt it to the site. For a Sarawak site, the practical next step is a survey at the real loading windows, so the plan is built from what the yard actually does.
