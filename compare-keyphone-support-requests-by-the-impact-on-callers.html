Compare keyphone support requests by the impact on callers

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical guide for Sarawak premises managers who need to sort keyphone support requests by how much they affect callers, using an issue form that records caller intent, observed outcome and the responsible staff role without diagnosing equipment or changing call configuration.

Start with a caller-impact issue form, not a fault theory

Before anyone ranks a support request, write one sentence describing what the form is for. A workable example: the form exists so that the premises manager, the reception team and the appointed service contact can discuss one keyphone system issue using the same words, without guessing at causes. Keep the form to five fields. First, a neutral reference such as the date and the counter or department affected. Second, caller intent: what the person was trying to do, in plain language, such as reaching a colleague, asking for a department, or waiting for a callback. Third, observed outcome: what the caller actually experienced, described only as they would describe it. Fourth, the staff role that noticed it, not a person's name. Fifth, the time window. Resist a sixth field for suspected cause. Cause belongs to whoever is qualified to investigate, and a form that invites diagnosis tends to collect confident guesses that later mislead everyone. The purpose of this page is comparison, so the form should produce entries that can be sorted side by side. If two entries cannot be compared without a conversation, the fields are too loose. Test the form on one fictional example before circulating it: a caller who wanted the accounts desk and was told the line was busy. That single sentence already gives you intent, outcome and a role to follow up with.

Sort requests into three caller-impact bands

A simple three-band sort is enough for most premises, and it keeps the discussion about callers rather than about equipment. Band one is a caller who could not complete their purpose at all: they gave up, were cut off, or were sent to the wrong place and no one noticed. Band two is a caller who completed their purpose but with delay or repeated explanation. Band three is an internal observation with no known caller effect, such as a handset that looks worn or a label that reads oddly. The bands are an author heuristic, not a standard, so adjust the wording to your own site and keep it stable once agreed. The value of the sort is that it forces a question: who can confirm the caller's experience? A band one entry should name the role that can describe what the caller said or did. A band three entry should say plainly that no caller effect is known, so nobody later treats it as urgent by accident. Where a site already runs other security and communications work, the same discipline helps. That habit of recording what was observed, rather than what was assumed, transfers neatly to a caller-impact form.

Write the observed outcome in caller language

The most common weakness in a support request is a description written in staff shorthand. "Line fault" tells a reader nothing about the caller. "Caller pressed the department option and heard a repeated message, then hung up" tells a reader what happened without naming a cause. Ask the person who took the call to write the outcome as a short sequence: what the caller did, what they heard or were told, and how the contact ended. Three clauses are usually enough. Avoid words that imply a diagnosis, such as faulty, down, misconfigured or overloaded. Those words belong in a later conversation with the service contact, not in the first record. Also avoid writing the caller's name or number into the form. A neutral reference and the department are sufficient for comparison, and keeping personal details out makes the form easier to share with the people who need to sort it. If a caller asked for a callback, record that as intent and note whether the callback happened, without storing the number in the shared form. This is the same restraint that applies elsewhere in premises documentation: describe the business question, keep private details in the approved place, and let the responsible role decide what more is needed. A form that reads cleanly in caller language is also the form that a new receptionist can fill in correctly on their first day.

Name the responsible role for each request

Every entry should end with a role, not a name: reception supervisor, facilities coordinator, service contact, department head. Roles survive staff changes and are easier to publish on a shared page. For each band, agree in advance who acts. A band one entry might go to the reception supervisor the same working day, with the service contact informed. A band two entry might be reviewed at a weekly huddle. A band three entry might simply wait for the next routine review. These timings are examples, not commitments, and each site should set its own. The role field also prevents a quiet failure: a request that everyone has read and nobody owns. When a request is passed on, add one line saying which role now holds it and what they were asked to do, without describing any technical step. If the request concerns a keyphone system that sits alongside other building services, keep the handoff separate from those services so that a telephony question does not stall a security task, and vice versa. A short responsibility line per entry is enough. The test is whether a reader joining the site next month could tell, from the form alone, who was expected to act and whether they did.

Keep configuration changes out of the comparison

This guide deliberately stops at comparison. Deciding what to change on a keyphone system, and making that change, belongs to the people responsible for it under the site's arrangements. The comparison page should never become a place where staff record proposed settings, dial codes or workarounds, because those notes age badly and can be misread as instructions. Instead, record the question that remains open. For example: "Callers to the accounts desk report a repeated message; the reception supervisor has asked the service contact whether the published department list still matches what callers hear." That sentence carries the intent, the outcome, the role and the open question, and it gives no technical direction. The same restraint applies to the wider premises record. Where a site also holds CCTV security systems, published material describes planning, camera placement, recording and remote viewing as services, and the operational detail stays with the appointed provider rather than in a shared log. Treat the keyphone comparison the same way: the log holds the caller's experience and the open question, and the qualified role holds the answer.

Review the sorted list on a fixed rhythm

A comparison is only useful if it is read. Set a short review, weekly or fortnightly, and keep the agenda to four questions. Which band one entries appeared since the last review, and were they confirmed by a role that spoke to the caller? Which band two entries repeated, and does the repetition suggest the published department list needs a plain-language check? Which entries have no owner, and who should take them? Which open questions are still waiting on the service contact? Keep the answers to a few lines each and date them. Over a month or two, the sorted list becomes a record of caller experience that a premises manager can take into a planning conversation, including any wider review of building services. If the site is also considering a site survey for other systems, the same list shows which public-facing points matter most to visitors, which is useful context to bring along. Do not turn the review into a scoring exercise with invented targets. The aim is a readable trail: what callers experienced, which role responded, and what remains open. A fictional example of a closing note: "Two accounts-desk entries this fortnight, both confirmed by the reception supervisor; the department list question is with the service contact; no owner needed for the worn handset note." That is enough for the next reader to pick up the thread.

A short worksheet to agree before the first entry

Use this worksheet once, with the people who will actually fill in the form, and keep the agreed answers on one page. One: what are the three bands called at this site, in caller language? Two: which role confirms a caller's experience for each band? Three: what is the review rhythm, and who chairs it? Four: where does the form live, and who may read it? Five: what is deliberately excluded, such as names, numbers, suspected causes and proposed settings? Six: how will a reader tell an open question from a closed one? Seven: what happens when a request concerns more than one service, and which role holds the thread? Answer each in a sentence. If an answer needs a paragraph, the process is probably too complicated for a busy counter. The worksheet is also the moment to note who at the site is qualified to discuss the keyphone system itself, so that the comparison stays a record and the technical conversation goes to the right place. Review the worksheet after the first month and change only what caused confusion. A stable form, sorted by caller impact and owned by named roles, gives a Sarawak premises manager something rare: a plain account of how callers actually experienced the office, written without guesswork and without touching the configuration.
