Security System Project Brief for a Sarawak Premises: A One-Page Approval Template

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical one-page brief template for Sarawak premises managers, tying CCTV, gate, alarm and access decisions into a single approval document with a decision log and a named owner per item.

Why one brief beats four separate quotes

Security work at a typical Sarawak premises rarely arrives as a single decision. A shop manager may ask for cameras first, then a gate motor, then an alarm, then door access, and each request travels on its own quotation with its own assumptions. By the time all four are approved, nobody holds a single document that says what the site is meant to achieve, who owns each item, or what counts as finished.

A one-page project brief fixes that. It is not a technical specification and it does not replace a site survey. It is the approval document that sits above the quotes: the problem statement, the scope boundaries, the named owner for each decision, and the acceptance test for each system. Suppliers can then quote against the same brief, and you can compare proposals on your requirements rather than on their packaging.

Eyonic Security Systems is the brand context for this template. The company plans, supplies and installs monitoring systems with camera placement, recording and remote viewing for homes, offices, shops and industrial sites, so a brief that covers CCTV alongside gate, alarm and access decisions matches the way these projects are usually discussed. Keep the brief short enough that a manager can read it in one sitting and sign it without a meeting.

A useful discipline: write the brief before you invite quotes, then attach it to every request. If a supplier's proposal contradicts the brief, that is a conversation, not a silent variation.

The six blocks of the brief

Use the same six blocks for every premises, whether it is a shophouse, an office floor or a small industrial unit. The blocks are deliberately plain so that a non-technical approver can follow them.

1. Purpose and risk statement. Two or three sentences on what the system is for: deterrence, evidence after an incident, controlling who enters, or monitoring a yard. Avoid vague goals such as improve security; name the specific concern.

2. Scope in and out. List what is included and, just as importantly, what is excluded. A brief that says cameras at the front counter and loading area, and explicitly excludes the neighbouring unit's corridor, prevents scope creep later.

3. Site facts. Address, premises type, operating hours, who is on site after hours, power availability at proposed equipment points, and any known lighting changes between day and night. These are the facts a CCTV site survey will confirm, so record what you already know.

4. Systems in scope. Tick only what applies: CCTV security systems, gate automation, burglar alarm, door access, keyphone, queue management. Each ticked system gets its own owner and acceptance test in block five.

5. Owners and acceptance. One named person per system, plus the test that proves it works. For cameras, that might be a recorded clip of a person walking through each entrance at the agreed time of day. For a gate, a documented sequence run with the installer present.

6. Decision log. A dated table of decisions made, by whom, and what changed as a result. This is the block that saves arguments months later.

Keep each block to a few lines. If a block needs a page, it belongs in an appendix, not the brief.

Writing the CCTV section so the survey has something to test

CCTV is usually the largest line in the brief and the one most likely to drift. Write the CCTV section around outcomes rather than camera counts. A sentence such as "record a clear view of every person entering through the front and side doors, and of vehicles at the rear yard" gives an installer something to design against. A sentence such as "install eight cameras" gives them a number to hit, whether or not the views are useful.

Three sub-items belong in this section. First, camera placement intent: which areas must be covered, which must not be covered, and any privacy limits such as a neighbouring shopfront or a staff rest area. Second, recording expectations: how many days of footage you expect to keep, and who is allowed to review it. Third, remote viewing: who needs to see the cameras from outside the premises, on what device, and who holds those credentials.

These map directly to the services a CCTV installation typically covers, including site survey, camera placement, NVR setup and remote viewing. Naming them in the brief means the proposal can be checked line by line at handover rather than discovered missing afterwards.

Gate, alarm and door access in the same document

The value of a single brief shows up when more than one system touches the same entrance. A gate, a door and a camera can all point at the same five metres of driveway, and if they are bought separately nobody checks that the camera sees the gate, that the gate's safety sensors are not blocked by a camera pole, or that the alarm zone covering the gate is named consistently.

For gate work, the brief should record what the site needs the gate to do: vehicle volume at peak, pedestrian separation, and whether the gate is shared with another occupant. Leave the engineering to the installer. The companion article on preparing an auto gate quote for a shared compound with a delivery lane covers measurement requests and acceptance documentation in more depth.

For a burglar alarm system, the brief records the zones you expect to be named, who receives notifications, and who is authorised to arm and disarm. Do not put codes or procedures in the brief; record the ownership and the handover requirement instead.

For smart lock door access, the brief should state the door or doors in scope, the user groups who need entry, and the fallback method if the lock is unavailable. Smart locks work best when the door condition, user roles and backup method are reviewed before installation, and for business use it is worth asking whether a standalone smart lock or an access-control panel is the more appropriate fit. That question belongs in the brief as a decision to be recorded, not assumed.

One owner per entrance is the rule. If the gate and the adjacent door have different owners, add a line saying who arbitrates when the two systems interact.

A worked example: fictional shophouse with a rear yard

Consider a fictional two-storey shophouse in Sarawak with a front retail counter, a side entrance used by staff, and a small rear yard with a roller shutter. The manager wants cameras, a gate motor on the yard shutter, an alarm and a keypad door on the side entrance. The following is an author's example of how the brief might read, not a recommendation for any real site.

Purpose: record everyone entering the front and side doors, and vehicles at the rear yard; control staff entry at the side door; alert the duty manager when the premises are armed outside trading hours.

Scope in: four camera positions covering front counter, side door inside and outside, and rear yard. Scope out: the neighbouring unit's corridor and the public five-foot way beyond the shopfront boundary.

Site facts: trading hours 9am to 7pm; one staff member on site until 8pm for closing; rear yard lit by a single wall lamp that is switched off at closing; power points available at the counter and in the rear store.

Systems and owners: CCTV security systems owned by the shop manager, acceptance test is a recorded clip of a person walking through each door at 7pm and again at 9pm. Gate owned by the landlord's representative, acceptance test is a documented open and close cycle with the installer present. Alarm owned by the shop manager, acceptance test is a walk test of each named zone. Side door access owned by the shop manager, acceptance test is entry by two test users and one revoked user.

Decision log: dated entries for the choice of camera positions, the decision to exclude the neighbouring corridor, and the choice of fallback entry method for the side door.

That is roughly one page. It is enough for three suppliers to quote against the same requirements.

The decision log and the named owner

The decision log is the part most briefs skip, and the part that matters most when staff change. Keep it as a simple table with four columns: date, decision, owner, and what it affects. Every entry should name a person, not a department.

Typical entries look like this. On a given date, the manager decides that the rear yard camera will cover the shutter and the approach, not the neighbouring wall; owner is the shop manager; affects camera placement and the privacy note. Later, the landlord's representative decides that the gate will be operated by the existing remote rather than a new one; owner is the landlord's representative; affects the gate scope and the handover test.

Two rules keep the log honest. First, no decision is recorded without an owner. Second, any change to scope after the brief is signed gets a log entry before work proceeds, not after. If a supplier proposes an extra camera, the log entry records who approved it and which acceptance test it joins.

At handover, the log becomes the index for the handover pack. Each system's acceptance test should reference the log entries that shaped it. When a new manager takes over, the log tells them what was decided and why, which is far more useful than a folder of quotations.

Keep the log in the same document as the brief, or in a clearly linked appendix. A log stored in someone's email is not a log.

A pre-approval checklist you can score

Before the brief goes for approval, run this short checklist. The scoring below is an author's heuristic for internal review, not a researched standard. Score each item yes or no, and treat any no as a reason to revise rather than to approve with a note.

One: does the purpose statement name a specific concern rather than a general goal? Two: does the scope list at least one exclusion? Three: is every system in scope assigned exactly one named owner? Four: does every system have an acceptance test that can be observed, not just described? Five: are the site facts recorded, including lighting changes between day and night? Six: does the CCTV section state recording expectations and who may review footage? Seven: does the remote viewing line name who holds credentials? Eight: is the decision log started, with at least the first entry dated?

A brief that scores eight out of eight is ready to attach to quotation requests. A brief that scores five or six is still useful, but expect questions during approval and be ready to fill the gaps.

Finally, decide where the brief lives. A single shared file with a clear owner and a review date is enough. The goal is not paperwork for its own sake; it is that the person approving the spend can see, on one page, what is being bought, who is accountable, and how everyone will know it works.
