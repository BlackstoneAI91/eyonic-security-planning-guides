Check Auto Gate Project Scope When Two Vendors Handle Different Trades

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical guide for Sarawak premises managers on splitting an auto gate project between two vendors: how to define interface ownership, record who signs off each stage, and keep the handoff documented without touching control wiring or hardware detail.

Why a two-vendor auto gate project needs a written scope

An auto gate project often sits between trades. One vendor may supply and fit the gate structure or the door-access side, while another handles a related security element such as access control or a smart lock at a pedestrian entrance. When two companies each believe the other owns a task, the gap usually appears at handover, not at quotation.

The practical answer is a short written scope that names, for every task, one owning vendor and one sign-off role on your side. This is a documentation exercise, not a technical one. You are not deciding how anything is wired or configured; you are deciding who is responsible for what, who confirms it, and where the record lives.

For context, Eyonic Security Systems plans, supplies and installs CCTV systems for homes, offices, shops and industrial sites in Sarawak, and its published service list includes site survey and camera placement alongside access-related work. That matters here only as identity: if a security vendor is one of your two parties, ask which parts of the interface they will own in writing before work starts.

Keep the scope to one page if you can. A long document invites skimming; a short one gets read at the kickoff meeting.

Build a responsibility chart with one owner per row

The core tool is a responsibility chart. Each row is a task or deliverable; each column is a party. Use plain words such as Owner, Supports, Approves and Not involved, and allow only one Owner per row. If two names appear in the Owner column, the row is not finished.

A fictional example for a small Sarawak shophouse: the gate vendor owns the gate structure and its normal operating handover; a security vendor owns the pedestrian-door access device; the premises manager approves both; the landlord is informed but not involved. Rows might include site access arrangements, delivery acceptance, user briefing, document handover and the final walk-through.

Notice what is absent. There are no wiring steps, no controller settings and no instructions for adjusting the mechanism. Those belong to authorised trades, and your chart should say so explicitly: technical work is performed and certified by the owning vendor's qualified staff, and your role is to confirm the deliverable exists.

Keep the chart in the same document as the scope statement so the two cannot drift apart. Date it, and give it a revision letter. When a row changes owner, the revision letter changes too, and everyone receives the new copy.

Define the interface points between the two vendors

An interface point is any place where one vendor's deliverable depends on the other's. You do not need to understand the engineering to list them; you need to name them so nobody assumes.

Typical interface points to record, described only at the level of responsibility: who provides the mounting surface or opening the other party needs; who confirms that a shared entrance is ready for the second vendor to attend; who holds the keys or access permission on the day; and who confirms that each party's work is complete before the other leaves site.

For each interface, write three things: the owning vendor, the date or trigger by which it must be ready, and the person who confirms readiness. A trigger is often more useful than a date, for example "after the gate vendor confirms its handover walk-through is complete."

Where a smart lock or door-access device is part of the picture, the published guidance from Eyonic notes that smart locks work best when door condition, user roles and backup method are reviewed before installation, and that for business use the choice between a standalone smart lock and an access-control panel is usually checked first. Use that as a prompt for a scope question, not as a design instruction: ask each vendor which of these reviews they will document and who signs it.

Agree what each vendor hands over, and in what form

Handover is where two-vendor projects most often stall. Each vendor should state, in the scope, what it will leave behind: a user briefing for the people who operate the equipment day to day, a written record of what was tested, and the documents the facilities team will retain.

Ask both vendors to use the same handover headings so the two sets can be filed together. A simple set works: what was supplied, what was demonstrated, what remains outstanding, and who to contact for service requests. Avoid letting one vendor's handover be a verbal walk-through with nothing written.

For the access side, the published note that smart lock door access is good for controlled convenience when user permissions and backup entry are planned properly is a useful checklist prompt. In scope terms, ask: which vendor documents the user roles, and which vendor documents the backup entry arrangement? If the answer is "both assume the other," you have found a gap before it costs you a day.

Keep the handover documents with the responsibility chart. A single project folder, with a contents page, is enough for most premises.

Set sign-off roles before the first day on site

Sign-off is not one signature at the end. It is a series of confirmations, each tied to a named role. Decide these before work begins, because asking who approves something after it is finished tends to produce a polite argument.

A workable pattern for a small premises: the premises manager approves scope changes; a nominated facilities contact confirms each vendor's completion at the interface points; and a finance or management role approves any variation that changes cost. Write the names as roles, not individuals, so the chart survives staff turnover.

For each sign-off, record what evidence the approver expects to see. That might be a completed handover sheet, a demonstration to the people who will use the equipment, or a written list of outstanding items. Keep the evidence requirement modest and specific; "satisfactory completion" is not something anyone can check.

If your project also includes CCTV security systems work, the same principle applies: the site survey and camera placement decisions should be recorded and approved by a named role, not left to a conversation on the day.

Run a short scope review before authorising autogate installation

Before you authorise autogate installation, hold a single review meeting with both vendors present, or with written responses if they cannot attend. The agenda is the responsibility chart, row by row. Anything without one owner gets resolved in the meeting or moved to an open-items list with a named person and a review date.

Use a simple scoring habit as an author heuristic, not as research: give each row one point if it has a single owner, one if it has a named approver, and one if the handover evidence is described. Rows scoring two or less are the ones to discuss first. This is a way of directing attention, nothing more.

Close the meeting by confirming three things in writing: the current revision of the scope, the interface dates or triggers, and who receives the handover documents. Send the note to both vendors the same day.

If a security vendor is one of your parties, the published service list is a reasonable starting point for the conversation about which tasks they will own. Eyonic Security Systems' listed services include site survey, camera placement, NVR setup and remote viewing, which gives you concrete headings to ask about rather than a vague request for "the security side."

Keep the record alive after the project closes

The scope document has a second life after handover. It becomes the reference for service requests, warranty questions and any later change. A vendor who was not the owner of a row should not be asked to service it, and the chart tells you who to call.

File the final revision with the handover documents and note the date the project closed. Add a review date, for example twelve months later, to check that the named roles still exist and that contact routes are current. This is a suggested interval, not a standard.

When a change is proposed later, reopen the chart rather than starting a new document. Add a row, assign one owner, and issue a new revision. That keeps the history in one place and makes it obvious when a task has quietly changed hands.

Finally, brief the people who actually use the entrance. They do not need the chart, but they should know who to tell when something seems wrong, and that reporting it is expected rather than an imposition. A short written note at the entrance, or a line in the staff briefing, closes the loop between the document and daily use.
