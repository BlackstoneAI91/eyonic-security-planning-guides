Make an Authorised CCTV Training Checklist Without Reusing Real Footage

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical guide for Sarawak premises managers who need to train staff on a new CCTV system using staged examples, clear authorisation and a short sign-off record, without copying real recordings.

Start with the training purpose, not the footage

Before anyone opens a training folder, write one sentence describing what staff must be able to do after the session. A fictional example: counter staff at a two-storey office in Kuching should be able to recognise when a camera view is being shown, know who to tell if a view looks wrong, and know which colleague approves a request to review a recording. That sentence keeps the checklist honest, because it names tasks rather than equipment.

A second sentence should state what the training is not. It is not a review of real incidents, not a copy of the live recording archive, and not a substitute for the installer's own handover briefing. Premises managers who write this boundary early avoid the most common drift, which is a session that quietly turns into a walkthrough of actual events.

Eyonic Security Systems plans, supplies and installs monitoring systems with camera placement, recording and remote viewing for homes, offices, shops and industrial sites, so a training checklist should sit alongside the handover rather than replace it. Keep the purpose statement to two or three lines and date it. If the purpose changes later, the checklist version changes with it.

Name the roles before naming the tasks

A checklist without named roles becomes a list nobody owns. Create four columns: task, who performs it, who confirms it, and the date. Use job titles rather than personal names so the record survives staff changes.

A workable set of roles for an ordinary premises project might be: the premises manager who approves the training scope, a supervisor who runs the session, a staff representative who attends, and the provider contact who confirms what was handed over. These are examples, not a required structure. The point is that every row has a person or title attached.

Keep the authorisation line separate from the attendance line. Attendance says who was in the room. Authorisation says who agreed that this training content, with these staged examples, was acceptable to use. Mixing the two makes later review difficult, because a full attendance sheet can look like approval when it is not.

If the provider is involved, ask in writing which parts of the handover they will explain and which parts remain the premises team's responsibility. Record the answer as a row, not as a conversation you remember later.

Build the checklist around generic staff tasks

The checklist should describe what staff do, in plain language, without describing how any system is configured. Useful rows for an ordinary project include:

Recognise the difference between a live view and a recorded view, and know which one is being shown.

Know the approved route for reporting a view that appears unclear or out of position.

Know who to contact before any request to review a recording is passed on.

Know where the written handover notes are kept and who updates them.

Know which colleague answers questions from visitors about cameras in public areas.

Each row should be something a person can demonstrate or describe in a short conversation. Avoid rows that require operating menus, changing settings or handling storage. Those belong to the provider's handover and to qualified staff, not to a general staff checklist.

A useful test: if a new employee could not complete the row after ten minutes of explanation, the row is too technical for this checklist. Move it to a separate technical handover list and note that it exists.

Use staged examples instead of real recordings

The instruction not to reuse real footage is easier to follow when the checklist says what to use instead. Staged examples can be simple: a printed diagram of a room with a marked camera position, a short written scenario, or a role-play in which one person asks to see a recording and another explains the approval route.

Write a short note for each staged example covering three things: what it illustrates, who prepared it, and the date it was approved for training use. That note is the authorisation record. It does not need to be elaborate, but it should exist before the session, not after.

Where a provider has supplied sample material, confirm in writing that it may be used for staff training and whether it can be retained afterwards. Do not assume that material shown during a handover is automatically reusable in later sessions.

Keep staged examples in a folder that is clearly labelled as training material, separate from any operational records. A clear label reduces the chance that a training file is mistaken for an incident file during a later review.

Ask the provider the right handover questions

A short written question list, sent before the handover, keeps the training checklist grounded in what was actually agreed. Useful questions include: which parts of the system will be explained to staff, which parts are for trained operators only, what written notes will be left behind, and who staff should contact with a question after the visit.

For a project that includes CCTV installation, it also helps to ask how camera placement decisions will be explained in plain language, so staff understand why a view covers a particular area. This is an explanation question, not a request for a layout drawing. Similarly, if remote viewing is part of the scope, ask who is authorised to use it and how that authorisation is recorded.

Eyonic CCTV's published service scope covers planning, supply and installation with camera placement, recording and remote viewing, which gives a premises manager a natural set of topics to raise at handover. The answers belong in the checklist as confirmed rows, with the date and the role of the person who confirmed them.

Keep a short training sign-off

The sign-off should fit on one page. It needs the date, the checklist version, the roles present, the staged examples used, and a line confirming that no real recordings were used. Add a space for open questions that were not resolved, because an honest record is more useful than a tidy one.

A fictional example of a completed line: session held on a Tuesday morning, attended by a supervisor and three counter staff, using two printed room diagrams and one written scenario, with one open question about who updates the handover notes after a staff change. That open question becomes the first item for the next review.

Set a review date, for example six months later, and treat it as an example interval rather than a rule. At review, check whether the roles still exist, whether the staged examples are still approved, and whether any handover notes have gone missing.

Store the sign-off with the project documentation, not with operational records. The aim is a record that a future premises manager can read in a few minutes and understand what was trained, by whom, and on what authority.

Where this checklist fits in a wider project

This checklist is one document among several. It sits beside the site survey notes, the handover record and the acceptance record, and it should reference them rather than duplicate them. If a project also includes a burglar alarm system or an access control system, keep those training rows in separate sections so a reader can find the relevant part quickly.

That scope is the context for the checklist, not a claim about what any particular session will cover.

A final practical habit: keep the checklist short enough to finish in one sitting. A long document tends to be signed without being read. Five or six sections, each with a named owner and a date, is usually enough for an ordinary premises project, and it leaves room for the open questions that matter most.
