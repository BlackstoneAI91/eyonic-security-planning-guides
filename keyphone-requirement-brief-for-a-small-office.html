Keyphone Requirement Brief for a Small Office: A Template for Comparing PABX Proposals

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical brief template for Sarawak premises managers to list extensions, hunt groups, voicemail needs and door-phone links, so PABX proposals can be compared on the same requirements.

Why a Written Brief Comes Before Any Keyphone Proposal

A keyphone system is easy to describe in general terms and surprisingly hard to compare once three suppliers have each quoted a different mix of handsets, cards and features. The problem is rarely the hardware; it is that each proposal answers a slightly different question. One supplier assumes every staff member needs a voicemail box, another assumes calls should ring a single reception handset, and a third quietly leaves out the door-phone link because nobody mentioned the back entrance.

A short written brief fixes the question before the answers arrive. It does not need to be technical. It needs to state, in plain language, how many people need to make and take calls, which calls should ring where, what should happen when nobody answers, and which doors or gates need to be reachable by phone. Once that is on one page, every proposal can be scored against the same list rather than against the persuasiveness of the sales visit.

This guide is written for premises managers in Sarawak who are planning an ordinary office fit-out or upgrade and who want a fair comparison. It is not a technical specification and it does not replace a site survey. It is the document you bring to that survey so the conversation starts from your requirements rather than from a catalogue.

The One-Page Brief: Fields to Fill In Before You Call Anyone

Treat the brief as a form you complete internally first. The following fields are a workable starting set; adjust the labels to your own office language.

Premises and people: address or building name, number of floors, approximate staff count, and how many people are on the phone for most of the day versus occasionally. Note any staff who work shifts or are often away from a desk.

Extensions: a simple table with columns for location, occupant or role, and whether the extension needs a display handset, a basic handset, or a cordless unit. Leave a column for future extensions so growth is visible.

Hunt groups: list each group by purpose, for example reception, accounts, or a service counter. For each group, state the order in which handsets should ring and how long the group should ring before the call moves on.

Voicemail: state which extensions need a personal mailbox, which need a shared mailbox, and whether messages should be retrievable from outside the office.

Door and gate links: list each door phone or gate intercom point and which handset or group should answer it. Note whether the caller needs to be able to release the door from the handset.

After-hours behaviour: state what a caller should hear outside office hours and whether any extension should still ring.

Keep the brief to one or two pages. A long document invites selective reading; a short one is harder to misplace.

Extensions and Hunt Groups: Describing Call Flow in Plain Words

Most disagreements about a keyphone system quote trace back to a vague sentence such as "reception handles incoming calls". That sentence can mean one handset, a group of three, or a rotating duty phone. Write the flow instead.

A useful pattern is to describe each incoming call path as a short sequence. For example: an outside call arrives, rings the reception group for four rings, then diverts to the accounts group for four rings, then goes to the shared voicemail box. That single sentence tells a supplier how many group members are needed, whether the system must support timed diversion between groups, and whether a shared mailbox is required.

For each hunt group, record the members by extension number or role, the ring order, and the no-answer destination. If a group has a busy period, note it, because a supplier may suggest a different handset arrangement for a counter that receives bursts of calls.

Where a door phone or gate intercom is involved, treat it as another call path. State which handset should ring when a visitor presses the door station, what happens if nobody answers, and whether the call should be able to release the door. This is the point where a keyphone brief naturally overlaps with access control and smart lock door access planning, because the phone is only one part of how a visitor reaches a member of staff. Smart lock door access is described in Eyonic's product notes as being good for controlled convenience when user permissions and backup entry are planned properly, and the same discipline applies to a door-phone link: decide who can release the door and what happens when the phone system is unavailable.

Voicemail, After-Hours Messages and Who Owns the Greeting

Voicemail is the part of a keyphone system that quietly becomes a management problem if nobody owns it. Before you compare proposals, decide three things.

First, which mailboxes are personal and which are shared. A shared mailbox needs a named owner who can change the greeting and clear old messages; otherwise it fills with callers who never received a reply.

Second, what the after-hours greeting should say. Write the exact wording in the brief, including whether callers are asked to leave a message and whether any extension still rings outside office hours. Suppliers cannot guess your preferred wording, and a greeting recorded in a hurry during handover is rarely the one you want.

Third, how messages are retrieved. State whether staff need to check messages from outside the office and whether that should be by phone or another method. This affects the handset type and the configuration work in the quote.

A practical habit is to include the greeting script as an appendix to the brief and to ask each supplier to confirm who records it and who can change it later. That single question often separates a considered proposal from a handset list.

Door-Phone Links and the Wider Security Picture

A keyphone brief rarely stands alone in a small office. The same reception desk that answers calls may also watch a door camera, release a gate, or manage a visitor queue. If those systems are planned separately, the result is a desk with three handsets, two screens and a clipboard.

When you list door-phone links in the brief, note the physical position of each door station and the handset that will answer it. Then note, separately, whether that door is also covered by a camera or controlled by an access control system. You do not need to design the integration yourself; you need to make the overlap visible so the suppliers can quote for it or tell you it is out of scope.

Keep the brief honest about what is already installed. If a door already has a standalone intercom, say so. If a gate is planned but not yet installed, say that too. Proposals built on an incorrect assumption about existing equipment are difficult to compare fairly.

A Simple Scoring Sheet for Comparing PABX Proposals

Once the brief is written, ask each supplier to respond to it in the same order. Then score the responses against a short list. The following is an author's heuristic, not a researched standard; adjust the weights to your own priorities.

Requirement coverage: does the proposal address every extension, hunt group, mailbox and door-phone link in the brief, or does it leave gaps that you have to chase?

Assumptions stated: does the supplier list what they assumed about cabling, power, handset positions and existing equipment? A proposal that states its assumptions is easier to compare than one that hides them.

Handover and training: does the proposal say who will show staff how to use the handsets, retrieve messages and change greetings? A keyphone system that nobody has been shown how to use tends to default to transferring every call to one person.

Change process: does the proposal explain how extensions or greetings are changed after installation, and whether that is something your staff can do or something you call the supplier for?

Growth: does the proposal show how many spare extensions or ports are available, so a small office can add two or three staff without replacing the system?

Score each item simply, for example from zero to three, and write one sentence of justification. The justification matters more than the number, because it forces you to say why one proposal is stronger. Keep the completed sheet with the brief so the decision can be explained later.

Handover Checklist: What to Confirm Before You Sign Off

The brief and the scoring sheet get you to a decision. A short handover checklist gets you to a working system. The following items are examples of what to confirm with the supplier, not a substitute for their own commissioning process.

Confirm that every extension in the brief has been installed and labelled, and that the labels match the brief rather than an internal numbering scheme that only the installer understands.

Test each hunt group by calling from an outside line and observing the ring order and the no-answer destination. Do this with the group members present so they see the behaviour themselves.

Record the after-hours greeting and confirm who can change it. Ask for the change procedure in writing.

Test each door-phone link, including what happens when the answering handset is busy or unattended. Confirm who can release the door and what staff should do if the phone system is unavailable.

Ask for a simple list of what staff can safely adjust themselves and what requires the supplier. Keep that list with the brief.

Finally, note the date of the handover and the names of the people who attended. A brief that ends with a signed handover note is far more useful a year later than a folder of quotations.
