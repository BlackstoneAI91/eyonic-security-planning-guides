CCTV Camera Placement Worksheet for a Corner-Lot Shop

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A room-by-room worksheet for Sarawak premises managers that maps each camera on a corner-lot shop to a named coverage gap and a mounting height, producing a one-page placement plan an installer can price from.

Why a corner lot needs its own placement plan

A corner-lot shop is watched from two street frontages, and that shape changes the placement problem. There is usually a main entrance on one road, a side entrance or roller shutter on the other, and a service or back lane behind. Each of those edges is a separate approach, and each one needs a camera whose job is written down before anyone drills a hole. The worksheet in this guide exists to stop the common drift where cameras are added one at a time and nobody can say what the fourth camera is actually for.

Start by naming the coverage gaps, not the cameras. A gap is a place where something you care about happens and you cannot currently see it: the till, the side shutter, the back door, the parking bay, the walk-in entrance. Write each gap on its own row. Only then decide which camera covers it and at what height. This order matters because it keeps the plan tied to a purpose rather than to a product list.

Set up the worksheet before the site walk

Build the sheet as a table with these columns: row number, location, coverage gap, what the camera must show, mounting height, view direction, lighting at that spot, and notes. Add a final column for the installer's comment. Keep it to one printed page so it travels with you on the walk.

Before you walk the premises, fill in the location and coverage-gap columns from memory. This is deliberately rough. The walk is where you correct it. Take a simple floor sketch of the corner lot and number the gaps on the sketch to match the rows. A corner lot usually produces rows for the main entrance, the side shutter, the back door, the counter or till, the parking or loading spot, and the perimeter edge along each street.

Decide in advance what "must show" means for each row. For a till, it might be the counter surface and the person standing at it. For a side shutter, it might be the full width of the opening and the ground in front of it. Writing this down forces you to be specific, and it gives the installer something to check against when the camera is aimed. A site survey is the point where these rough rows get tested against walls, cabling routes and power, so treat the sheet as the agenda for that survey rather than a substitute for it.

Walk the corner and fill each row

Walk the perimeter first, in the order a person would approach. At each gap, stand where the camera would go and look at what you want covered. Then stand where the activity happens and look back at the mounting point. Both views matter: the first tells you whether the camera can see the gap, the second tells you whether the gap is lit well enough and whether anything blocks the line.

For each row, record a mounting height. As an author heuristic rather than a rule from any standard, most shop placements fall into a few bands: high enough to be out of easy reach, low enough to keep a useful angle on faces and hands at a counter. Write the band you and the installer agree on, and note why. A camera above a till looking steeply down will show the top of a head; a camera at a gentler angle shows more of the counter. Record the trade-off in the notes column so the decision is visible later.

Note the light at each spot. A camera facing a bright street at dusk sees a different scene from one under a shaded walkway. You do not need to solve the lighting on the worksheet; you need to flag it so the installer can advise on the camera and its position. Mark any row where the view is partly blocked by a signboard, awning, pillar or parked vehicle, and note whether that obstruction is permanent or occasional.

Match each gap to a camera and a height

Now convert the rows into a placement plan. For each coverage gap, write one sentence that names the camera's job, the direction it faces, and the height band. If two gaps can be covered by one camera without weakening either view, merge the rows and say so. If one gap needs two cameras because the approach and the doorway are different scenes, split the row. The plan should read as a set of decisions, not a wish list.

Keep a separate short list of places you deliberately chose not to cover, with a one-line reason. This is often the most useful part of the page. It shows a supplier that you have thought about the corner lot as a whole, and it prevents a later argument about why a particular spot was left out. It also keeps the camera count honest, because every added camera has to displace a stated reason rather than simply appear on the quote.

Where the shop also uses a smart lock door access arrangement on a staff or side door, note it on the relevant row so the camera view and the door are planned together. Eyonic's product guidance notes that smart locks work best when the door condition, user roles and backup method are reviewed before installation, and that for business use a standalone smart lock or an access-control panel may be the more appropriate choice. That review belongs beside the camera row for the same door, not in a separate conversation months later.

A worked example for a fictional corner-lot shop

The following is a fictional example, not a real premises. A shop on a corner has a main entrance on the front road, a side shutter on the cross street, a back door to a service lane, a till near the front, and two parking bays at the side. The manager fills the sheet as follows.

Row one: main entrance, gap is the doorway and the first few steps inside, must show people entering and the door frame, height band high on the front wall, facing the door. Row two: side shutter, gap is the full shutter width and the ground in front, must show the shutter edge and anyone standing at it, height band above the shutter line, facing along the cross street. Row three: back door, gap is the door and the short lane approach, must show the door and the path to it, height band above head height on the back wall. Row four: till, gap is the counter surface and the person at it, must show hands and counter, height band giving a moderate downward angle, facing the counter. Row five: side parking, gap is the two bays, must show vehicles and the walk to the side shutter, height band at the corner of the building, facing the bays.

The manager then adds a not-covered list: the roof is excluded because there is no access, and the far side of the cross street is excluded because it is public road. The page is now a set of five decisions and two stated exclusions. That is enough for an installer to price from and enough for the manager to defend later.

Turn the worksheet into a brief for CCTV installation

Before you send the sheet out, do a consistency pass. Check that every camera row has a named gap, a direction and a height band. Check that no two rows claim the same camera without a note explaining the merge. Check that the not-covered list is present. Then add a short paragraph at the top stating the shop type, the number of street frontages, and the outcome you want: a placement plan you can approve and a quotation that follows it.

When you request CCTV installation, attach the sheet and ask the supplier to mark up any row they would change and to say why. A supplier who engages with the rows is giving you a more useful response than one who returns a generic package. Keep the marked-up copy; it becomes the record of what was agreed before work started. This is also the moment to confirm how recording and remote viewing will be handled, since the placement plan determines what those functions can actually show.

Quick self-check before you sign off the plan

Run this short check as an author heuristic, not a certified audit. Score each line yes or no, and treat any no as a prompt to revisit the row rather than a failure.

One: every camera row names a coverage gap in plain words. Two: every row states a view direction and a height band. Three: the till or counter row says what must be visible on the counter. Four: each street frontage of the corner lot has at least one row or a stated reason for exclusion. Five: the not-covered list exists and gives a reason per item. Six: any smart lock or door access on a covered door is noted on the same row. Seven: the sheet fits on one page and has a date and a version note.

If you can answer yes to all seven, the page is ready to send. If not, fix the weak rows first. The goal is a placement plan that a supplier can price from and that you can check against once the cameras are up, so that the corner lot is covered by decisions you made on purpose rather than by whatever happened to be easy to mount.
