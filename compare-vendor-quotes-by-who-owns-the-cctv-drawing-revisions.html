Comparing Vendor Quotes by Who Owns the CCTV Drawing Revisions

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical guide for Sarawak premises managers on comparing CCTV vendor quotes by drawing revision ownership, sign-off roles and survey differences, with a revision column and a short list of differences to request.

Why the drawing revision column belongs in every quote comparison

Two quotes for the same premises can look identical on the summary page and still describe different buildings. One vendor's drawing may show a camera on the north wall; the other may show it on the fence line. If neither drawing carries a revision number, a date and a named owner, the comparison collapses into a conversation about memory.

A revision column fixes that. For each drawing a vendor submits, record four things: the revision label, the date it was issued, the person or role who issued it, and the person who approved it. That last field is the one most quote comparisons leave blank, and it is the one that decides who is accountable when the site survey changes.

This is a documentation exercise, not a technical one. You are not judging lens choice or cable routes; you are establishing which document is current and who signed it. A quote that references a drawing without a revision label is a quote you cannot compare fairly against one that does.

Eyonic Security Systems plans, supplies and installs CCTV security systems with camera placement, recording and remote viewing for homes, offices, shops and industrial sites, so a drawing-led quote is a normal part of that kind of project. The revision discipline below applies to any vendor you shortlist.

Build the revision column before you open the quotes

Set up the comparison sheet before any quote arrives, so every vendor answers the same fields. A simple table works: vendor name, drawing reference, revision label, issue date, issuer, approver, and a free-text note on what changed from the previous revision.

Add two more columns that are easy to forget. The first is "survey basis" — did the drawing come from a site survey visit, a phone description, or a copy of an older plan? The second is "revision owner" — the single named role responsible for issuing the next revision if the layout changes.

A worked example, entirely fictional: Vendor A submits drawing A-01, revision B, dated 3 March, issued by a surveyor, approved by the premises manager. Vendor B submits drawing B-01 with no revision label and no approver. On paper both quote the same camera count. In practice only Vendor A has given you a document you can hold someone to.

Keep the sheet to one page. If a vendor cannot fill a field, write "not stated" rather than leaving it blank — the gaps are the comparison.

Who signs off a revision, and why it should not be the vendor alone

The sign-off owner is the person who accepts that a drawing reflects the premises as it will be used. That should be someone inside your organisation, not the vendor. A vendor can issue a revision; only your side can approve it.

Name the approver by role, not by person, so the arrangement survives staff changes: for example, "premises manager" or "operations lead." Then record what approval actually covers. Does it cover camera placement only, or also the recorder location and the remote viewing arrangement? Write the scope of the approval next to the signature line.

A short approval note is enough. Something like: "Revision B approved for quoting purposes; camera placement accepted; recorder location to be confirmed at next survey." That sentence tells the next vendor exactly what is settled and what is still open.

If two people share approval — say a landlord's representative and a tenant's manager — record both, and note which one prevails if they disagree. Ambiguity here is what turns a small drawing change into a stalled project.

Turn the site survey into a short list of differences

Two site surveys of the same premises rarely produce identical drawings, and that is useful information rather than a problem. The comparison task is to list the differences in plain language and decide which ones matter.

Keep the list short — five to eight items is usually enough for an ordinary premises. Typical entries include: a camera shown at a different height, a recorder placed in a different room, a view that one survey treats as covered and another treats as a blind spot, and a mounting point one survey assumes is available and the other does not.

For each difference, record which revision it appears in and who raised it. Then mark it as "accepted," "to confirm" or "rejected." A difference marked "to confirm" is a question for the next survey, not a reason to discard a quote.

This is where a CCTV site survey earns its place in the comparison. A survey that produces decisions — this view is in scope, that one is not — gives you a drawing you can approve. A survey that produces a wish list gives you a document nobody can sign.

Compare quotes on revision ownership, not just price lines

Once the revision column and the difference list exist, the price lines become easier to read. A quote that includes a stated number of drawing revisions, and names who issues them, is describing a working relationship. A quote that mentions no revisions at all is describing a single snapshot.

Ask each vendor two questions in writing. First: how many drawing revisions are included before work starts, and who issues each one? Second: if the layout changes after approval, who produces the updated drawing and who approves it? Record the answers in the same sheet.

You are not looking for the vendor that offers the most revisions. You are looking for the one whose answer is clear enough that you can plan around it. "Two revisions, issued by the surveyor, approved by the premises manager" is a better answer than "as many as needed," because you can put it in the project file.

Where a quote bundles CCTV installation with other work, keep the drawing ownership question separate for each system so responsibilities do not blur.

A one-page handoff sheet for the approved revision

When you have chosen a vendor, write a single handoff sheet that travels with the project. It should state the approved drawing reference and revision, the date of approval, the approver's role, the revision owner for future changes, and the short list of differences marked "to confirm."

Add a line for what happens if a change is needed after approval: who is told first, who issues the new revision, and where the superseded drawing is filed. Keep superseded revisions rather than discarding them — the history is what lets a new manager understand why a camera sits where it does.

This sheet is also the natural place to note the systems the drawing covers. If the project later extends to NVR setup, remote viewing or a smart lock door access decision, each addition should get its own revision entry rather than being pencilled onto the approved drawing.

A handoff sheet of this kind takes perhaps twenty minutes to write and saves far more than that in clarification later. Treat the timings here as an author's suggestion, not a measured benchmark.

A short checklist to score each quote before shortlisting

Use this checklist as a scoring aid, not a formal standard. Give each vendor one point per line answered clearly, and treat the total as a conversation starter rather than a verdict.

One: does the quote reference a drawing with a revision label and date? Two: is the issuer named? Three: is an approver on your side named by role? Four: is the survey basis stated? Five: is the revision owner for future changes identified? Six: does the quote say how many revisions are included before work starts? Seven: is there a stated process for changes after approval? Eight: are superseded revisions retained and referenced?

A vendor scoring six or more has given you a document you can manage. A vendor scoring three or fewer has given you a price and left the drawing ownership open — ask the missing questions before you compare costs.

Keep the completed checklist with the quotes. When the project is reviewed later, the checklist explains why a particular vendor was shortlisted, which is often more useful than the price alone.
