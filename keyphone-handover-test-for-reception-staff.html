Keyphone Handover Test for Reception Staff: A Short Script to Run Before the Installer Leaves

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical test script for Sarawak premises managers and reception staff to verify transfer, hold, voicemail and after-hours routing on a new keyphone system before the installer packs up.

Why the handover test belongs to reception, not the installer

The installer knows how the keyphone system is meant to behave because they configured it. Reception staff know how the desk actually behaves on a Monday morning. The gap between those two views is where most handover problems hide, and it closes fastest when the people who answer calls run the test themselves while the installer is still on site.

This guide is a short script, not a technical manual. It assumes a small office or shop counter in Sarawak where one or two people handle incoming calls, take messages, and route callers to colleagues. The aim is to confirm four everyday actions work from the reception handset: transferring a call, putting a caller on hold, reaching voicemail, and sending calls somewhere sensible after hours. Everything else can wait.

A useful frame is to treat the handover as an acceptance moment rather than a demonstration. The installer shows; reception does. If a step fails, it is written down and fixed before the van leaves, not logged for a return visit next week. Keep the script to about thirty minutes and run it from the actual reception position, using the actual handset, not a spare phone on the installer's bench.

Set up the test before anyone dials

Before the first test call, agree on a few practical things so the results mean something.

First, decide who plays the caller. A second staff member using a mobile phone is ideal, because it tests the real path an outside caller takes. Note the mobile number on the worksheet so the same number is used for every step.

Second, decide who plays the internal extension. Pick one colleague's desk that reception calls often. That extension becomes the transfer target for the whole test.

Third, write down the expected result for each step before you start. For example: a call to reception should ring the reception handset; a transfer should connect the caller to the colleague without the caller having to redial; a held caller should hear something rather than silence. Writing expectations first stops the test drifting into a general demonstration.

Fourth, ask the installer to stay silent during the test unless something needs explaining. The point is to see whether reception can complete each action from memory or from a short note, not whether the installer can.

Finally, keep a simple worksheet with four columns: step, expected result, what actually happened, and pass or fail. This is an author heuristic, not a formal standard, but it keeps the conversation concrete.

Test one: transfer and hold from the reception handset

Transfer and hold are the two actions reception will use most, so test them first while attention is fresh.

For the transfer test, the mobile caller dials the main number. Reception answers, then transfers the call to the agreed colleague extension. The pass condition is that the caller reaches the colleague and the colleague can hear them clearly. Repeat it twice: once with the colleague ready to answer, and once with the colleague deliberately not answering, to see what happens to the caller in that case. That second attempt is often the one that reveals a gap.

For the hold test, the mobile caller dials again. Reception answers and places the caller on hold. The pass condition is that the caller hears whatever the system is configured to play, and that reception can retrieve the call. Ask the caller afterwards what they heard, because the person on hold is the only one who knows.

Record both results on the worksheet. If a transfer drops the call or a held caller hears nothing, that is a fail to be fixed on site, not a note for later. These are the two functions that shape how a caller experiences the office, and they are worth getting right at handover.

Test two: voicemail and the message that actually arrives

Voicemail is easy to configure and easy to leave untested, because testing it means deliberately not answering.

Run this step with the mobile caller dialling reception and reception letting it ring through to voicemail. The pass conditions are: the caller reaches a greeting, the greeting is understandable, the caller can leave a message, and reception can retrieve that message from the handset.

Retrieval is the part that matters most. A greeting that plays but a mailbox nobody can open is worse than no voicemail at all, because callers believe a message has been left. Have reception retrieve the test message in front of the installer and confirm the message is audible and attributed to the right mailbox.

If the office has more than one mailbox, test the one reception actually uses, not a general mailbox. Note on the worksheet which mailbox was tested and who is responsible for checking it. That single line prevents the common situation where two people each assume the other listens to the messages.

Test three: after-hours routing

After-hours routing is the step most often skipped, because it requires pretending the office is closed.

Ask the installer to place the system temporarily into its after-hours mode, or to simulate it in whatever way the configuration allows, and then have the mobile caller dial the main number. The pass condition depends on what the office has decided should happen: the call may ring a nominated mobile, play a closed message, or go to a mailbox. Whatever the choice, the test confirms it happens.

Two details are worth capturing. First, what the caller hears or reaches, described in the caller's own words. Second, how the office returns the system to normal hours, and who is allowed to do it. Write both on the worksheet and keep it with the handset instructions.

If the office has never agreed what after-hours calls should do, this is the moment to decide rather than a reason to skip the test. A short written answer, agreed by the person who manages the premises, is enough.

A short worksheet reception can sign

The worksheet does not need to be elaborate. A single page with the following rows is enough, and it gives the installer a clear list to close out before leaving.

Row one: outside call reaches reception. Row two: transfer to the agreed extension connects. Row three: transfer to an unanswered extension behaves as agreed. Row four: hold plays audio and the call can be retrieved. Row five: voicemail greeting plays and the message can be retrieved. Row six: after-hours routing behaves as agreed. Row seven: reception can return the system to normal hours.

For each row, record pass or fail, the name of the person who tested it, and the date. Where a row fails, write one sentence describing what happened, because that sentence is what the installer needs to fix it.

At the end, both the reception lead and the installer sign. The signature is not a legal document; it is a simple record that the four everyday actions were seen working by the people who will use them. Keep a copy at reception and a copy with the premises file.

If the office also runs a queue management system at the counter, note on the same page how a caller is handed between the phone and the counter, so the two do not compete for the same staff member's attention.

Where the keyphone fits alongside the rest of the premises

A keyphone system is one part of how a premises handles people arriving and calling, and the handover test is easier to plan when it is seen that way.

If the reception desk is also the place where visitors are signed in or access cards are issued, the keyphone handover can be scheduled alongside those checks so staff are not pulled away twice.

For premises that use smart lock door access, the review question is whether a standalone lock or an access-control panel suits the door, and that decision is best made before installation rather than after. The keyphone handover does not depend on it, but both belong to the same practical question: can the people at the front desk operate what has been installed without calling someone for help?

Keep the handover script short, run it from the real desk, and write down what happened. That is the whole method, and it is usually enough to catch the small configuration gaps that would otherwise surface on the first busy morning.
