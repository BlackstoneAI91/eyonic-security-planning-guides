CCTV Site Survey Questions for a Shophouse With Shared Walls

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical question list for Sarawak premises managers surveying a shophouse with shared walls, covering cable routes, neighbour consent points, recorder placement and what to confirm before quoting.

Why a shared-wall shophouse needs its own survey questions

A shophouse with shared walls is a row building before it is a security project. The wall you want to mount a camera on may be a party wall. The ceiling void above your unit may continue into the next lot. The back lane may be the only place a cable can run, and it may also be the place your neighbour parks a lorry. A generic survey that counts cameras and picks heights will miss all of this.

The purpose of the question list below is to turn the survey into a set of decisions rather than a wish list. You want the surveyor to leave with a marked floor plan, a list of routes that are confirmed, a list of routes that need someone else's agreement, and a clear answer on where the recorder can sit without crossing another tenant's space. A site survey is a normal part of scoping this kind of work, and it is the right moment to raise boundary questions rather than after the first drill hole.

Bring the questions in writing. A written list keeps the conversation on constraints, and it gives you a record of what was agreed when the quote arrives. Treat the timings suggested here as examples of how a survey might be run, not as fixed rules.

Questions about the boundary and the party wall

Start with the wall itself, because everything else follows from it. Ask which walls are shared and which are yours alone, and ask the surveyor to mark the boundary on the plan. Then work through these:

Which cameras are proposed on a shared wall, and at what height? A camera at first-floor level may be above a neighbour's roofline or looking into a window; a camera at ground level may sit where a neighbour's signage or awning is fixed.

Does any proposed view look into a neighbouring unit, a back lane used by other tenants, or a shared staircase? If so, can the camera be angled or fitted with a narrower view to keep the coverage on your own premises?

Are there existing fixtures on the shared wall, such as meter boxes, pipes, air-conditioning brackets or signage, that a new bracket would obstruct or share?

Who owns the wall for the purpose of fixing things to it? This is a question for your landlord or management corporation, not something the installer can decide. The surveyor can tell you what fixing is proposed; the permission is yours to obtain.

If a camera must be removed later, what will be left on the shared wall? Ask about bracket holes, sealant and cable entry points so the exit condition is known before work starts.

Write the answers next to the camera positions on the plan. A camera that cannot be agreed on the shared wall may need to move to your own frontage, and that changes the coverage plan.

Questions about cable routes through shared voids

Cable is where shared walls cause the most quiet trouble. A route that looks short on the plan may pass through a ceiling void that serves two units, or drop down a riser that other tenants also use. Ask the surveyor to trace each route out loud and mark it:

Where does each cable leave the camera, and where does it enter the building? Ask for the entry point to be shown on the plan, not just described.

Does any route cross a shared ceiling void, a common riser, a back lane or a corridor used by other tenants? If yes, who needs to agree to it, and is there an alternative route entirely within your unit?

Can the route avoid drilling through the party wall? Surface trunking within your own unit is often less contentious than a concealed run through a shared structure, even if it looks less tidy.

What happens at a fire-rated wall or a structural beam? The surveyor should flag it; the decision on how to proceed belongs with the building's requirements, not with the installer's convenience.

Is there a route that keeps cabling away from areas where stock, shelving or counters will later be moved? A route fixed to a wall that gets rearranged every year becomes a maintenance problem.

Ask for the total cable length per camera and where joints, if any, would sit. Joints in a shared void are hard to inspect and harder to explain to a neighbour.

If a route needs another tenant's agreement, note it as an open item on the survey record. Do not let it be absorbed into a quote as though it were settled.

Questions about where the recorder can sit

The recorder is the part of the system that decides whether footage still exists when someone asks for it, so its location matters as much as the cameras. In a shophouse the obvious cupboard may be in a shared area, which creates a problem: the recorder should not sit in a space another tenant controls or can switch off.

Ask where the recorder is proposed, and whether that position is inside your own demised area. Ask whether the power point for it is on your meter. Ask who can physically reach it, because a recorder in an open back corridor is reachable by anyone who uses that corridor.

Then ask about heat and dust. A back storeroom with no ventilation is a poor home for a recorder that runs continuously. Ask whether the proposed position has airflow and whether it is away from a loading area where dust settles.

Ask how the recorder connects to the network, and whether that connection stays within your unit. If remote viewing is planned, the recorder needs a network path, and that path should not depend on a neighbour's router or a shared line you do not control. Ask the surveyor to explain, in plain terms, what the recorder needs and who provides it.

Finally, ask for the recorder position and its power source to be marked on the same plan as the cameras. One plan, one set of decisions.

Questions about coverage, lighting and the working day

Once the boundary and routes are settled, move to what the cameras will actually see. A shophouse frontage often faces a five-foot way, a back lane or a side street, and each has different light at different times.

Ask which camera covers the entrance and whether it sees faces at the door or only the top of heads. Ask which camera covers the till or counter, and whether the view is blocked by a display or a queue barrier at busy times.

Ask about the back door and the loading point. These are the positions most often added late, and they are the ones that need a route decided early.

Ask how the view changes between day and night, and whether the proposed camera handles the light at that position. A camera pointed at a bright street lamp or a reflective sign at night is a common disappointment; the survey is the time to notice it.

Ask what happens when the shutters are down. A camera inside a closed shop sees nothing useful; a camera outside needs to be placed where it is not blocked by the shutter box.

Ask the surveyor to name the gap each camera closes, not just the wall it goes on. If two cameras cover the same doorway and nothing covers the back lane, the plan has a gap with a camera count that looks complete.

A one-page survey record you can sign

The survey is only useful if it produces something you can act on. Before the surveyor leaves, ask for a single page containing:

A simple floor plan with the boundary marked, each proposed camera position numbered, and each cable route drawn.

A table with one row per camera: position, what it is meant to see, mounting height, and whether the wall is shared or yours.

A recorder position, its power source, and its network path.

An open-items list: neighbour consent, landlord consent, fire-rated penetrations, and any route that is not yet confirmed.

A short note on what is excluded, such as making good after drilling or moving existing fixtures.

Sign and date it, and ask the surveyor to do the same. This page becomes the basis of the quote and the reference point if a route changes later. It also gives you a fair way to compare two quotes: the one that matches the agreed plan is easier to judge than the one that is cheapest on paper.

If the project goes ahead, the same plan carries into the handover, so the person who signs off the finished work is checking against the survey rather than against memory.
