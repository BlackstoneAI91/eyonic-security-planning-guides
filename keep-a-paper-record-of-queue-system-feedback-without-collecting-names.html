Keeping a Paper Record of Queue System Feedback Without Collecting Names

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical guide for Sarawak premises managers on running a short anonymous feedback route for a queue management system, summarising common navigation issues, and keeping the paper trail clean without gathering personal details.

Start with a purpose statement before you print a single form

Before anyone designs a feedback slip, write one sentence describing what the record is for. A workable example: the record exists so that the premises manager and the service desk can see which parts of the visitor journey cause hesitation, and can decide what to change next. That sentence belongs at the top of the folder, not buried in a file.

Next, decide what the record will never contain. For an anonymous route, that means no visitor names, no telephone numbers, no identity card details, no ticket numbers that can be traced back to one person, and no staff names attached to a complaint. If a visitor writes a name on the slip anyway, agree in advance who removes it before the slips are counted, and note that step in the folder so the routine survives a change of staff.

Keep the form short. Three or four tick boxes plus one open line is usually enough for a first version. Examples of neutral prompts: whether the visitor found the counter they needed, whether the display or announcement was easy to follow, and whether anything made the visit harder. Avoid asking visitors to rate staff performance, because that invites identifying detail.

Finally, name the owner. One role, not one person's memory, should hold the folder, the blank slips and the summary sheet. This is an administrative routine, separate from any security procurement decision, and it should stay that way.

Design the slip so it cannot quietly become a visitor register

A feedback slip drifts into a register when it collects more than it needs. Use a fixed layout: a date box for the day only, a location box naming the counter or hall, tick boxes for the questions, and a single open line. Leave out time stamps precise enough to single out one visitor in a quiet period, and leave out any field that asks who served them.

Provide a sealed box or a plain envelope for completed slips, placed away from the counter where staff can see who is writing. A visible collection point is fine; a watched one is not. Print a short line on the slip itself explaining that no name is needed and that slips are counted in batches, so visitors understand the route.

Decide the counting rhythm in advance. A weekly count of a small batch is easier to keep honest than a daily count that gets skipped. Record only totals per question and a short paraphrase of open comments, grouped by theme rather than quoted at length. If a comment mentions a person, rewrite it as a process observation or leave it out.

Store the folder where the premises manager can reach it, and set a review date on the cover. When the review date arrives, the owner reads the summary, not the raw slips, unless a specific issue needs a closer look.

Where the queue management system fits in the feedback loop

A queue management system shapes how visitors find the right counter, wait and get called forward, so feedback about navigation is genuinely useful to the people who plan the service area. Keep the feedback route focused on what a visitor experiences: whether the category names made sense, whether the counter was easy to locate, whether the calling announcement was clear from where they stood.

This is a documentation exercise, not a device exercise. Nothing in the paper route requires anyone to change settings, add hardware or monitor visitors. If the summary repeatedly points at the same navigation problem, the premises manager can raise it as a question with the appointed provider and record the answer in the same folder.

A one-page summary sheet that stays anonymous

The summary sheet is where most anonymous routes fail, because it is tempting to add detail that re-identifies people. Build the sheet with fixed columns and no free-text identity fields.

Suggested columns, as an author heuristic rather than a standard: week ending, location, number of slips counted, count who could not find the right counter, count who found the display or announcement unclear, count who reported another difficulty, and a theme column limited to short phrases such as signage, waiting area or counter numbering. Add a final column for the action agreed and the role responsible.

Keep the theme list closed. If a new theme appears, add it deliberately at the next review rather than inventing a phrase on the spot. This keeps month-to-month comparison honest and stops the sheet turning into a diary.

Write a short note at the foot of the sheet stating what it excludes: no names, no contact details, no individual case histories. Anyone picking up the folder later should be able to see the boundary without asking. When the sheet is full, file it behind the purpose statement and start a new one, so the folder reads as a sequence rather than a pile.

Agree the retention question with the right owner

How long to keep paper slips is a question for the person who owns the premises records, not a decision to improvise at the counter. Put the question in writing early: who approves the retention period, where the folder lives, and what happens to slips at the end of the period.

A practical approach is to keep the summary sheets longer than the raw slips, because the summary carries the useful pattern and the slips carry the most detail. Set a review date on the folder cover and note the disposal decision in the same place. This guide does not give a retention period, because the right answer depends on the organisation's own records policy and any advice its owner takes.

If a visitor or a staff member asks what happens to the slips, the answer should be a short, written sentence that matches the folder. Keep that sentence with the purpose statement so the two never drift apart.

Where the premises also runs other security-related records, such as a CCTV handover pack, keep the queue feedback folder clearly labelled as a service-area record. Mixing the two makes both harder to review and invites questions the folder cannot answer.

Hand the routine over so it survives staff changes

A paper routine is only as good as its handover. Write a single handover page covering four things: where the blank slips are kept, who counts them and when, where the summary sheet lives, and who reviews it. Add the name of the role, not the person, so the page stays accurate after a transfer.

Include a short note on what to do when something unusual happens: a slip with a name on it, a comment about a specific incident, or a batch that was never counted. For each, write the neutral next step, such as passing the item to the premises manager for a decision, rather than a rule that assumes a conclusion.

When a new staff member takes over the counter, walk them through the folder once and let them count one batch alongside the outgoing owner. That single session does more than a written procedure alone, and it gives the new owner a chance to ask about the theme list.

Finally, review the whole routine at the same time each year. Ask whether the questions still match what visitors struggle with, whether the summary columns are still readable, and whether the purpose statement still describes what the folder is for. If the answers have changed, rewrite the page rather than adding a second one.

Keep the record honest about what it is not

An anonymous paper route is a modest tool, and the folder should say so. It does not measure satisfaction across the whole premises, it does not represent visitors who never pick up a slip, and it does not replace a formal complaint channel. Writing those limits on the inside cover protects the summary from being over-read later.

It also keeps the record separate from procurement. If the premises is comparing security quotations, the queue feedback folder is not evidence about a provider's performance, and it should not be filed as if it were. Keep the two conversations apart, and let each record answer only its own question.

For teams planning a wider premises project, the Eyonic CCTV homepage is a reasonable starting point to see how the provider describes its scope before drafting questions. Route any scope questions through the provider's published contact channel rather than through the feedback folder.

A short annual note in the folder, recording what changed because of the feedback and what stayed the same, closes the loop without collecting a single name.
