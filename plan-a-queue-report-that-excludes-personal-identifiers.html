Plan a queue report that excludes personal identifiers

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical guide for Sarawak premises managers on designing a daily queue report that records counts, categories and timings without collecting personal identifiers, with a worksheet for fields, retention questions and handover.

Start with the decision the report must support

A queue report is easy to over-build. Before choosing any field, write one sentence describing the decision the report should help you make. Examples of ordinary decisions include: how many visitors arrived each day, which service category was busiest, and whether staffing at a counter matched demand. If a field does not change one of those decisions, leave it out. This single test removes most personal data before it is ever collected.

A useful exercise is to draw three columns on paper: question, field, owner. For the question "how busy was Tuesday?", the field is a daily count and the owner is the reception supervisor. For "which category needs more staff?", the field is a category tally. Notice that neither question needs a name, a phone number or an identity document reference. Those belong to the service transaction, not to the queue report.

Keep the report separate from any visitor log that a site may keep for other purposes. Mixing the two is how identifiers end up in a summary that only needed numbers. Write the separation into the report header so a future reader knows what the document is and is not.

Choose non-identifying fields and a fictional daily layout

A workable daily layout usually needs only a date, a location or counter label, a category tally, a first-seen and last-seen time, and a short note column for operational remarks such as a counter closing early. Category labels should describe the service, not the person: for example "general enquiry", "document collection" or "appointment". Avoid free-text fields that invite staff to type names or descriptions of individuals.

Here is a fictional example for one day at a service centre. Date: 14 March. Counter A: general enquiry 22, document collection 9. Counter B: appointment 14. First visitor 08:55, last visitor 16:40. Note: Counter B closed 12:00 to 13:00. Nothing in that block identifies anyone, yet it answers the three questions from the previous section.

If your team wants richer detail, add a banded wait time (for example under five minutes, five to fifteen, over fifteen) rather than a per-visitor timestamp. Bands are easier to aggregate and harder to misuse. Treat any suggested field list as a starting draft for your own review, not a standard.

Write the retention question before the first report

Retention is the part teams most often skip. Before the first daily report is saved, write down three things: how long the report is kept, who is allowed to read it, and who decides when it is deleted. These are questions for the proper owner in your organisation, not answers this guide can supply. If your premises has a data protection lead, a compliance officer or a legal adviser, route the question there and record the answer next to the report template.

A simple worksheet helps. Column one: report type. Column two: suggested review period, clearly labelled as an example only. Column three: approving role. Column four: deletion owner. Column five: date the answer was confirmed. A fictional entry might read: daily queue summary, review after one quarter (example), approved by operations manager, deleted by reception supervisor, confirmed on a stated date.

Keep the retention note with the template rather than in someone's inbox. When staff change, the note travels with the document. This is ordinary documentation practice, not legal advice, and it should be confirmed by whoever owns the question in your organisation.

Separate the queue report from the security brief

Premises managers often plan queue reporting alongside other building projects. Keep the documents distinct. A queue report describes service demand; a security brief describes monitoring, access and communications. If your site is also reviewing CCTV security systems, the queue report should not become a place to record footage references or descriptions of individuals seen on camera.

That work is a separate scope from queue reporting, and the two should have separate owners and separate documents. Where a site survey touches a reception area, note the survey date and the responsible contact in the project file, not in the daily queue summary.

A short cross-reference line is enough: "Queue reporting owner: reception supervisor. Security project owner: facilities lead." That line prevents a future reader from assuming one document covers both.

Agree who fills, checks and hands over the report

A report without named roles drifts. Assign three roles in writing: the person who completes the daily tally, the person who checks it at the end of the week, and the person who receives it at handover. Use role titles rather than personal names in the template so the document survives staff changes.

A fictional handover note might read: completed by front desk, checked weekly by operations assistant, reviewed monthly by the premises manager. Add a line for what to do when the counter is unstaffed, so the report records a closure rather than a gap that looks like missing data.

When a queue management system is in use, the report should still describe what was counted and by whom, even if the counts are drawn from a summary view. Do not assume the system produces the fields you need; confirm with the provider which non-identifying summaries are available and record that answer. If the site is planning a queue management system as part of a wider reception upgrade, keep the reporting requirement in the project brief so it is discussed alongside other scopes rather than added later.

Review the report quarterly with a short checklist

Set a review date and use a short checklist. Suggested items, offered as an author heuristic rather than a standard: does every field still map to a decision; has any free-text field crept in; is the retention note still current; are the role owners still correct; and has anyone asked for a field that would identify a person. Score each item yes or no and note the date.

A fictional review might find that a remarks column had started collecting descriptions of visitors. The fix is to replace it with a fixed list of operational codes, such as counter closed, system slow or staff shortage. That keeps the note useful without turning it into a record about people.

Finally, keep the report template and the review notes in one folder with a clear owner. If your organisation is also planning broader security work, such as access control or a burglar alarm system, keep those project documents in their own folder. The queue report stays small, numeric and easy to hand over, which is exactly what makes it durable.
