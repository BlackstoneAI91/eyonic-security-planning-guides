CCTV Camera Placement Worksheet for a Shop Entrance and Till

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A one-page worksheet that maps each camera to a named view at a shop entrance and till, records mounting height and lens choice, and turns the result into a fixed list an installer can quote from.

Why a written placement list beats a walk-around guess

A shop entrance and a till are two of the busiest, most changeable spots on any premises. The entrance carries every person who comes in, the till carries the money and the staff who handle it, and both are usually lit differently at noon and at closing time. When a supplier quotes from a quick walk-around, the price is built on assumptions about how many cameras, where they go, and how the cable reaches them. Those assumptions surface later as variations.

A placement worksheet fixes the assumptions before the quote. Each row names one camera, the view it must deliver, the mounting height, and the lens or field of view. The installer then prices a defined list rather than an imagined one. This is the same logic behind a structured CCTV site survey: decisions are recorded on paper while the manager and the installer are standing in the same room, not reconstructed from memory afterwards.

The worksheet itself is yours to fill in; it is a planning aid, not a substitute for the installer's professional judgement on site.

The worksheet: one row per camera, one named view per row

Copy this structure onto a single sheet. Add rows as needed, but keep it to one page so it can be signed and attached to the quotation.

Column 1, Camera ID: a short label such as EN-01 or TL-01. Use the same label on the floor sketch so the two documents match.

Column 2, Named view: describe the picture the camera must produce, not the hardware. Examples: "face of every person entering through the main door", "till drawer and the hands of the cashier", "stockroom door and the corridor in front of it". A view that cannot be described in one sentence is usually two cameras pretending to be one.

Column 3, Mounting height: record the height in metres and who proposed it. Height affects both what the lens sees and how easy the camera is to reach for cleaning or adjustment.

Column 4, Lens or field of view: note whether the view needs a wide sweep or a narrow, tighter frame. A wide lens at the entrance captures faces and bodies; a tighter frame at the till captures the drawer and the counter surface.

Column 5, Light and backlight notes: record whether the camera faces a bright doorway, a window, or a dark corner. This is the single most common reason a well-placed camera still produces a poor image.

Column 6, Cable route: a one-line note on how the cable reaches the recorder, and whether the route crosses a ceiling void, a wall, or another tenant's space.

Column 7, Owner and status: who confirmed the row, and whether it is proposed, agreed, or installed.

Filling in the entrance rows

The entrance is where the worksheet earns its keep, because it is the view most often described vaguely. "Cover the door" is not a view. Work through these prompts for each entrance camera.

Does the camera see the face of a person entering, or only the top of a head? A camera mounted high and pointed down captures heads and shoulders; a camera mounted lower and angled across the doorway captures faces. Decide which the shop actually needs and write it down.

Does the view include the door frame and the threshold? A frame that shows the door opening tells you when someone entered; a frame that shows only the interior tells you someone is inside but not when they arrived.

Is there a second exit or a side door? If so, it needs its own row. A camera at the main entrance does not cover a rear door, and assuming it does is how gaps appear.

What happens at closing time? Note whether the entrance view still works when the shutters are down and the interior lights are off. If the answer is uncertain, mark the row for a site check rather than guessing.

For a shop with a corner or angled frontage, the entrance rows multiply quickly. A separate worksheet for a corner-lot shop handles that geometry in more detail; this page stays focused on a straightforward entrance and till.

Filling in the till rows

The till is a different kind of subject. The entrance view is about people arriving; the till view is about a small, fixed area where hands, a drawer, and a counter surface matter more than faces at a distance.

Start with the drawer. Does the camera see the drawer when it is open, or only the closed counter? Write the answer as a view: "open till drawer and the counter surface in front of the cashier".

Then consider the cashier's position. A camera directly above the till looks down on heads and hands; a camera offset to one side sees the drawer and the customer's side of the counter. Note which side the camera will sit on and why.

Record the counter layout. If a display stand, a card terminal, or a queue barrier sits between the camera and the drawer, the view is partly blocked. Note the obstruction and whether it can be moved.

Note the lighting above the till. A bright downlight directly over the counter can wash out the drawer; a shadowed corner can hide it. Record what is there now, not what you hope to add later.

Finally, decide whether the till needs one camera or two. A single wide view of the counter and a tighter view of the drawer are different jobs, and the worksheet should show them as separate rows if both are needed.

Adding the stockroom door and the recorder location

Most shop placement plans stop at the entrance and the till and forget the door between the shop floor and the back room. That door is where stock, cash floats, and staff bags move, and it deserves its own row with its own named view: "stockroom door and the two metres of corridor in front of it".

Record whether the camera sees the door from the shop side or the stockroom side, and note who has keys to that door. If the door also carries a smart lock or a card reader, note it on the same row so the installer can see the two systems share a location. Smart lock door access works best when the door condition, user roles and a backup entry method are reviewed before installation, and the same review applies to a door that carries both a lock and a camera.

Then add a row for the recorder. Where will it sit, and is that place locked, ventilated, and reachable for playback? NVR setup decisions about recording schedules and user accounts depend on the recorder being somewhere sensible, and a recorder tucked into an unlocked cupboard undermines the rest of the plan. Note the proposed location and who will hold the admin account.

Turning the worksheet into a quote and a handover

Once the rows are filled, the sheet becomes the basis for the quotation conversation. Ask the installer to price each row by camera ID, so a change to one view is a visible line item rather than a lump-sum surprise. Ask which rows need a site check before they can be confirmed, and mark those clearly.

Use the same sheet at handover. When the work is done, walk the rows and confirm each named view is actually delivered on the monitor. A row that says "face of every person entering" is either true or it is not, and the manager can check it in a minute. Record the result beside each row and sign the sheet.

If remote viewing is part of the plan, add a final note on who will hold remote access and how it is withdrawn when a staff member leaves. Remote viewing is convenient, but it widens the circle of people who can see the till, so the access list belongs on the same page as the placement list.
