Smart Lock Battery Life Planning Worksheet for a Rental Unit

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical worksheet for Sarawak premises managers to estimate smart lock battery life from usage patterns, plan replacement schedules, and keep backup entry working in a rental unit.

Why a Rental Unit Needs Its Own Battery Plan

A rental unit is not a home you occupy. The people who open the door change, the number of openings per day changes with each tenancy, and the person who notices a low-battery warning may be a tenant who has never read the manual. That is why a battery plan written for an owner-occupied house rarely transfers cleanly to a rented unit.

The worksheet below treats battery life as an estimate, not a promise. Manufacturer figures are usually quoted for a nominal number of cycles under lab conditions. Your unit will differ because of how often the door is used, how the lock communicates, and how the door itself behaves. The goal is not a precise number of months. The goal is a defensible replacement interval, a named person responsible for it, and a backup entry method that does not depend on the battery.

This matters most for smart lock door access, where convenience depends on a working power source. Eyonic notes that smart locks suit controlled convenience when user permissions and backup entry are planned properly. A battery plan is part of that planning, not an afterthought.

Use the worksheet as a planning aid. Fill it in before the lock is ordered, revisit it at each change of tenancy, and keep a copy with the unit's handover documents.

Worksheet Part 1: Count the Openings, Not the Tenants

Battery drain follows openings and radio activity more than it follows headcount. Start by estimating a typical day.

Column A, door openings per day. Count every unlock event: the tenant arriving home, leaving for work, a family member coming and going, a cleaner, a delivery left inside, a contractor. A unit with two working adults and one child might see eight to twelve openings on a weekday and fewer at weekends. Write a low, typical and high figure rather than a single number.

Column B, openings per week. Multiply the typical daily figure by seven, then adjust for the pattern you actually observe. If the unit is empty for part of the year, note those months separately.

Column C, remote or app unlocks per week. Every remote command wakes the radio and costs more than a keypad or fingerprint entry. A landlord who unlocks remotely for viewings adds to the count.

Column D, keypad and credential use. Note whether the tenant uses a code, a card, a fingerprint or a phone. Mixed use is common and worth recording.

Column E, door condition notes. A door that sticks, a misaligned strike plate or a heavy door that needs a push can make the lock work harder. Record anything the tenant reports.

Column F, communication method. Whether the lock talks to a hub, a phone directly, or not at all affects standby drain. Record what was actually installed, not what you assumed.

Leave a column for the date each estimate was made. A worksheet that is dated is far more useful at the next tenancy than one that is not.

Worksheet Part 2: Turn Usage Into a Replacement Interval

This is the estimation step. Treat every number here as an author heuristic for planning, not as a manufacturer specification.

Step 1. Take the typical weekly opening count from Part 1. Add remote unlocks at a weight of roughly two, since a remote command typically costs more than a local entry. Call this the weighted weekly load.

Step 2. Ask the supplier for the lock's rated cycle count and the rated standby period. Record both, with the source and date. If the supplier gives only one figure, note that gap rather than filling it with a guess.

Step 3. Divide the rated cycle count by the weighted weekly load to get an estimated weeks of active use. Then compare that with the rated standby period. The shorter of the two is your planning ceiling.

Step 4. Apply a planning margin. A common approach is to schedule replacement at around seventy to eighty per cent of the estimated ceiling, so the lock is serviced before the tenant experiences a failure. Label this as a margin you chose, and write down why.

Step 5. Set the replacement month. Work back from the estimated ceiling, subtract the margin, and write a calendar month and year. Put the same date in the tenancy handover pack and in the manager's own calendar.

Step 6. Add a review trigger. Any change of tenancy, any reported door stiffness, and any change in the number of occupants should send the worksheet back to Part 1.

A worked fictional example: a two-bedroom unit in a mixed residential block, ten typical openings a day, three remote unlocks a week, a lock rated for a cycle count the supplier confirms in writing, and a chosen margin of seventy-five per cent. The worksheet produces a replacement month roughly nine months out. That figure is an illustration of the method, not a prediction for any real lock.

Worksheet Part 3: Backup Entry That Does Not Rely on the Battery

A battery plan is only half a plan. The other half is what happens when the battery is flat, the lock misbehaves, or the tenant cannot get in at an awkward hour.

Record the backup method in writing. Common options include a mechanical key override, an external power terminal, or a second credential path. Whatever the lock provides, write down exactly how it works and who is allowed to use it. Then test it once with the tenant present at handover, and note the date of that test.

Decide who holds the mechanical key. In a rental unit this is a governance question as much as a technical one. Options include the landlord, a managing agent, or a key safe in a location the tenant knows about. Whichever you choose, record the holder's role rather than a personal name in the tenant-facing document, and keep the personal record separately.

Agree the low-battery response. Write a short sequence: what the lock does when the battery is low, what the tenant should do first, who they contact, and what the manager does if the tenant is locked out. Keep it to a few lines so it can be read on a phone at the door.

Eyonic's review guidance notes that smart locks work best when door condition, user roles and backup method are reviewed before installation, and that for business use the choice between a standalone smart lock and an access-control panel is worth checking. A rental unit with several unrelated tenants and a shared entrance may fall closer to that business case, so record the reasoning rather than defaulting to a standalone lock.

If the unit also has a burglar alarm system or an access control system at the building entrance, note how the smart lock interacts with them. A lock that is expected to disarm an alarm on entry needs that behaviour confirmed and documented, not assumed.

Worksheet Part 4: The Handover and Tenancy Change Checklist

A battery plan that lives only in the manager's head dies at the first change of tenant. Use this checklist at every handover.

Before the new tenant moves in: confirm the lock is charged or freshly powered, record the battery install date, test the backup entry method, and give the tenant the low-battery response card. Note the planned replacement month on the handover sheet.

At the start of each tenancy: re-run Part 1 with the new tenant's expected pattern. A single occupant who travels will load the lock very differently from a family of four. Update the replacement month if the estimate moves by more than a month or two.

During the tenancy: ask the tenant to report any change in how the door feels. A door that has started to stick is a battery and reliability signal, not just a maintenance nuisance. Log the report and the date.

At the end of a tenancy: revoke the outgoing tenant's credentials before the unit is shown to anyone new. Record the revocation date. This is the point where a battery plan and a user-permission plan meet, and it is the step most easily skipped in a busy month.

At replacement: record the date, the battery type, and the reading or warning that triggered the change. Over two or three cycles this record becomes far more useful than any generic estimate, because it reflects your actual unit.

Keep the completed worksheet with the unit file. If the building also runs CCTV security systems or a keyphone system, keep the smart lock sheet in the same folder so a new manager can find the whole picture in one place.

Where the Worksheet Fits in a Wider Security Plan

A rental unit rarely stands alone. It sits in a building or a compound with other systems, and the battery plan should connect to them rather than float free.

If the unit is part of a managed block, the entrance may already have an access control system, and the smart lock is a second layer at the unit door. Record which system controls which door, so a tenant is not issued two credentials for the same purpose without explanation.

If the compound has an autogate installation, note how residents reach the unit door after the gate. A gate remote and a smart lock are separate credentials with separate replacement and revocation steps, and both belong in the handover pack.

If the building has CCTV, check whether the unit door is in view of a camera and whether that footage is retained long enough to be useful after an incident. This is a question for the building's system owner, not something to assume.

For managers planning a wider project across several units, a short site survey before ordering locks is worth the time. Common Planning Mistakes to Check Against

Before you file the worksheet, run through these checks. Each one is a question, not an accusation.

Did you use a single opening count instead of a range? A range survives a change of tenant better than a point estimate.

Did you record the source and date for the rated cycle count? A figure without a source cannot be defended when the estimate turns out wrong.

Did you write the replacement month somewhere the next manager will look? A date in a personal calendar is not a plan.

Did you test the backup entry with the tenant present? A backup method that has never been tried is a hope, not a control.

Did you separate the tenant-facing document from the personal record of who holds the mechanical key? Mixing them creates a privacy problem at handover.

Did you note the door condition at installation and again at each tenancy change? Door behaviour is the most common practical variable in how a lock performs.

Did you check whether a standalone lock or an access-control panel suits the entrance? For a shared or commercial entrance, this is worth a deliberate decision rather than a default.

A worksheet that passes these checks is short, dated, and owned by a named role. That is enough to make smart lock door access predictable in a rental unit, and to make the next handover a fifteen-minute task rather than an investigation.
