Match a CCTV Quotation to an Approved Room List Before Scheduling Work

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical guide for Sarawak premises managers on checking room names, scope versions, approval ownership and open items in a CCTV quotation before any work date is booked.

Start with the approved room list, not the quotation

A quotation is easier to read once you know which rooms it is supposed to describe. Before opening any proposal, take the room list that your organisation has already approved and give it a version label and a date. A fictional example: a two-storey shop-office in Kuching lists a front counter, a stockroom, a rear corridor, a staff room and an outdoor parking edge, approved as version 3 on a Tuesday. That list becomes the reference document. The quotation is then checked against it, rather than the other way round. This order matters because a supplier can only quote what was described to them, and a room that was never named cannot be expected to appear. Write down who approved the list and where the signed copy is kept. If two versions exist, mark the older one as superseded so nobody schedules work against it. Keep the list short and use the names your staff already say out loud, because mismatched labels are the most common reason a quotation and a room list appear not to agree.

Normalise room names before you compare anything

Room names drift. One document says lobby, another says reception, a third says front counter. Before comparing a quotation line by line, build a small naming table with three columns: the name on the approved list, the name used in the quotation, and a note on whether they mean the same place. A fictional example: approved list says staff room; quotation says pantry; note says same room, renamed after a refit. This step is administrative, not technical. You are not judging whether a position is suitable, only whether the two documents are talking about the same space. Where a name cannot be matched with confidence, leave the row open rather than guessing. Open rows are useful: they become the questions you send back in writing. Keep the table to one page and date it. When a supplier plans, supplies and installs monitoring systems with camera placement, recording and remote viewing for homes, offices, shops and industrial sites, the room list is the shared vocabulary both sides work from, so agreeing the words early saves a later round of clarification.

Check the scope version and what each line claims

Every quotation should state which version of the room list it responds to. If it does not, ask. Then read each line and mark it in one of three ways: named on the approved list, not on the list, or unclear. A fictional example: a quotation includes a line for a rear corridor that appears on version 3, and a line for a side entrance that appears on no approved version. The second line is not wrong, but it is outside the agreed scope and needs an owner before anyone books a date. Do not attempt to judge whether a proposed position is good or poor; that belongs to the site survey discussion. Your job at this stage is documentary: does the quotation describe the rooms you approved, and does it say so in words you can trace? Note also what the quotation leaves silent. Silence is not a fault, but it is an open item. A quotation that mentions a site survey, camera placement, NVR setup and remote viewing gives you named topics to confirm in writing, and each one can be tied back to a room on the list.

Name the approval owner for each room

A room list without owners is just a wish. For each room, write the name of the role that can approve work there, not a person's private details. A fictional example: front counter approved by the branch manager role; stockroom by the operations role; outdoor parking edge by the landlord's representative. This matters most where premises are shared or leased, because the person who pays the bill is not always the person who can agree to work in a space. Where an owner is missing, mark the room as pending and do not schedule it. Keep the owner column separate from the quotation so that a change of staff does not silently change the scope. When you send questions back, address them to the named role and keep a copy of the reply with the version of the list it refers to. This is ordinary procurement housekeeping, and it gives you a defensible record if a later invoice or handover conversation asks why a particular room was or was not included.

Write the open-items list before any date is agreed

The most useful output of this check is a short list of what remains unspecified. Use four headings: rooms with no matching quotation line, quotation lines with no approved room, rooms with no approval owner, and topics the quotation does not address. A fictional example: the staff room appears on the list but not in the quotation; the side entrance appears in the quotation but not on the list; the outdoor parking edge has no named owner; the quotation does not say what happens after a site survey. None of these are accusations. They are questions. Send them in one message, numbered, and ask for written answers rather than a phone explanation. Keep the list to a single page and date it. When the answers arrive, update the list rather than starting a new document, so the history stays visible. Only when the open-items list is empty, or every remaining item has a named owner and a decision, is it reasonable to talk about scheduling.

Use the same discipline for adjacent systems

The same room-list method works for other systems quoted alongside CCTV. If a proposal includes smart lock door access, the review question is not only which door, but whether the door condition, user roles and backup method have been described before installation. For business premises, it is reasonable to ask the provider whether a standalone smart lock or an access-control panel is the more appropriate route, and to record the answer against the relevant door on your list. A fictional example: a staff-only store door is listed with an owner, a user group and a stated backup arrangement, while a shared corridor door is left pending because the landlord has not confirmed. Keep these notes at the same level of detail as the CCTV rows, and do not treat a supplier's general description as approval for a specific door. The aim is a single, versioned document that shows what was approved, by which role, and what is still open across every system in the project.

A short pre-scheduling checklist

Before a work date is booked, run this checklist and score it as an author's heuristic, not as an industry standard. One point each: the approved room list has a version label and a date; every quotation line maps to a room or is flagged; every room has a named approval owner role; the open-items list has been sent and answered in writing; the quotation states which list version it responds to; and any adjacent system, such as door access, has its own rows on the same document. A fictional example: a premises manager scores five of six and holds the date until the missing owner for an outdoor area is confirmed. A score is only a prompt to look again; it is not a pass mark. The point of the exercise is a clean handoff: a dated list, a dated quotation, a named owner for each room, and a written record of what remains unspecified. That package is what makes a scheduling conversation short, and it is what a future property manager will thank you for.
