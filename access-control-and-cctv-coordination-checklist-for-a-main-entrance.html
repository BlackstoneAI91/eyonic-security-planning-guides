Access Control and CCTV Coordination Checklist for a Main Entrance

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical checklist for Sarawak premises managers to make sure access control events at a main entrance trigger the right camera recording, alerts and handover tests.

Why the main entrance needs one coordinated plan

The main entrance is where two separate systems meet. An access control system decides who may pass; a CCTV system records what happens when they do. If the two are planned apart, you can end up with a door that opens cleanly but a camera that faces the wrong way, or footage that exists but is not tied to the moment the door was used. This checklist is for premises managers in Sarawak planning an ordinary project, not a high-security facility. It treats coordination as a documentation and handover task: decide what should happen, write it down, then test it before you accept the work. Eyonic Security Systems plans, supplies and installs monitoring systems with camera placement, recording and remote viewing for homes, offices, shops and industrial sites, so the entrance conversation usually sits inside a wider CCTV installation scope. Keep the checklist short enough to finish in one meeting, and give every line a named owner.

Step 1: Map the entrance before choosing hardware

Start with a simple plan of the door area. Mark the approach path, the door leaf and its swing, the reader or keypad position, the exit button, and any reception or guard point. Note where a person stands while presenting a credential and where they walk after the door releases. These two positions are your candidate camera views. A camera that sees only the top of a head, or only the back of a person already inside, is less useful than one that captures the face at the reader and the direction of travel through the doorway. Record lighting at different times of day, including whether direct sun or a shaded corridor changes what the camera sees. This is the same thinking behind a CCTV site survey, and it is worth doing before any quote is compared. If the entrance also uses a smart lock door access arrangement, note the door condition and the backup entry method at this stage, because those choices affect where hardware sits and what the camera should cover.

Step 2: Decide which access events should trigger recording or alerts

Not every door event deserves the same response. Write a short table with three columns: event, camera action, and who is notified. Example events include a valid card entry outside working hours, a door held open beyond a set time, a failed credential attempt repeated several times, and a door forced open. For each, decide whether the camera simply records continuously, records on the event, or sends an alert to a named person. Keep the list realistic. A small office may only need after-hours entry alerts and a record of failed attempts; a shop may care more about the rear door than the front. Mark which events are handled by the access control system alone and which need the camera or recorder to act. This table becomes the acceptance reference later, so write it in plain language that a technician and a manager can both read.

Step 3: Agree the recording and retention expectations

Coordination only works if the recorder keeps the footage long enough to be useful. Decide how many days of recording you expect to hold, and check that the storage and camera settings support it. Ask where the recorder will sit, who can log in, and how playback is searched by time and camera. If remote viewing is part of the plan, confirm which devices will be used and who holds the credentials. These points belong in the NVR setup conversation, not in a separate afterthought once the door is working. A useful habit is to write one sentence per camera describing what it must capture, then check that sentence against the actual view at handover. If a camera is meant to show faces at the reader, test it with a person standing in the normal presenting position rather than judging from a still image taken at a distance.

Step 4: Match the access method to the door and the users

The access method should follow the door and the people who use it, not the other way round. For a business entrance, it is worth checking whether a standalone smart lock or an access-control panel is more appropriate, because the two suit different patterns of use. Smart locks work best when the door condition, user roles and backup method are reviewed before installation. Write down who needs access, how often roles change, and what happens when a credential is lost or a person leaves. Include a backup entry method and test it. If the entrance is shared with a gate, note how the gate and door interact so that a person is not left waiting between two systems. Keep this section as a roles-and-methods register rather than a hardware wish list.

Step 5: Run a coordination test before sign-off

Testing is where coordination is proved or exposed. Use a written sequence and record the result of each step. A practical example sequence: present a valid credential and confirm the door releases and the camera records the entry; hold the door open and confirm the expected alert or log entry; present an invalid credential and confirm the failed attempt is recorded; use the exit button and confirm the camera still captures the person leaving; check playback for each event by time and camera. Add a column for defects and a named person responsible for closing each one. Treat the timings in your test as examples set by you, not as industry standards. If remote viewing is included, repeat one event while viewing from outside the premises so the manager sees the same result the technician sees.

Step 6: Hand over the documents, not just the passwords

A coordinated entrance is easier to run when the paperwork matches the hardware. Prepare a small handover pack: the entrance plan with camera positions marked, the event table from Step 2, the recording and retention note, the user and credential register, the backup entry method, and the completed test sheet with defects closed. Confirm who can add or remove users, and how that is recorded. Keep the pack with the premises file so a new manager can pick it up without calling the installer for basic questions. This is also the moment to confirm that the camera placement still matches the plan after any last-minute changes to signage, counters or door furniture. If something moved, note it and decide whether the view still meets the sentence you wrote for that camera.

A short worksheet you can copy

Use these lines as a starting worksheet. Entrance plan attached and dated. Camera positions marked with the view each must capture. Event table completed with camera action and named recipient for each alert. Recording days agreed and checked against settings. Remote viewing devices and credential holders listed. Access method chosen with door condition, user roles and backup entry reviewed. Coordination test run with results recorded and defects closed. Handover pack filed with a named owner. Score the worksheet by counting closed lines against total lines; treat the score as your own project heuristic, not a certified measure. Review it again after the first month of use, when real entrance patterns are clearer than they were at planning.
