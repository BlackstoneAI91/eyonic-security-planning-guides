Plan a Company Directory Transition When Two Office Numbers Become One

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical guide for Sarawak premises managers on mapping old public references to one approved contact route, briefing staff, and tracking exceptions when two office numbers merge into one.

Start with a public-reference inventory, not a phone decision

Before anyone debates which number survives, list every place the two old numbers appear in public view. Treat this as a reading exercise, not a technical one. Walk the premises and note signage at the entrance, reception counter, notice boards, lift lobby panels, vehicle decals, letterheads, quotation templates, invoices, email signatures, delivery instructions and any printed forms handed to visitors. Add digital surfaces you control: website contact pages, social profiles, directory listings and the footer of documents you issue. For each entry, record the exact number shown, where it sits, who owns that surface and whether it is printed or editable. A simple four-column sheet is enough: reference location, number shown, owner, action needed. Keep the inventory factual. You are not judging whether a number is good or bad; you are recording what a caller or visitor would see today. This step usually takes longer than expected because numbers hide in places nobody re-reads, such as old maintenance notices pinned near a service entrance. Finish the inventory before agreeing any wording, so the approved message is written once and applied consistently rather than improvised surface by surface.

Agree one approved contact route and write it down

The transition only works if everyone describes the same destination in the same words. Hold a short meeting with the people who answer calls, greet visitors and sign off public material, and agree a single approved contact route: the number that will be published, the hours it is answered, what a caller should expect, and who receives messages when nobody picks up. Write this as a one-page contact statement. Include the exact wording to use on signage and documents, and a separate line for internal use so staff know where to transfer or refer a caller. Avoid inventing promises such as guaranteed response times unless the responsible owner has confirmed them. If the premises also runs a keyphone system, keep the public statement about callers and outcomes, and leave the internal configuration questions to the people responsible for that equipment. The statement should also name who may approve a change to the published wording, because ad hoc edits are how two versions of the truth reappear. Circulate the draft to reception, security and any tenant-facing teams, collect corrections, then freeze a dated version. Everything that follows references that frozen version.

Map old references to the approved route with a replacement plan

Now convert the inventory into a replacement plan. For each reference, decide one of four outcomes: replace with the approved route, remove entirely, keep temporarily with a dated review, or leave unchanged because it is historical and not public-facing. Give every line an owner and a target date, and mark whether the change is printed, digital or physical. Group the work by surface type so it can be batched: one print run for forms and letterheads, one signage order, one round of website edits. This is where a fictional example helps. Suppose a two-storey office in Kuching has the old second number on a reception plaque, a car park notice and a supplier form. The plaque and notice are replaced in one signage batch; the form is updated at its next print run and the old stock is withdrawn from the counter drawer on a stated date. Record the withdrawal so nobody quietly reissues old stock. Keep the plan visible to the people doing the work, and review it weekly until every line is closed or has a dated exception. Do not delete the inventory; it becomes your evidence that the transition was completed.

Brief staff on what to say, not just what changed

A directory change fails quietly when the front desk still gives out the old number out of habit. Prepare a short briefing for everyone who speaks to callers or visitors. Cover four things: the approved route in exact words, the reason for the change in one neutral sentence, what to say if someone mentions the old number, and where to log a question that the briefing does not answer. Keep the briefing to a single page and repeat it at shift handovers so part-time and shift staff hear it too. A useful habit is a two-line script: one line for giving the new route, one line for acknowledging the old number without apology loops. Ask staff to flag any surface they notice still showing the old number, and give them a named person to tell. This turns the whole team into extra eyes on the inventory. If the premises uses a queue management system or counter signage, check that the wording staff speak matches what visitors read, so a caller is not told one thing while the wall says another. Briefing is not a one-off event; schedule a short refresher after the first month.

Keep an exception tracker for anything that cannot change yet

Some references will resist a clean replacement: a landlord-owned sign, a shared directory board, a printed brochure with remaining stock, a listing controlled by another organisation. Do not force these into the plan or pretend they are done. Open an exception line for each one with five fields: what the reference is, why it cannot change now, who owns the decision, the interim wording callers will hear, and a review date. The interim wording matters most. If a caller dials an old number that still connects, staff should know what to say so the caller reaches the approved route without confusion. Review exceptions on the stated date and close them or extend them with a new date and reason. Keep the tracker short enough that it is actually read; ten open lines with owners beats a hundred rows nobody maintains. Share a summary with the approving manager monthly until the list is empty. This record also protects the team later, because it shows which references were known, owned and scheduled rather than overlooked.

Verify by observation and close the transition formally

Completion is a reading test, not a feeling. After the replacement work is done, walk the premises again with the original inventory and confirm each public reference now shows the approved route or has a live exception. Ask a colleague who did not do the work to make the same check, because fresh eyes catch what familiarity hides. Then run a small caller check: have someone ring the published route during stated hours and confirm the greeting matches the approved statement, without recording anything private. Note the date, the surfaces checked and any remaining exceptions. Close the transition with a short handover note for the next premises manager: the frozen contact statement, the final inventory, the exception tracker and the briefing page. Store them together so a future change starts from a known position rather than a rebuild. If the site also runs CCTV security systems or an access control system, keep this directory record separate from any security documentation, and refer technical questions to the responsible provider rather than folding them into the contact guide.

A worksheet you can copy for the first week

Use this sequence as an author's heuristic, not a researched schedule. Day one: build the public-reference inventory and photograph each surface for your own records. Day two: draft the one-page contact statement and circulate it for correction. Day three: freeze the statement and convert the inventory into a replacement plan with owners and dates. Day four: brief reception, security and shift teams, and open the exception tracker. Day five: batch the first round of changes and set the review date. Score your readiness honestly on four questions: does every public reference have an owner, is the approved wording frozen and dated, does every staff member who speaks to callers know the script, and does every unresolved reference sit on the tracker with a review date? A score of four out of four is a reasonable signal to proceed; anything less points to the specific gap to close first. Keep the worksheet with the handover note so the next transition, however small, starts from a proven pattern rather than a blank page.
