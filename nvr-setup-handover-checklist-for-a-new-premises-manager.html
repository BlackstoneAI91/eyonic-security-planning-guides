NVR Setup Handover Checklist for a New Premises Manager

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical handover checklist for a new premises manager in Sarawak: confirm where the recorder sits, transfer the admin account, run a playback test, and record where the written login details are kept so footage can be retrieved without calling the installer.

Why the recorder, not the camera, decides your handover

The cameras are the visible part of a CCTV project. The network video recorder is the part that decides whether last week's footage still exists when someone asks for it. A handover that ends with a working live view on a monitor is not finished; it is only half demonstrated. The new premises manager needs to know three things before the installer leaves: where the recorder physically sits, who holds the administrator account, and how to pull a clip from a past date without help.

This checklist is written for the manager taking over an ordinary small-to-mid premises in Sarawak, such as a shop, office or light industrial unit. It assumes the system was planned and installed by a supplier, and that you are the person who will be asked for footage months from now. Treat the items as a sequence: each one depends on the one before it. Where a step needs a decision, the decision should be written down and signed, not remembered.

Part 1: Locate and document the recorder

Before anything else, walk to the recorder with the installer and stand in front of it. A recorder in a locked riser, a back office or a ceiling void is fine, but you must be able to reach it, see its status lights and power it down safely if needed. Record the following on a single sheet:

Room and position: the room name and a plain description of where the unit sits, for example the second shelf of the back-office cabinet. Power source: which socket or circuit feeds it, and whether it is on a UPS or surge protector. Network point: which switch port or router port the recorder uses, and whether the connection is wired. Storage: how many drives are fitted and their approximate capacity, so you can sanity-check retention later. Serial or asset label: whatever identifier the supplier uses, copied exactly.

Ask the installer to show you the recorder's normal state: which lights should be on, and what a disk fault or network loss looks like. Photograph the front panel in its healthy state and keep the photo with the sheet. If the recorder is in a shared or tenanted space, note who else has a key, because that affects who can reach the unit.

This is also the moment to confirm that the recorder's location was agreed during the site survey rather than chosen on the day. If the position is awkward, raise it now, while the installer is still on site.

Part 2: Transfer the administrator account properly

The administrator account is the one that can add users, change recording schedules and delete footage. On handover day it should stop belonging to the installer and start belonging to the premises. Do this in front of each other, not by message afterwards.

Use a named account, not a shared one. A generic admin login that three people know cannot be audited. Create one administrator account tied to the manager's role, and a second named account for a deputy who can act when the manager is away.

Change the default password at handover. If the recorder arrived with a factory password, it should be changed before the installer leaves, and the new password written into the login record described in Part 5.

Confirm what each account can do. Ask the installer to show you the user list and the permission level beside each name. A viewer account that can only watch live and play back is usually enough for most staff; reserve configuration rights for one or two people.

Remove or disable temporary accounts. Any account created for the installation itself should be disabled once the handover is signed.

If the system supports remote CCTV viewing, the same discipline applies to the viewing app: named users, a known device list, and a clear owner for removing access when someone leaves. That topic deserves its own register, but the principle starts here.

Part 3: Run a playback test you perform yourself

Watching live video proves the camera works. Playing back yesterday's footage proves the recorder works. The manager, not the installer, should drive this test.

Pick a camera and a time. Choose a camera covering a door or counter, and a time earlier in the day when someone walked through. Ask the installer to stay quiet while you find it.

Search by date and time, then play, pause and step. You are checking that the timeline is populated, that the timestamp matches the real clock, and that you can export or copy a short clip to a USB drive if that is part of your process.

Check the clock against your phone. A recorder whose time drifts will mislead anyone reviewing an incident. Ask how time synchronisation is set and who can change it.

Confirm the recording schedule. Is it continuous, motion-triggered, or a mix? Which cameras record at night? Write the answer down, because it determines what you can honestly promise when someone asks for footage.

Note the retention you actually observed. If the recorder holds roughly a set number of days, record that figure and the date you checked it. Retention shrinks when cameras are added or resolution is raised, so this is a living number, not a one-off.

If any part of the playback test fails, do not sign. A failed playback is a commissioning issue, and it is far easier to resolve while the installer is on site.

Part 4: Walk the camera placement against the plan

Playback is only useful if the cameras point at something worth reviewing. Take the camera placement plan from the site survey and walk it camera by camera with the live view open on a phone or monitor.

For each camera, confirm the view matches the agreed purpose: an entrance showing faces, a till showing the counter, a loading area showing the door. Check that the image is not washed out by a window or a lamp, and that the mounting height gives a usable angle rather than a view of the top of heads.

Mark any camera whose view has drifted since commissioning, and note the date. Small adjustments are normal after cleaning or building work; a camera that has moved on its own needs a bracket check.

Record the camera names as they appear in the recorder. If they are labelled Camera 1 to Camera 8, rename them to match the plan so that a future request for footage can be answered by location rather than by guesswork.

This walk is also the right time to note blind spots you accept. A written list of what the system does not cover protects you from over-promising later.

Part 5: The written login record and where it lives

The single most common reason a new manager calls the installer is a lost password. Prevent that with a written login record kept in a fixed, agreed place.

The record should list, for each account: the account name, the person or role it belongs to, its permission level, and the date it was created. Passwords should not be written in the same document as the account list if you can avoid it; use a sealed envelope or a password manager held by the manager, with the location noted on the record.

Decide where the record lives and tell the deputy. A locked drawer in the manager's office, a safe, or a managed password vault are all workable; what matters is that the location is written on the handover sheet and that two named people know it.

Add a change log. Every time an account is added, disabled or has its password reset, the date and the reason go on the sheet. This turns a static list into a record that survives staff turnover.

Finally, agree the escalation route. If the recorder fails, who is called first, and what information should be ready? Write the supplier's role and your internal contact on the same sheet so the answer is not buried in an email thread.

Part 6: Sign-off sheet and a 30-day review

Close the handover with a one-page sign-off that both parties keep. It should state what was demonstrated, what was not, and what remains open.

A simple structure works: a list of the items above with a pass, fail or not-applicable mark; the name and role of the person who performed the playback test; the date; and a short list of open items with an owner. Anything marked fail should have a follow-up date before the sheet is signed.

Then book a review about thirty days later. This is an author's suggestion, not a standard, but it catches the problems that only appear in normal use: a camera that fogs in the morning, a schedule that misses the evening shift, a password that nobody can find. At the review, re-run the playback test and update the retention figure.

Keep the sign-off, the login record and the camera list together in one folder, physical or digital. When the next manager takes over, that folder is the handover. If the system was installed as part of a wider CCTV installation project, the same folder should hold the survey notes and the placement plan, so the story of why each camera sits where it does is not lost.

For premises in Sarawak planning a new or replacement system, the practical lesson is to agree the handover format before work starts, so the installer knows what will be tested and the manager knows what will be handed over.
