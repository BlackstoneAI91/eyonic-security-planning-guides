Keeping the Counter Open During CCTV Installation: A Downtime Plan for an Operating Counter

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A phased work sequence and daily cutover checklist for Sarawak premises managers who need a service counter to keep trading while CCTV installation, cabling and camera mounting proceed.

Start With the Counter, Not the Cameras

The usual mistake in planning a CCTV installation downtime plan for an operating counter is to begin with the camera list. A better starting point is the counter itself: the hours it must be staffed, the equipment on it, the queue that forms in front of it, and the routes staff use to reach the back office. Write those down before anyone discusses camera models.

A simple one-page counter profile is enough. List the opening hours, the busiest two-hour window, the number of staffed positions, the power outlets in use, and any fixed furniture that cannot be moved. Note where customers stand and where they wait. This profile becomes the constraint that every later decision is tested against, and it gives the installer a fixed picture instead of a vague brief.

This is also the moment to separate what must stay live from what can pause. A payment terminal, a receipt printer and the counter phone usually belong in the first group. Decorative lighting, a spare monitor or a second printer can often wait. Marking these on the profile prevents an argument on the morning the technician arrives.

Split the Work Into Phases That Each End in a Trading Counter

Downtime is easier to manage when the project is broken into phases that each finish with a working counter, rather than one long disruption. A workable sequence for a small shop or office counter looks like this.

Phase one is survey and marking. The installer walks the site, confirms camera placement positions, and marks cable routes with tape or chalk. No drilling, no counter closure. Phase two is backbone cabling along routes that avoid the counter face, run after closing or before opening where possible. Phase three is camera mounting and recorder work, again scheduled around trading hours. Phase four is configuration, testing and handover, which is the only phase that genuinely needs the counter staff involved.

Grouping the work this way means the counter is only exposed during short, planned windows. It also gives you natural pause points: if a phase overruns, you can stop at the end of it and trade normally the next day rather than pushing through a half-finished cable run.

Camera placement decisions made in phase one should be recorded on a simple sketch with a mounting height for each position. That sketch is what the installer prices and installs from, and it is what you check against at the end. Agree the Daily Cutover Window in Writing

A cutover window is the period each working day when the counter may be affected. It should be written down, agreed by both sides, and short enough that staff can plan around it. As an author heuristic rather than a rule from research, many premises managers find that a window ending at least an hour before the busiest period gives a useful buffer for overruns.

The written window should state four things: the start and end time, which counter positions are affected, what the installer is allowed to switch off, and who on your side confirms the counter is back in service. That last point matters. Without a named person, the counter can sit half-restored while everyone assumes someone else is checking.

Keep a daily cutover checklist and run it at the end of every window. A short version: counter terminals powered and logged in, receipt printer tested with a sample transaction, counter phone dial tone confirmed, card payment tested, queue area clear of tools and cable offcuts, floor swept, and any temporary cable taped and labelled. Tick each item and initial it. The checklist takes a few minutes and gives you a dated record if a fault appears later.

If the counter uses a queue management system or a keyphone system, add a line for each: a test ticket from the dispenser, and a test call from the counter handset. These are quick checks that catch a disconnected cable before the next customer arrives.

Protect the Counter Face and the Customer Path

Most of the visible disruption in a counter project happens at eye level and underfoot, not at the ceiling where cameras go. Plan for both.

At the counter face, agree in advance which surfaces may be drilled and which may not. A common approach is to route cable above the counter and drop down behind fixed joinery rather than across a finished panel. Ask the installer to confirm the route on the sketch before any hole is made. Where a surface must be penetrated, agree who makes good afterwards, because patching and painting is often outside an installation scope and is easy to leave unresolved.

Underfoot, the customer path needs a clear walking route at all times. Ladders, cable drums and tool bags should be kept to one side, and any temporary cable crossing a walkway should be covered or rerouted before the counter opens. A simple daily photo of the customer path, taken on a phone, is a cheap way to record that the area was left safe.

Dust is the other practical issue. Drilling above a counter that holds terminals and printed stock is worth scheduling for a closed period, or protecting with sheeting agreed in advance. Add a dust and clean-up line to the daily checklist so it is not left to memory.

Keep Recording and Viewing Working Through the Change

A counter project often coincides with a recorder change, and that is where continuity of recording needs a decision rather than an assumption. Before work starts, confirm how many days of footage the existing system holds and whether the new recorder will be configured to match or exceed it. If the old recorder is being replaced, agree whether it stays powered until the new one is confirmed working.

NVR setup should be treated as its own task with its own acceptance check, not as a loose end at the end of the day. At minimum, confirm that each camera is recording, that the recording schedule matches trading hours plus the closed period, that time and date are correct, and that the retention period has been checked against what you asked for. Record the results on paper and have the installer initial them.

Remote viewing deserves the same treatment. If managers or owners will check the counter from a phone, test it on the devices that will actually be used, on the network that will actually be available, before sign-off. A viewing test done only on the installer's phone in the car park proves very little about the counter manager's device on the shop network.

A One-Page Downtime Plan You Can Hand to the Installer

Before the first day on site, fill in a single page and give a copy to the installer and to the counter supervisor. The page should carry the following fields, kept short enough to read at a glance.

Counter profile: opening hours, busiest window, staffed positions, and equipment that must stay live. Work phases: the four phases above with a target date for each. Daily cutover window: start time, end time, affected positions, and the named person who confirms the counter is back in service. Protected surfaces and cable routes: what may be drilled, what may not, and who makes good. Recording continuity: existing retention, target retention, and whether the old recorder stays powered during changeover. Handover checks: recording confirmed, remote viewing tested on named devices, sketch of camera positions signed, and daily checklists filed.

Keep the page to one side and update it as the work progresses. A plan that is edited during the project is more useful than a perfect one written in advance and never opened again.

If the same premises also has a smart lock door access project or an access control system change planned, resist running both at the counter at once. Sequence them so each has its own cutover window and its own checklist. Eyonic's published review of smart locks notes that door condition, user roles and backup entry should be reviewed before installation, and the same discipline of reviewing before installing applies to any counter-adjacent work.

What Good Looks Like at the End of the Project

A counter project is finished when three things are true, not when the last camera is screwed to the wall.

First, the counter has traded through every working day of the project, or the exceptions are documented and explained. Second, the daily cutover checklists are filed and signed, giving you a dated record of what was tested each day. Third, the handover pack contains the camera position sketch, the recording and retention confirmation, the remote viewing test result, and a named contact for faults.

A short closing walk with the counter supervisor is worth the time. Walk the customer path, look at each camera position from the counter's point of view, and confirm that nothing blocks a view that was promised. If a position needs adjusting, it is far easier to raise it now than after the installer has packed up.

For premises managers in Sarawak planning an ordinary counter project, the value of this approach is that it turns a vague worry about disruption into a set of dated, checkable steps. The cameras are the visible outcome, but the plan that kept the counter open is what the staff will remember.
