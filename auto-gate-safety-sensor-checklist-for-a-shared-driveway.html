Auto Gate Safety Sensor Checklist for a Shared Driveway

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical checklist for Sarawak premises managers to verify that safety sensors on a shared-driveway auto gate are correctly placed, tested and documented before handover.

Why a shared driveway changes the safety question

A shared driveway is used by more than one household, tenant or business, and that changes what a gate safety check has to cover. On a single home driveway, the people who use the gate are usually known to each other. On a shared driveway, the users include residents, their visitors, delivery riders, contractors and sometimes staff of a business operating from the same compound. Each of those groups approaches the gate differently, at different speeds, and with different expectations about whether the gate will stop for them.

Safety sensors exist to reduce the chance that a moving gate closes on a person, a vehicle or an animal. They do not replace supervision, signage or sensible operating rules, and no checklist can guarantee that an accident will never happen. What a checklist can do is make sure the sensors are present, positioned where they actually detect, tested in a repeatable way, and documented so the next manager knows what was verified and when.

This guide is written for premises managers in Sarawak who are planning an ordinary gate project or taking over an existing one. It treats the sensor check as a documentation and handover exercise, not a technical installation manual. Where the work involves wiring, motor adjustment or electrical safety, that belongs with the installer. Your job is to define what must be true before you accept the gate and to record the evidence.

The checklist below is written so it can be used whether the gate is new or already in service.

Before the visit: define the shared users and the gate's duty

Start by writing down who uses the driveway and how. A simple table works: user group, typical vehicle or on foot, usual times, and whether they carry a remote, a code or rely on someone else opening the gate. Include residents, tenants, staff, regular contractors, delivery riders and visitors. This table becomes the reference for every later decision, because a sensor arrangement that suits a slow-moving resident car may not suit a motorcycle courier arriving at speed.

Next, describe the gate itself in plain terms: the type of gate, how it opens, how far it travels, and what sits on each side of the opening. Note the ground surface, any slope, and whether the gate swings or slides across a pedestrian route. If pedestrians share the vehicle opening, say so explicitly, because that is the single most important fact for the sensor plan.

Finally, decide what the gate is expected to do when it detects an obstacle. Most safety arrangements are built around stopping or reversing, but the exact behaviour should be agreed in writing with the installer rather than assumed. Ask the installer to state, in the quotation or scope, what the gate does when each sensor is triggered, and who is responsible for adjusting that behaviour later.

This is also the point to confirm that the gate work is being planned as part of a wider site scope. If the same compound is adding cameras or door access, the gate sensor check should not be done in isolation, because the handover documents will be easier to manage if they share a naming convention and a single owner.

Sensor placement checks on the day

Placement is where most sensor problems begin, and it is best checked on site with the gate in manual or slow operation, with the installer present. Work through the following as a list of questions rather than a set of instructions.

First, does each sensor cover the full width of the opening, including the edges where a child or a pet might slip past? Walk the opening and note any point where a person could stand without being detected. Second, are the sensors mounted at a height and angle that suits the users you listed earlier, including low vehicles and people on foot? Third, is anything likely to block the beam in normal use, such as parked cars, stored goods, plants or signage?

Fourth, are the sensors protected from the conditions they will actually face? On an open Sarawak driveway, rain, sun and occasional flooding at the gate line are realistic concerns, and the installer should be able to explain how the chosen units are mounted and protected. Fifth, is there a clear separation between the safety sensors and any access-control device, so that a person triggering the safety sensor is not confused with a person presenting a remote or card?

Record each answer with a photo and a short note. A photo of the sensor in place, taken from the position a user would approach from, is more useful at handover than a description. If a position is rejected, write down why, so the same debate does not reopen at the next service visit.

Functional test sequence for the acceptance visit

A functional test is only useful if it is repeatable. Agree the sequence with the installer before the visit and run it in the same order every time, so results can be compared across visits and across staff changes. The sequence below is an example structure, not a fixed standard; adapt it to the gate type and record what you actually did.

Step one: with the gate stationary and open, confirm each sensor shows a healthy state on whatever indicator the system provides. Step two: with the gate closing slowly under installer control, introduce a test object at the centre of the opening and confirm the gate responds as agreed. Step three: repeat at the left edge and the right edge. Step four: repeat with a low object to check detection near the ground. Step five: repeat with a person walking through at normal pace, if the installer considers this safe to do under supervision.

For each step, record pass or fail, the time, and who witnessed it. A defect log column is essential: note what happened, who owns the fix, and the date it must be re-tested. Do not sign off a failed step on a verbal promise that it will be adjusted later; leave it open in the log until it is re-tested.

Keep the test objects consistent between visits. If a cardboard box was used the first time, use the same box the next time, or note the change. Consistency is what turns a one-off test into a record that means something.

If the compound also has cameras covering the gate, ask whether the acceptance test can be captured on the recording system, so the footage can be kept with the handover file. That is a documentation choice, not a safety guarantee, and it should be agreed in advance.

Documentation and handover for a shared site

On a shared driveway, the handover file matters as much as the hardware, because the person who accepts the gate today may not be the person managing it next year. Build a single folder containing the sensor layout sketch, the photos taken during placement checks, the completed test sequence with pass and fail results, the open defect log, and a short plain-language note explaining what each sensor does and what a user should do if the gate behaves unexpectedly.

Name an owner. On a shared site, ambiguity about who reports a fault is a common cause of delay, so write down who residents or tenants contact, and who then contacts the installer. Include the escalation path for a gate that is stuck open or stuck closed, and make sure that path does not depend on one person being available.

Record the operating rules that the sensor check depends on. For example, if pedestrians are expected to use a separate route, say so and make sure the route is actually usable. If the gate is expected to be left open during busy delivery periods, note who authorises that and how it is reversed. These rules are operational decisions for the site, not technical settings, and they should be reviewed whenever the mix of users changes.

Finally, schedule a review. A short re-test at a fixed interval, and after any motor repair, sensor replacement or change in site layout, keeps the file current. The interval is a management choice; what matters is that it is written down and that the same test sequence is reused.

Where the gate fits in the wider site plan

Gate safety is easier to manage when it is planned alongside the other systems on the same compound. If the site is also adding cameras, the gate approach is a natural camera position, and the sensor test can be recorded as part of the same handover visit. If the site is adding door access or a smart lock on a pedestrian gate, the user list you built for the driveway can be reused, so the same people are not registered twice under different names.

A one-page worksheet to take to the visit

Copy the following into a single page and fill it in during the acceptance visit. It is a working template, not a certification, and it should be adapted to the gate and the site.

Site and gate: compound name, gate type, opening width, date, installer representative, site representative.

User groups: list each group from your earlier table and tick whether they use a remote, a code, a card or rely on someone else.

Placement checks: full width covered, edges covered, low detection, no normal-use obstruction, weather protection, separation from access devices. For each, record pass, fail or not applicable, with a photo reference.

Functional tests: centre, left edge, right edge, low object, person at walking pace. For each, record pass or fail, time and witness.

Defect log: description, owner, target re-test date, result of re-test.

Handover: sensor layout sketch attached, photos attached, operating rules written, fault reporting path agreed, review interval set, named owner recorded.

Sign-off: both representatives sign and date the page, and a copy goes into the shared site folder. If any item is open, the page is signed as partial, with the open items listed. That small discipline is what makes the checklist useful six months later, when the gate is behaving oddly and nobody remembers what was tested on the day it was handed over.
